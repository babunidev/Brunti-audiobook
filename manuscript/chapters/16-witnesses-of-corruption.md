# Chapter Sixteen — Witnesses of Corruption

The rain had found a hole in the ferry-house roof.

It fell upon Velen's records, not heavily but with terrible accuracy. One drop struck the corner of the first page, then another found the same place. The ink there began to spread before anyone noticed.

Brunti moved her cup beneath the leak.

Sira took the papers.

Velen stood in the doorway, holding a packet of dry reed cloth and looking at the little black stain with an expression Brunti had never seen them wear.

“It's the archive seal,” they said.

“The seal is still legible,” Sira replied.

“The date is not.”

Brunti looked over her shoulder.

A copied account of a ruined settlement lay on top of the pile. Along its margin a line of numbers had dissolved into a dark blur. Someone had written an earlier date beneath, but it might have marked the original witness or the day the account was copied.

“Which date did it lose?” Brunti asked.

“That,” Velen said, “is precisely the problem.”

Outside the raised ferry house, water slipped among the pilings. Karrhu's reeds bowed beneath steady rain, then rose again. The ferry master had closed the northern channel until the current fell. No boat could safely leave before midday, and perhaps not then.

Velen spread the surviving records across three dry tables.

There were twelve accounts in the packet from Aetherion. Some bore workshop or witness marks. Others were second or third copies, their headings added long after the events described. Each had been selected because someone believed it concerned beings whose elemental nature had been twisted.

Brunti had seen the names in Orven's reports and along the roads: Ashen Revenants, Blightwraiths, Crumblegheists and Tempestwailers. The four peoples of Morkahja had suffered their distortions differently.

“Are these all from the Ravage?” she asked.

Velen glanced up.

“No.”

“Some of the reports mention destroyed settlements.”

“So did Alen's village.”

“Ogrus and Gobbls destroyed that.”

“Exactly.”

Brunti set the cup closer beneath the leak.

A drop struck the bottom with a clear little note.

“I thought we might find a pattern.”

“We may,” Velen said. “But first we need to know which events the pages describe.”

Sira had finished drying the corners. She came to the table and examined the twelve records.

“Then we should read them,” she said.

***

The first account carried a smear of green pigment across its upper margin.

An unknown witness had stood among scorched ruins where no living fire remained. A sickly green light pulsed between broken walls, and a woman waited at the center, gaunt and blackened, her surface split like charred timber. Poisonous magic gathered around her shoulders in long flowing shapes resembling ruined robes. Each whispered syllable stirred the vapors at her feet.

The account named her an Ashen Revenant Witch.

The witness had described a bitter smell so strong it seemed to lodge in the throat. Withered roots darkened under the creeping mist, and the last pale weeds collapsed into the ash. When the woman turned her hollow gaze toward a movement beyond the ruins, the whispering stopped.

Then the green light brightened between her fingers.

The writing ended there.

“No outcome?” Brunti asked.

“No outcome in this copy,” Velen said.

“Was the witness killed?”

“We don't know. There might have been a continuation. Or the witness might have left.”

Brunti looked at the interrupted line.

The voice of the writer did not survive with the page. Only the description remained, copied by a hand that had perhaps never seen those ruins.

Beside it lay a second account of an Ashen Revenant. This one concerned a Seer standing in a barren wasteland beneath a heat haze, although no open flame burned upon the ground. His blackened form was split by narrow seams of sick orange fire, as if something within him were pressing against a brittle shell. Dark mist carried the taste of ash and hot metal. Pale plants curled under its passing.

The Seer lifted his head. His eyes burned through the smoke. A whisper escaped from behind charred teeth, too low for the witness to understand, and the fire within the ruined body flared.

Someone had titled the entry *Prophecy at the Ashen Plain*.

Velen crossed out the word *prophecy* on their own index slip.

Brunti looked at them.

“He was a seer.”

“The witness heard a whisper,” Velen answered. “They didn't understand it. The title tells us what a later copyist thought the whisper meant.”

“That is a difference.”

“Yes.”

Sira sat beside the window with her letter packet in her hands, listening.

“The witch's mist and the seer's fire both hurt things nearby,” she said.

“They do.”

“But the people who saw them may have been in different lands, years apart.”

Velen nodded.

The third Ashen record was newer as a copy, though it gave no trustworthy date for the sighting. A Warlock stood in pale light among fractured stones, far taller than the Witch, with a blackened body opening along molten fissures. Something like thick dark oil pulsed beneath the brittle surface. His long deep-green beard shifted as though moved by a current separate from the air.

He held his hands apart. Sickly streams of magic gathered into a pale veil between them. Strange symbols appeared, hung for a moment, then dissolved before the witness could follow their shapes. Heat rose from his broken form, carrying an acrid smell of scorched stone and bitter smoke. Dust trembled around his feet.

“The writer drew one symbol,” Velen said.

They turned the page.

There it was: three strokes and a circle, copied with evident uncertainty. The final line might have belonged to the sign or to a fold in the magical veil.

Brunti leaned forward.

“Could it be a craft mark?”

“It could be many things.”

“Did anyone compare it with the Ashen Seer's whisper?”

“How would we compare a shape with a sound nobody recorded?”

Brunti looked at the incomplete symbol.

She had hoped it might be familiar.

Instead she saw a frightened person's attempt to preserve something that vanished while it was being watched.

Velen wrote beneath the three records: *Same named corrupted form. Distinct manifestations, unknown dates, no shared witness.*

It was not an answer to the cause of corruption.

But it was something solid enough to keep.

***

By noon the rain had weakened. The ferry master reopened the lower landing, though the northern passage still carried a strong current. People moved between boats carrying baskets of herbs, wrapped tools and small bundles of letters.

Sira went downstairs to meet a courier who had come from the western relay houses.

Brunti watched her through the open shutter.

The courier had an oilskin cover tied over her bag. Sira spoke with her for a long time beneath the eaves, then took out a small strip of parchment. They compared the names written there with those on another sheet. Once Sira shook her head and pointed toward the western channel.

Velen had begun sorting the next accounts.

“You ought to go down,” they said.

“Why?”

“She is asking about her people.”

“She can ask without me.”

“Yes,” Velen replied. “But you have been looking out the window since she left.”

Brunti considered this.

Then she went downstairs.

The courier introduced herself as Rava. She had worked among the western channel stations and carried messages between Karrhu's raised crossings and the drier roads beyond. Her list held three families who had passed through after the roads out of Evergrove reopened.

“Not all from the raided village,” she said. “Some had joined the caravan later.”

Sira pointed to two names.

“Where did those families go?”

“One toward the southern gardens. The other north, after the bridge was made safe.”

“And the child with the carved walking stick?”

Rava looked again.

“I remember a child with a stick at the second station. There were several children on that road.”

“Did you see this one?”

“I cannot say.”

Sira folded the list.

“Who kept the arrival records?”

“The western relay keeper. She is still there, unless the water has moved her office again.”

Rava smiled faintly at the old joke.

Sira did not.

“How long by the channel road?”

“Several days, with good crossings. Longer if the north bank is still flooded.”

Brunti looked from one to the other.

Sira's work had found a particular place to go.

She knew how the decision would sound before it was spoken.

***

Back upstairs, Velen had arranged the Blightwraith accounts beside the window.

A forest path filled the first description.

The writer had seen a fern curl inward before any figure appeared. Moss blackened between roots. A pale leaf darkened during its fall. Then the Huntress came through the trees, lean and quick, her shape still carrying traces of someone who had once belonged among living things.

Where she walked, grass failed and ivy drew back. Her ruined face turned toward a sound deeper in the wood. She stopped, listening, while the withering continued around her.

“The writer calls her a predator,” Brunti said.

“The way she follows movement gives that impression,” Velen replied.

“And what she does to the plants?”

“That is witnessed harm.”

Sira touched the page's edge.

“The plants did not attack her.”

“No.”

“Then no one could leave them there while hoping to reason with her.”

“Probably not.”

“But no one has said whether she chose to become what she is.”

Velen looked up.

The line held them both.

The next witness had encountered something almost unrecognizable. Before seeing it, they smelled rot: sour water, wet bark, mushrooms collapsing into slime. Beneath the trees moved a swollen crooked body of black wood, split bark and ruined flesh. One heavy arm dragged. Rootlike growths writhed along the other.

The account named it Rootrot.

Each lumbering step left ferns wilted and a young trunk shivering with sudden decay. The witness described a sound from inside the body, low and cracking, like roots forcing their way through dead soil.

There was no record of speech.

A later reader had written *mindless* in the margin.

Sira tapped the word.

“How did they know?”

Velen considered it.

“They did not, from what survives.”

They marked the term as an added interpretation.

The third Blightwraith had been encountered in a clearing already blackened with dying roots. This one was hunched, with horns and distorted armor tangled into the body. A withered face retained the trace of an expression the witness called playful, though its eyes were lifeless.

It chanted.

Tendrils of dark magic reached from the armor seams into the undergrowth. A young sapling trembled; black veins entered its wood, and its leaves lost their color while the Occultist murmured. Ferns folded toward the earth. The fresh scent of greenery became bitter decay.

The witness had watched until a branch above them shed its leaves.

Then the account broke off.

Brunti sat back.

“The Huntress moves through things. Rootrot harms them just by passing. The Occultist appears to be doing it deliberately.”

“Appears,” Velen repeated.

“The third is chanting and drawing something from a sapling. That is stronger evidence than the other two.”

“Yes.”

Brunti looked at Sira.

“Is that what you wanted us to see?”

“No,” Sira said. “I wanted us to remember that there were people within the forms before this happened. And there may still be. It doesn't make them safe.”

Sira's voice had gone quiet.

Brunti thought of the old Verdani village, where gardeners worked around fallen walls and injured roots. The living people there needed protection from whatever harmed them. The corrupted forms in the reports had once belonged to those same elemental families.

Both truths could remain.

She reached for the next page.

***

The Crumblegheist accounts felt different in Brunti's hands.

Not because the paper weighed more, but because she recognized the materials described within them: mountain stone, steel plates, cracks that moved through a structure faster than its surface could bear.

The first report came from a cave beneath the Shattered Peaks.

A Mage stood beneath a jagged overhang, a broad dark-green hat shading his face. His leather armor still carried carefully worked angular patterns, an echo of craft surviving within the ruined form. Light flashed beneath his hat as he held one hand up. Dark currents gathered around a trembling point within the palm.

Loose fragments rattled against the cavern floor.

The witness had heard a whispered incantation but could not identify the words. The magic tightened into a restless vortex, and the Mage remained in front of the passage, guarding it.

“Guarding what?” Brunti asked.

“No one knew,” Velen said.

“His home?”

“Perhaps.”

“A relic?”

“Perhaps.”

“Something dangerous?”

“Also perhaps.”

Brunti rubbed the edge of her thumb against the page.

The green hat bothered her more than the growing vortex. Someone had made it, chosen its color and kept it through years of wear. Someone had cut and stitched the leather patterns of the armor. Those details belonged to a life before the cave became a place of warning.

The second account was headed *Crumblegheist Roughian*.

A massive iron-clad being moved down a mountain slope with such force that gravel skipped ahead of its feet. Steel armor scraped over a body that seemed hewn from rock yet hollowed by deep dark fissures. Spikes rose from its plates, marked by old blows.

With each step the ground opened. Flat stones tilted and split. Dark energy snapped around its limbs, and the air near it felt dense, as if the mountain had drawn in a breath.

The witness did not approach.

“Roughian,” Brunti said. “Is that a word?”

“It is in the record.”

“I've heard Berserk used for a similar form.”

“Then they may be alternate names, or different figures. We cannot tell.”

They left the source's unusual heading unchanged.

The third Crumblegheist was described as a woman.

She stood among jagged rock in heavy, spiked steel armor, wild red hair falling over battered plates. Dark currents moved along the metal. Cracks spread beneath her boots as she straightened, grinding the rocks under her weight. Her eyes burned beneath a tangled fringe, and she released a harsh breath before planting her feet more firmly.

The last tremor ran outward through the ground.

A separate witness had named her the Barbarian.

Brunti looked at the line of cracks drawn beneath the figure's feet. They resembled no work of her own, yet she could imagine what they would do to a Skyland joint if the same pressure passed through a support.

Her mind began moving toward the memorial.

A damaged Grimnir body. A wall that carried its own sound incorrectly. Could the same corruption that ruined the Crumblegheists have changed the old stones?

She opened her pack and set the two-bed archive sketch beside the account.

Velen waited.

Brunti traced the old casing's supports, then the dark lines beneath the Barbarian's feet.

“Both concern structure,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Both concern cracks.”

“Yes.”

“And the source of the pressure?”

“Not described in the same way.”

Brunti looked again.

The Crumblegheist's form carried corruption as it moved. Her memorial was an inanimate assembly whose altered sound had worsened under an added brace. One was a creature; the other a construction. The similarity of a fracture was not proof of a shared cause.

She folded the drawing.

“I still don't know what happened to our wall.”

“No,” Velen said.

It was oddly easier to hear from them now.

***

The ferry house filled with travelers as evening came.

Rain had stopped, leaving a humid silence broken by boats knocking gently against their moorings. The cook hung wet cloth near the hearth and served a stew rich with roots and herbs. Brunti ate mineral grain from a small dish while Velen copied the last records onto dry slips.

Sira did not sit with them immediately.

She was speaking with Rava again at the western landing, asking for a place aboard a small mail boat departing when the channels were safe. Brunti watched through the window, then deliberately turned back to the table.

“Tomorrow,” she said.

Velen did not look up.

“What happens tomorrow?”

“Sira goes west.”

“Has she decided?”

“She is asking about passage.”

“That is evidence of an intention, not yet a departure.”

Brunti gave them a look.

Velen put down the pen.

“I know,” they said. “I would rather she stayed too.”

Brunti was startled.

“You have hardly said that to her.”

“It's not a useful thing to say while she is trying to arrange her work.”

They sat quietly for a while.

At last Velen drew the fourth group of pages toward them.

“The storm accounts,” they said.

***

The first was a drawing of a woman standing alone beneath a widening spiral of cloud.

A witness had seen crimson hair whipped by fierce wind, rain cutting across her still body while the surrounding grass lay almost flat. The Tempestwailer Wildheart raised one hand. Dark currents coiled around her arms like shadowed veins, and lightning moved through the cloud above.

The writer had described her certainty.

She seemed to welcome the corruption moving beneath her skin, speaking quiet words through the thunder as though pronouncing judgment upon a world she believed had strayed. The storm gathered at her raised hand, waiting.

Brunti read the final sentence twice.

“How can the witness know what she believed?”

“From her words, perhaps,” Velen said. “But this copy gives no exact quotation.”

“Then we know the impression.”

“Precisely.”

The second storm report was from a woodland where the canopy hid the sky.

A slender woman stood among the trunks wearing dark blue and earthy-brown robes. Loose strands of her hair stirred in a wind that did not reach the leaves beyond her. Small arcs of lightning ran between her fingers, briefly illuminating her sleeves. Thunder deepened above the trees.

The Tempestwailer Stormcaller whispered.

Every syllable drew a new flicker of electricity. The air carried approaching rain and a sharp metallic tang. When she turned toward a movement among the trees, the whispering stopped, but the storm continued. One thin thread of light remained wrapped around her raised hand.

“That was a local disturbance,” Brunti said.

“Observed locally,” Velen corrected. “It may have spread farther, but the report doesn't say.”

“It was not the same storm as the Wildheart's.”

“We have no evidence it was.”

The final report disturbed Sira when she returned to the table.

She had just sat down when Velen read of a Tempestwailer Seductress drifting above restless earth in green and gold robes. She seemed untouched by the wind. Her face was calm, her smile almost welcoming.

Around her fingers rose greenish vapors carrying a sweet unfamiliar fragrance. They curled into delicate forms of gold and shadow. Beneath the perfume a faint disorienting murmur traveled through the air. The observer described an urge to step nearer, although thunder rolled beyond the clouds.

Her gentle gaze held the world below.

Then she raised one hand, and the vapors gathered.

Sira went very still.

“Did the writer follow her?”

“The account doesn't say,” Velen answered.

“Was the scent the magic?”

“It might have been part of it.”

Sira looked toward the dark water outside.

“Someone could follow that smile because they wished to be safe. The danger wouldn't look like danger until they were close.”

Brunti thought of Frostlace, whom road travelers had mentioned in stories of the northern snow. Beautiful figures did not all belong to the same tradition, and fear could easily make one alluring stranger into another. The record before them named the Tempestwailer Seductress, and no more.

Sira rubbed her hands together.

“Do we know where any of these people are now?”

“Not from these copies,” Velen said.

“Then what can we do with them?”

Velen looked at the twelve pages.

“We can warn people about the signs. We can avoid presenting one person's guess as another person's certainty. And we can ask whoever made the original records what they actually saw.”

Sira nodded, but she did not look satisfied.

Brunti understood.

The pages were useful, though they offered nothing to those who had stood close enough to the corruption to lose homes, companions or their own forms. The people in the accounts remained somewhere beyond the paper, if they remained at all.

***

They spent the next morning building a better register.

Velen had found a waxed board at the ferry house and asked Olri's counterpart at the eastern station for permission to use it. The keeper provided dry slips and a small piece of string for fastening them into groups.

Brunti had expected twelve neat entries in four columns.

Instead Velen made three rows beneath each name.

*Seen.*

*Inferred.*

*Unknown.*

Under the Witch's name they listed green miasma, withered roots and the figure's cracked body. Beneath that came the later label *queen*, which they marked as an image rather than a title. Under *Unknown* they left the date, the witness's fate and the meaning of the Witch's last gesture.

The Seer had inward fire and dark mist, but no recorded prophecy. The Warlock had shifting symbols, but no decipherable message. The Blightwraiths harmed living growth, yet the witnesses could not tell how much identity remained within them. The Crumblegheists broke stone by very different movements, and the Mage stood guard over a cave whose contents were never described. The Tempestwailers bent the weather, yet their intentions were not all equally knowable.

The headings did not erase the danger.

They made it possible to speak accurately about it.

While they worked, a ferryman came through the door with mud on his boots and a broken oar under one arm.

“Who marked the eastern lower path?” he asked.

Brunti stood.

“We helped with the sign near the grotto.”

“That's the one. A family turned away after seeing it yesterday. They went round by the stone landing. Their boat was late, but they reached us.”

“Good,” Sira said.

The ferryman nodded and went on toward the repair bench.

Brunti looked back at the records.

A warning could change what happened to a traveler.

They had proof of that much.

***

By afternoon Sira had made her decision.

She brought the route plan to the table. It showed the western Karrhu channels, then a line of ordinary road and relay stations leading toward the places where the Evergrove families had scattered. Rava had found a place for her on a mail boat leaving at the next safe water level.

“I can make the western relay house in a few days,” Sira said. “From there I'll travel by the station roads, asking who kept the arrival lists.”

Brunti looked at the map.

“And after that?”

“Where the replies take me. I can send letters through the Aetherion routes and toward the northern road.”

“Do you know when we'll meet again?”

“No.”

Brunti folded her arms.

She wanted to name a place. A particular inn, a specific bridge, a date that would make the separation small enough to manage. But neither of them knew how long the messages would take or which roads would remain passable.

Velen set down their pen.

“We'll leave a forwarding address at the northern relay offices,” they said. “And at the Everwinter trading post, if we reach it.”

“Good,” Sira said.

Brunti looked at her.

“Are you certain you want to go alone?”

“No. I am certain I need to go. The route is ordinary enough, and I won't travel alone the whole way. There are other couriers.”

“You could be delayed.”

“So could you.”

“There may be danger.”

“There has been danger since we left the Vale.”

Brunti looked away.

Sira's voice softened.

“You know why I'm going.”

“Yes.”

“The records might tell us whether that child crossed north, or stayed with the aunt's party. They may tell us nothing. But Alen asked for news, and the families deserve answers when there are answers to give.”

Brunti nodded.

“The child is not confirmed missing.”

“No,” Sira said. “I want to keep it that way if the truth allows.”

They sat for a while with the route between them.

At last Brunti unfolded the map and placed one finger beside the northern crossing.

“We can travel together as far as the next dry road,” she said. “Then your western route takes the river, and ours climbs away from it.”

“That is what Rava suggested.”

“Then we'll do that.”

Sira smiled.

It was a small smile, tired around the edges.

Brunti did not ask her to stay.

***

They traveled together for three more days. More than two months had passed since Brunti left her Skyland, and their paths were about to divide in opposite directions. The distance between friends would have to be crossed by ordinary letters.

The wet country receded gradually. Reeds gave way to dense woodland, then to stony paths where small streams descended from the hills. They slept at wayhouses, crossed ordinary bridges and learned from different guides which stretches of road had been cut by the recent rain.

One evening they reached a junction where two caravan routes divided.

The western path followed a broad river toward the relay houses. The northern one rose between low hills toward the long approach to Sarga and, much farther beyond, the Frostwind Mountains. A small shelter stood where the paths separated. Its roof was patched with old planks, and someone had set a bench beneath the eaves.

Sira's mail caravan was preparing to leave at first light.

Brunti helped secure the last bundles. Velen checked that the public route contacts had been copied twice, once for Sira and once for the northern records. Neither tried to decide what Sira should carry.

After supper, they sat on the bench.

The night was cool. A gentle current moved through the trees and turned the shelter's little hanging sign from side to side. Somewhere below the junction a wheel struck a stone, and the sound traveled upward through the dark.

Sira had brought her small book of messages.

She showed Brunti the names she had copied from Alen and Iren, and the unconfirmed routes of the divided caravan. A few entries had been answered with safe arrivals. Others remained empty.

Brunti recognized how carefully Sira had left the empty spaces blank.

“You're becoming like Velen,” she said.

Sira looked up.

“How cruel.”

Brunti laughed.

A moment later Velen, who had been arranging their papers inside, called out, “I heard that.”

They laughed again.

For a while the road felt no different from the evenings they had spent together in the Vale.

Then Sira closed the book.

“I may send word before I know anything certain,” she said. “Only that I've reached a station or met someone who can help.”

“Do,” Brunti said.

“And you?”

“We'll send word when we reach the northern collections.”

“Not just when you find the answer.”

Brunti considered.

“Not just then.”

Sira looked at the hammer resting beside Brunti's pack.

“You know, when we met, I thought you wanted to repair everything you saw.”

“I did.”

“And now?”

“I still want to.”

Sira smiled.

“That sounds right.”

Brunti turned the little dark pebble the Earthsinger had given her between her fingers. She had carried it since the Vale, meaning to take it home to Nemi. It was small and plain. She had nearly given it away more than once.

“Do you remember the first song you taught me?” she asked.

“The one you sing badly?”

“Yes.”

“I remember.”

Brunti tried the first phrase.

It was a little better than the night before. Sira joined on the second line, and their voices traveled quietly beneath the shelter roof. When the melody ended, Velen came out carrying three cups and sat beside them without speaking.

No one suggested a second song.

***

At dawn the caravans assembled.

The western carts carried letters, herbs and household goods. The northern group had mineral supplies and tools for the long road toward the mountain trade circuits. Brunti checked a loose wheel fitting while Velen returned a borrowed lantern.

Sira stood beside the lead western cart, her blue robe covered by a traveling cloak. She had tied the message book inside a dry wrap and kept the pouch where she could reach it.

Brunti crossed the yard.

“I don't know what to say,” she admitted.

“You have said quite a lot during the last few days.”

“Mostly about broken road supports.”

“Yes. I have enjoyed those lectures enormously.”

Brunti smiled.

Sira stepped closer and placed her hands against Brunti's mineral shoulders.

“Take care of your own road,” she said.

“You too.”

“And don't let Velen convince you that a page can never be wrong.”

“I know pages can be wrong.”

“Good.”

Velen appeared behind them.

“I am standing here.”

“We know,” Sira said.

She embraced them both.

Then she climbed onto the cart.

The driver called to the animals, and the western caravan moved away beneath the pale morning light. Sira turned once, raising her hand above the edge of the cart. Brunti answered.

They watched until the last wheel disappeared behind the river bend.

After that there were only two of them.

Velen adjusted the strap of their document case.

“We have a long road,” they said.

“Yes.”

“And more uncertain records.”

“Yes.”

They waited for the northern guide to give the signal.

Brunti looked toward the western bend long after the cart was gone. A flower had fallen from the cord on Sira's travel bag; it lay beside the wheel rut, its petals pressed flat on one side. Brunti picked it up and laid it beside the little dark pebble she meant to bring home to Nemi.

The northern guide lifted a hand.

Brunti took up her pack, and she and Velen followed the road into the hills.
