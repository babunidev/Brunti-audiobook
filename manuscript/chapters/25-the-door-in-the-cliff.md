# Chapter Twenty-Five — The Door in the Cliff

The whisper came from above.

Brunti stood with her back to the dark rock and listened until the last of it thinned into the branches. The sound had no clear syllables. It might have been wind moving through a crack in the cliff. It might have come from something that knew the difference between a name and the wish to hear one.

She had followed wishes for several days.

The rock rose almost straight from the forest floor. Strips of pale stone lay between darker bands, all of them wet with a moisture that did not seem to fall as rain. Roots pressed into the seams. Where the cliff disappeared into fog, something scraped once and became still.

Brunti examined the ground.

There were marks left by falling stones, and narrow shelves where a person might find a foothold. Nothing resembled a path. The last clear piece of forest behind her was a small gap between two trees; when she looked again, another trunk had appeared across the opening.

She did not write down that the tree had moved.

She wrote that she could no longer see the gap.

The distinction felt embarrassingly small.

Then a faint glow emerged high on the wall.

A door stood there.

It was made of wood, not stone, and it occupied a place where Brunti had seen only rock a moment before. Its dark timber was weathered almost to the color of the cliff. There was no ledge before it large enough for a traveler to stand comfortably, only a narrow line of broken stone rising from the roots below.

Strange runes covered the surface.

Their shapes appeared to shift whenever the pale light touched them. One marking twisted into another, too slowly to be called movement and too clearly to remain a fixed carving. Beneath them a dull radiance pulsed, brightening and fading as though the wood drew breath.

Brunti thought of the stone gateway she had left untouched in southern Keldor.

That doorway had been clasped by roots from a living tree. Its faces were carved in stone. Its lock had hummed beneath the vines.

This was different.

The door in the cliff had no visible stone frame, no carved faces, no known passage beyond it. No route led to its height.

She had found the Blackwood Portal.

Or she had found something in Keldor that matched the account closely enough to make the name inevitable.

She stared at it.

In all the miles since Everwinter, Brunti had wanted her discoveries to become more precise when examined. A chip beneath a toolmark should reveal how a stone had been set. A badly preserved inscription should yield a cleaner edge beneath the dust. If a wall held two sources of resonance, careful work should distinguish the joins between them.

The runes above her did the opposite.

She looked directly at one and lost its shape.

A coldness gathered along the cliff. The metal fittings in Brunti's belt grew unpleasantly still against her mineral body. The air seemed to lose the small movements that carried scent through the trees.

From beyond the wooden surface came a low, mournful sound.

She could not say whether it was a voice.

She could not say whether it was beyond the door.

She could only hear it.

***

A few narrow ledges led upward.

The first crumbled under Brunti's boot, scattering grit through the undergrowth. She moved back before the edge gave way entirely and searched for a more stable line.

A broad Grimnir body was not well suited to balancing on cliff shelves made for nothing larger than nesting birds. Her mass helped her hold firm ground; here it gave each foothold more to bear. She tested the rock with her hammer's wooden handle, listening for the solid, short response of stone properly joined to the cliff.

The third ledge held.

The next one did not.

She descended again.

Twice more she tried an approach, leaving small chalk marks where the rock was reliable enough to stand upon. The fog thickened during the second attempt. When she returned to the lower shelf, only one mark remained visible, though she thought she had drawn four.

She felt a quick, unreasonable anger at the chalk.

The marks were not responsible for the forest.

Neither was the hammer.

She sat beneath an overhang and took a measure of mineral-rich water from her dwindling flask. Fine grit caught against the inside of her throat. She had been in Keldor long enough for damp and poor rest to settle into her crystal joints. A thin fracture along her left wrist had widened, not from a single blow but from too many small efforts made before the last one had healed.

Brunti pressed her palm against the stone.

Its cool density answered her.

She set her breathing—an old habit of timing, not an answer to any secret mechanism—to the slow cadence by which Grimnir steadied their own internal structure. The wrist quieted a little. It did not mend completely.

From above came another whisper.

The same faint rise and fall.

She thought she heard Darrik.

Her head lifted before she could stop it.

There was no reason for Darrik to be here. If her letter had reached the Skyland, he would be doing what he had always done: keeping a damaged memorial away from inhabited chambers, checking the next transfer carefully, asking other hands to inspect the joints before anything moved.

His voice belonged to that work.

Brunti shut her eyes.

For an instant she could see him standing beside the old wall. She could even remember the exact inclination of his head as he studied a seam.

Then the imagined room widened.

The door at the end of it was made of dark wood, and behind Darrik's shoulder branches grew through the floor.

She opened her eyes.

Only the wet cliff stood before her.

“Not testimony,” she said.

The words fell without an echo.

She wrote them in the notebook.

At its edge, the pencil had become too short to hold comfortably. Velen would have sharpened it to a point and set aside the shavings. Brunti had left the last proper knife for mending things, not writing.

She opened the page to an old sketch of the memorial: two stones, two separate seats beneath one casing.

The little drawing was still clear.

It hurt to recognize how easily she could have preserved that clarity at home.

The cold deepened.

***

Near the base of the cliff, under a loose shelf, Brunti found an old piece of cord caught around a root.

It had weathered into pale fibers. There was no knot at either end, no mark of ownership, no way to know whether someone had dropped it yesterday or a lifetime ago.

She did not take it as a sign.

Her own cord was stronger.

She looped it around a sound stone projection and tested the hold before climbing again. The line did not reach the door; it barely reached the next ledge. At least it gave her a way to lower herself if the stone broke.

The wood high above looked nearer.

Brunti climbed slowly, keeping her weight close to the rock. Somewhere in the forest a branch snapped. The sound traveled oddly: first from below, then from the same height as her left shoulder.

She stopped.

One hand held the cliff.

The other found a seam beneath a thin band of root.

A pulse passed through the rock.

It was small enough to mistake for her own internal resonance, and she almost did. The sensation came again, then stopped.

She counted the intervals.

There were two.

She counted once more.

There was only one.

A skilled stone worker ought to have been able to distinguish a vibration coming through the wall from a tremor in her own body. Here every surface seemed to return a different account of where the pressure began.

She rested her forehead against the damp rock.

The runes brightened overhead.

The low murmurs continued.

The way up was uncertain. The way down was equally uncertain, though she had set a cord behind her.

Brunti understood, with a painful precision, that this was the last place she could still make a choice resembling the one she had made at Elda's gate.

She could descend.

She could leave the wooden door unopened.

It would be hard, and the path beyond the cliff remained dangerous, but no one had forced her hand against the runes.

She thought of Sira walking beside the recovered girl, matching her pace to the slow knocking of the long stick. She thought of Velen turning a book so the next person could read the same line for themselves.

Neither of them had come to this cliff.

They had refused.

For a moment Brunti wanted their refusal to be proof that she had to continue alone.

It was not.

Their refusal meant only that they had chosen differently.

She tightened her grip and reached the next shelf.

***

The door remained above her.

Up close, its wood had no grain she could follow. Fine lines curved through the weathered surface and vanished beneath the runes. A little light moved along the carvings, then gathered under a mark she could no longer recognize.

She could not read them.

Somewhere inside her pack were copied fragments of seven engraved letters from an earlier road. They had troubled her for so long that she knew the shapes by the feel of their pencil strokes.

She did not take them out.

The letters were not a password merely because another old door stood before her.

She had once believed every unexplained mark might belong to the same unfinished instruction.

Velen had spent months teaching her why resemblance was not enough.

The door gave no answer.

It did not open.

Brunti waited on the narrow shelf until her joints ached and the light in the carvings dimmed almost to nothing. A murmur traveled through the wood. Then another, deeper, as though the rock itself had taken up the sound.

Her first clear impulse was to step closer and press her hand against the door.

The stories had warned against touching it.

She stayed where she was.

The light brightened under the runes and sent a shiver through the rock. The shelf beneath her foot felt suddenly farther from the cliff than before. She turned, caught a rough edge with her left hand, and held still.

For a breath the forest below seemed to lie at the same height as her face.

The trees were near.

Then they were distant.

Brunti shut her eyes and concentrated on the cord at her belt, the tool handle in her palm, the weight of the pack across her shoulders.

She named the things in order.

Hammer.

Water flask.

Notebook.

Cord.

The names remained simple, ordinary and hers.

She opened her eyes.

The cliff was gone.

***

The first thing she could remember was the smell of wet wood.

It was not the green damp of the forest floor. The air held an older, closer scent, as though the inside of an enormous tree had been cut open and left unhealed.

Brunti stood on a narrow span of root.

Darkness extended beneath her.

She did not know how she had come there.

She lowered herself immediately, testing the surface with both hands. The root was real enough to carry her weight. Its bark was cool, ridged and faintly moist. Fine fibers moved along its surface when she touched them.

Above, another mass of wood arched into the dark.

She looked behind her.

There was no cliff.

No wooden door.

No narrow shelf.

Her cord hung from her belt, but one end was frayed, and the length she had tied around the stone projection was gone. She could not tell whether it had broken, been cut, or never belonged to this part of the journey at all.

Brunti sat very still.

The last sight she trusted was the door's unmoving dark timber and the runes brightening beneath it.

The next was this root beneath her knees.

There was no memory between.

She searched for an injury that might explain the missing interval. The fracture at her left wrist remained. Her pack was intact apart from the shortened cord. Her notebook was damp, but the pages had not been torn away.

No obvious blow had struck her head.

No known spell had announced itself.

She had no proof that the door had opened for her.

She had no proof that she had opened it.

Yet she was no longer standing outside.

Brunti wrote two lines.

*Last remembered: wooden door in cliff, closed.*

*Next remembered: standing on root above darkness.*

She left a blank space between them large enough for an explanation she did not possess.

Then she put the pencil away.

Far beneath the root, something creaked.

***

The roots appeared first.

They rose out of the dark in immense twisted masses, thicker than any pillars Brunti had known. Some crossed overhead, others plunged into blackness so complete that she could not find their ends. They knotted around one another, forming narrow passages whose walls were living wood.

The place was cold and damp.

Somewhere high above, a long groan moved through the tangle. It was followed by a slow creaking, as if enormous roots were shifting against each other in a wind that never reached her skin.

Brunti crouched beside the first junction.

Three passages led away from the root on which she had woken. One was narrow enough to require crawling. Another sloped downward behind a curtain of fibers. The third continued almost level into darkness.

She marked the junction on a page.

A straight line for the path.

Three short branches.

Then she turned to look at the place she had come from.

The root behind her divided into four.

For a moment she stared.

She had drawn three.

She knew she had seen three.

The fourth opening might have been concealed by shadow. She could have counted incorrectly. The roots might have shifted. She could no longer treat one explanation as more certain merely because it made her feel less lost.

She crossed out the little diagram.

The pencil left a dark smear.

She tried the level passage first.

It ran between two walls of wood so close together that her pack scraped the bark. The roots curved overhead, opening occasionally into hollows large enough to stand upright.

In one hollow she heard a sound like water striking stone.

She waited.

There was no second drop.

A little farther on, a narrow opening appeared on the left. It sloped downward, then vanished behind a root thicker than the one that framed it. Brunti looked back to estimate its distance from the first junction.

The corridor behind her seemed longer than the one she had walked.

She stopped counting steps.

She had learned to trust steps on mountain roads, where rock held its shape and distance remained a question of movement. Here distance appeared to be something the place could take away.

Her lamp showed only the next few lengths of wood. Shadows gathered between them and held their depth even as she brought the light nearer.

One narrow path ended at an empty drop.

She found it by extending her hammer's handle ahead of her foot. The end met no ground. A small chip of bark fell from the rim and made no sound she could hear below.

Brunti withdrew.

She did not try to measure the depth.

***

There was a time, early in her work at the Skyland, when Brunti had been proud of knowing the weight of a stone merely by the way it answered her fingertips.

Darrik had watched her test a foundation block and asked what she had learned.

“It's sound,” she had said.

“Sound enough for what?”

“For a wall.”

“Which wall?”

She had been irritated by the question.

A foundation block did not know which rooms it would support. It held weight or it did not.

Darrik had shown her the difference between a block that could bear a memorial and one that could safely stand beneath a resting chamber. Both were strong stone. One received a slow, repeated load; the other had to live with the small movements of sleeping bodies and the work of healers who could not wait for a craftsman's mistake to be corrected.

A stone was never merely strong.

It had to be strong for the work asked of it.

Brunti thought of this as she moved across the roots.

She could be strong enough to carry a body out of a forest.

She could be patient enough to repair a broken crossing.

Neither skill made her fit to interpret an abyss that altered the distance between her own footsteps.

The thought did not tell her where to go.

It kept her from stepping over the next edge without looking.

She found a place where two roots crossed and settled into a hollow between them. It was not comfortable, but the wood carried her weight. She drank from her flask and ate a portion of the prepared mineral food she had been saving for the return journey.

The food tasted faintly of grit and dried fruit.

Its ordinary taste almost hurt.

In the darkness, some enormous piece of wood creaked, then settled.

Brunti rested until the thin tremor along her wrist quieted.

When she woke, she did not know how long she had slept.

The hollow looked different.

One of the roots above her seemed to hang lower than before. Another had curved farther toward the wall. She examined the bark for the place her hammer had scraped it.

The mark was there.

She held her hand against it.

The scrape was the same length and shape.

Only the space around it appeared to have changed.

She took out her notebook and began to draw.

A page later she stopped.

The drawing was neat.

It was also useless.

The position of one root was not a map of the space between all the others.

She closed the book.

***

Somewhere farther within the tangle, something moved without showing itself.

Brunti heard a slow weight passing through wood, followed by a silence that made the air feel pressed close to her face.

She stood.

The nearest passage bent beneath a low arch. It was wide enough for her to pass if she removed her pack. She did so and slid it ahead, careful not to catch the straps among the root fibers.

Beyond the arch, the darkness widened.

A long root curved across the space like a bridge. Its far end joined a wall of knotted wood beneath a higher opening. A cold current moved through the gap, though nothing near Brunti stirred.

She stepped onto the root.

Halfway across, she stopped.

A murmur traveled up from the darkness below.

It had the shape of a conversation heard from another room: one voice rising, another answering, neither distinct enough to understand. She looked down, but the lamp showed no floor.

The murmur continued.

Her body recognized something about the intervals.

Not a specific voice.

Not a name.

A spacing.

Two sounds separated by a pause.

She had heard the two memorial stones produce a similar interval before her first mistaken repair. One rhythm had begun, and another had answered through the same casing, each distinct until her brace made the two seem fused. She still did not know whose lives the stones preserved.

That was a fact belonging to home.

The sounds beneath this root were only sounds.

She repeated the distinction silently as she crossed the bridge.

Yet the old cadence remained in her mind after the murmur faded, and it seemed to fit the motion of her feet.

She hated how quickly she had wanted it to mean more.

At the far end she lowered her pack and found that the leather strap had caught on a splinter. A fine line of pale abrasion ran across the shoulder fastening.

She mended it with the spare cord left in her pouch.

The repair took several minutes.

Her hands knew what to do.

When the last knot was secure, she felt a brief and disproportionate relief.

An actual problem had changed because she had worked upon it.

The strap now held.

The roots around her remained indifferent.

***

A narrow passage led down and then upward again.

Brunti moved through it with one hand against the wall. Wood pressed beneath her palm, neither fully soft nor fully hard. In places the bark was smooth as stone; in others it rose into ridges sharp enough to catch the edge of her glove.

She heard creaking overhead.

Something shifted in the dark.

The gap behind her seemed to close.

Brunti turned.

The passage was still there, but a root had bowed across it at an angle she did not remember. She could squeeze through if she left her pack behind. The larger route ahead offered room enough for both.

She looked at the pack.

Without its water and minerals she would have less strength to return.

Without a known route, keeping every supply might mean carrying weight into a deeper trap.

She sat and considered the choice until the sound overhead passed.

Then she took from the pack only what she could secure against her body and left the heaviest spare bundle in a dry notch beside the bowed root.

She marked the knot with chalk.

A few paces later she looked back.

She could still see the white sign.

That comfort lasted until the next turn.

At the bend, a different root carried a similar pale mark.

She approached it.

The chalk line was fresh.

Her own hammer had chipped the bark just below the mark.

The bundle was gone.

Brunti stared at the place where it should have rested.

She did not know whether she had returned to the same notch by an impossible path or whether the root had moved beneath her supplies. She searched the nearby hollow and found nothing.

Her inventory had changed.

She wrote that down.

She did not write *stolen*.

She did not write *taken by the forest*.

She continued with the food and water she still carried.

***

A long time later she reached a place where the roots spread around an opening large enough to hold a house.

There were no houses.

Only wood, shadow and the sound of something distant settling into its weight.

Brunti set her lamp upon a low rise.

In the faint light she could see a single root running across the chamber's center. It was thicker than her whole body and had been worn smooth along its upper surface, though she could not know by what.

She approached and touched it.

The vibration that passed through her hand was slow, almost patient.

It repeated.

She moved her hand.

The rhythm changed.

For an instant she imagined that the root was answering the motion of her own crystal body.

Then she remembered the pounding of distant wood, the tremors that had traveled through every passage since she arrived. Contact could change how a vibration was felt without changing its source.

She moved her palm again.

The rhythm shifted once more.

She could make no reliable distinction.

She took her hand away.

The root continued to tremble.

Brunti sat beneath it, her back against a wall of damp wood.

There had been moments on the road when she had acted before understanding and still helped someone. The injured young Verdani at the forest's fringe had needed to be carried. The girl with the walking stick had needed a safe path, and the scholar had needed food.

Those needs had belonged to people who could answer her.

This place offered no such answer.

It offered only resemblance.

And she had walked into it because she could not bear to leave one unanswered question at home.

***

The black tree stood beyond the next opening.

At first Brunti mistook it for another collection of roots. Then the lamp reached upward and found a trunk so immense that its highest branches disappeared into darkness. Its bark was black, deeply furrowed and twisted around old scars that might have been wounds or ordinary growth.

Gnarled roots spread through the surrounding stone.

They crossed the narrow passage again and again, leaving scarcely a way through.

Brunti stopped.

The tree creaked.

Not with the slow settling she had heard elsewhere. The sound came from several parts of the trunk in sequence, as if a weight were moving through its body without any visible footfall.

One root drew back.

Another curled forward.

Above them, long branches bent toward the passage. Their tips moved despite the still air.

Brunti recognized the account.

Blackwood Guardian.

She could not have said whether the tree recognized her.

Whispers drifted through the darkness, faint murmurs that seemed to originate within the trunk. They carried no words she could understand. Their rise and fall passed along the wood until she could no longer separate sound from vibration.

She held her place.

A thick root curled across the ground where her next step would have fallen.

She stepped backward.

The root withdrew.

Another crossed the passage farther away.

Brunti watched the movements for a long time, trying to find the safest interval.

There was no interval she could trust.

The tree did not move in a rhythm she could count. Its roots overlapped, closed and opened without giving the same passage twice. The long branches above shifted with slow, searching motions that made the ceiling seem lower.

She had no reason to believe strength would help.

The root nearest her was thicker than a support beam in the Skyland's oldest chambers. Even if she could move it, the weight beyond belonged to an entire living mass.

And she was exhausted.

She sat on a dry stone and placed her lamp between her feet.

***

The old account had said that a forgotten password or an item of particular significance could grant passage.

The world archive had been less certain. There were claims of names, offerings, memory and recognition, but no reliable condition that any witness could demonstrate to another.

Brunti had once read those competing statements as a practical problem.

Find the correct name.

Find the correct object.

Test the lock.

The ideas looked less like instructions here.

There was no lock she could reach.

No place to set an offering.

No figure to question.

The tree moved.

It was the only statement the scene itself confirmed.

Brunti opened her pack.

She had a hammer with worn wood at the grip. A small stone button. A scrap of cloth used to tie a shelter marker. A notebook filled with places she could no longer find.

All of these things mattered.

The button had once fastened a cloak. The scrap of cloth had belonged to a route people used to carry one another to safety. The hammer had been in her hands when she damaged a wall, and when she later made repairs that caused no harm.

They were not less significant because they were ordinary.

But nothing in the root-bound passage gave her reason to believe that personal significance was a key.

She closed the pack.

The Guardian's branches continued their slow movement.

Brunti thought of taking out the seven engraved letters. She could remember some of their shapes even now: the little turns and breaks she had copied by lamplight when Sira had still been willing to follow her questions across the Vale.

She kept the copies wrapped.

A letter found elsewhere was not a password to be spoken merely because she was afraid.

She waited.

The great roots crossed once more.

A whisper moved along the bark.

It almost resembled Velen saying her name.

Brunti looked up.

The trunk remained black.

No figure emerged.

“Whatever this is,” she said softly, “I don't know what it wants.”

The roots kept moving.

She listened to the sound of her own voice disappear among them.

Then she added, quieter, “I don't know whether it wants anything.”

The second admission felt more difficult.

***

She tried to go back.

The passage behind her had changed, or she had misremembered it. What had seemed a straight corridor now bent around a mass of roots so dense that her lamp barely reached through.

Brunti followed the opening for a short distance.

It ended beside an empty drop.

She stopped before the edge and retreated toward the Guardian.

There was another passage behind the dark stone where she had rested. She had not noticed it before. It was wide enough to enter, but the bark along its edges was smooth and damp, and the ground beneath it sloped into deeper shadow.

She marked the entry with the last of her chalk.

Then she waited for the root across the first passage to withdraw.

It moved, slowly, and stopped with its curve still suspended above the ground.

The opening beneath it remained too low for her pack.

Brunti took the pack from her shoulders.

She looked at the gap.

The root hung there.

Waiting, according to the account.

Waiting, if waiting was something a tree could do for a stranger.

She did not try to force the wood upward. She did not offer the hammer. She did not speak a name she had no grounds to trust.

She sat down where she could see both the closed return and the raised root.

The lamp burned low.

She drank what remained of the water in her flask.

A new shiver moved through the black trunk.

Brunti closed her eyes for a moment, not to sleep, but to feel the mineral seams of her body and check that her left wrist still held together.

When she opened them, the lamp had gone out.

***

She remembered the root above her.

She remembered the stillness.

She remembered the dark after the lamp failed.

The next clear sensation was a cold surface beneath her hand.

She lay on stone.

The black tree no longer stood before her.

Her pack rested beside her, though she had no memory of lifting it again. The hammer was still at her belt. The scraps of cloth were still folded in their pouch.

A narrow passage continued ahead.

Brunti pushed herself upright and looked behind her.

There were roots there, but not the Guardian's trunk. The ceiling curved low above an opening that led into darkness too thick to distinguish.

She moved back a few steps.

Nothing looked familiar.

She could not say whether she had crawled beneath the lifted root, passed through some other opening, or been carried without consciousness between one place and another.

She could not say whether the tree had allowed her past.

She could not say whether she had ever stood exactly where she now remembered standing.

There was only the change in her location.

The old account's promised password and significant object had explained nothing to her.

She took out the notebook.

On a new page she wrote:

*Blackwood Guardian observed. Roots move.*

Then she hesitated.

*Passage beyond it encountered.*

A long blank followed.

She could not fill it.

Her hand shook as she closed the book.

Brunti looked at the hammer, the pack and her mineral body, all still present and all bearing marks of wear. She had wanted one answer that no one could dispute.

What she had gained was another gap she would have to describe honestly.

The path ahead narrowed.

She went on because she could not find a reliable way back.

***

Somewhere beyond the root passages, the air changed.

It grew less damp, though the smell of old wood remained. The creaking that had filled the Tangled Depths faded until Brunti could hear only the small sounds of her own movements.

She stopped to listen.

No echo came.

Even the soft knock of the hammer against her belt seemed to vanish before it reached the walls.

The passage widened into a black space with no visible ceiling.

Brunti raised the dead lamp, forgot for a moment that it no longer carried light, and lowered it again.

A faint illumination lay ahead.

Not a lamp.

Not moonlight.

Just enough to reveal enormous shapes hanging down from a darkness above.

At first she mistook them for roots.

Then one turned, and she saw that it was a tree suspended upside down, its branches reaching toward the ground.

Others hung beyond it.

The trees moved very slowly.

No wind touched her face.

Brunti took one more step and waited for the sound of her foot to reach the stone.

Nothing answered.

The silence held.
