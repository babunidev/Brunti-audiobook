# Chapter Twenty-Four — Paths Among the Dead

The southern wagon left without Brunti.

Its driver waited while the last supplies were counted. The wheels stood in shallow ruts beyond the western shelter, the animals shifting beneath their harnesses as morning light found the grass. Velen had already secured a place beside the sacks, and Sira was helping the young Verdani girl settle her grandfather's walking stick across her knees.

Brunti held the gate open.

For a little while she did nothing else.

The carpenter had fitted the new hinge carefully. Its pin turned with an even, familiar resistance beneath her palm. Brunti could feel the metal bearing its weight exactly where it ought to. It was a small repair, complete and sufficient.

She released the gate.

“I won't be coming on the wagon,” she said.

Velen looked up.

Sira continued to settle the stick, then rose slowly. She had heard the words; there was no reason to pretend she had not.

“Where are you going?” she asked.

“Back into Keldor.”

Varun, standing by the road marker, stopped winding his cord.

“The search is over,” Sira said.

“I know.”

“The girl is safe. The boy is here. The woman we brought out has a room and someone who can care for her ankle.”

“I know.”

“Then what are you looking for?”

Brunti felt the shape of the answer before she found its words.

In Everwinter, Kaldric had tried to finish a gesture that the snow would not hold. In the ruins of Nekalos, an ancient seal had preserved impressions without their meaning. At home, two voices still lay beneath one memorial casing, their rhythms overlapping where a failed brace had made them unsafe.

Keldor's deeper places were said to contain voices, echoes and memories that did not belong to the people hearing them.

She had heard enough to know those were different things.

That was precisely what made the thought difficult to leave alone.

“If the forest can make a memory sound like something that happened,” she said, “perhaps there's a way to tell one echo from another.”

Velen closed their document case.

“That is a question,” they replied. “It is not a link between Keldor and the memorial.”

“I didn't say they were linked.”

“You are proposing to walk toward the Blackwood Portal because you hope they might be.”

Brunti looked at them.

“I have read the accounts.”

“So have I. None of the material we checked establishes that Blackwood can distinguish the two original stones at home. A damaged record in Everwinter doesn't establish it either.”

“I know.”

“Then the purpose has changed,” Velen said. “You're no longer going because someone needs you.”

The wagon driver cleared his throat from the road. He would not wait much longer.

Sira stepped closer.

“I've watched people come back from this forest unable to say where they have been. Some remember things no one else can confirm. You saw what careful work did for the people here. Why would you throw that away for a possibility you can't even describe?”

Brunti studied her friend's face. Light moved beneath Sira's skin in small, unsettled currents.

“I thought I could leave it,” Brunti said.

“And now?”

“I don't think I can.”

“That isn't the same thing as needing to go.”

“No.”

Sira waited for another answer.

Brunti had none that made her choice better.

“I am going anyway.”

For the first time since they had met, Sira looked at her without trying to soften the silence.

“I won't call this a rescue,” she said.

“I'm not asking you to.”

“And I won't go inside with you. There are people here who still need help.”

Velen held the case against their chest.

“I'll take the records to the next relay. Darrik's transfer plans will have to stand on the evidence we already have.”

“They should,” Brunti said.

“Then don't come back claiming a new memory is an old fact.”

Brunti's answer was very quiet.

“I'll try.”

“That is less than I wanted.”

“I know.”

The girl on the wagon asked Sira whether the road was going to be long. Sira turned to help her. It was an ordinary question, and it deserved an answer from someone who meant to remain.

Varun offered Brunti the map he had used on the outer paths.

“It ends before the places you're talking about,” he said.

“I know that too.”

“Then don't make a mark beyond what I've seen and put my name beside it.”

She gave his map back.

The driver called for departure.

Brunti stepped away from the gate. Sira stayed beside the child. Velen sat among the travel sacks, their face pale and intent.

The wagon moved toward the southern road.

Brunti watched until it passed beneath the last trees.

Then she turned toward the forest.

***

She did not find the first chest by following a treasure hunter's trail.

The path simply ended beneath an immense dead tree.

Its roots spread through the earth like the ribs of a broken roof, holding up a mass of bark that had stopped living long ago. Between them lay an enormous cube-like trunk, darksteel and ancient ironwood pressed so deep into the ground that moss crossed from root to metal almost without interruption.

Brunti crouched.

An edge of the heavy lid stood out from the green fringe. Layers of overlapping ironwood enclosed a narrow seam, and rusted locks secured the fittings. A heavy drop of water fell from the dead wood above. It struck an ancient fitting with a hollow metallic knock, darkened the rust around one lock, and slowly trickled into the narrow seam beneath it. Pale fungi gathered in the deeper crevices of the chest, while tiny insects crossed its darksteel plates undisturbed.

This was no little travel chest.

She touched the ground beside it. The soil had settled around the weight over many seasons. Removing it would require lifting roots and stone, perhaps breaking a tree already dead but still supporting the earth around it.

Her old instincts supplied a dozen ways to begin.

A wedge beneath the nearest corner. A lever against the iron band. A sequence of careful cuts through the rotten wood.

She took out neither wedge nor lever.

The stronghold offered no evidence that its contents belonged to her problem. It had not even offered evidence that it could be safely opened.

Brunti stood and followed the rootline away.

A second object waited farther on, under a different tree.

This one was rectangular, smaller and stranger. Green corrosion marked the iron bands securing its darkwood body. Fine engravings crossed the closed lid and continued beneath the fittings, their lines visible between patches of moss. One delicate groove emerged from beneath an iron band on the other side, worked with a care that the green corrosion had not erased. A beetle wandered over the darkened fitting as a glimmer briefly lit the underside of a fallen leaf.

A dull pulse passed through it.

Brunti stopped.

For a moment the moss barely stirred. Then a thin glimmer appeared where the engraved lid met the dark wood. It faded, leaving the ordinary shade of leaves across the metal.

She waited.

The pulse returned.

It was faint enough to be mistaken for shifting light, except that the same seam glimmered beneath the same unmoving band.

Brunti placed two fingertips against a nearby root and felt for vibration.

Something passed into the wood, small and difficult to separate from the living motion of the tree.

A rhythm.

Her first impulse was to count it against the old twofold cadence of the memorial.

One. Then the next.

She recognized the danger in herself.

“An object pulses,” she said aloud. “That does not make it a voice.”

She wrote those words in her own notebook.

The reliquary remained closed, its iron fittings holding whatever lay within. Brunti moved away before the next faint glow could become an invitation to stay.

By the third shelter of roots the forest had begun to change the scale of its walls. The trees towered close enough for their branches to overlap, yet an old stone doorway stood among them, bound to the trunk of an immense living tree.

Faces looked outward from its frame.

Their expressions were distinct, sharply worked beneath the moss. Between them ran lines of script that Brunti could not read. Thick roots clasped the stone while vines crossed its surface and vanished among the branches.

A low hum gathered at the center.

The magical lock brightened and subsided.

Brunti came close enough to see that the door's interior remained completely hidden. Whatever passage lay beyond, the roots and stone gave no view of it.

She examined the frame for a mark she recognized.

There were spirals.

There were lines joined at angles.

There were shapes that a tired traveler might have mistaken for several of the old letters she had copied in Ethera's Vale.

None of them became legible because she wished it.

The lock gave another small pulse.

She took one step backward.

This was the Sylvan Rune Gateway. It stood in southern Keldor, rooted into a great tree, and its purpose remained unverified. The Blackwood Portal, in the accounts Velen had read aloud, was a wooden door set high in a remote cliff.

The distinction was exact.

Brunti left the stone door unbroken.

She had already entered the woods for a false hope. She would not add a destroyed threshold to the cost of it.

***

By evening, the ground no longer matched the route she had drawn from the last reliable marker.

A small rise ought to have led south, toward a stream whose direction Varun had trusted. Instead the rise descended into a trough of dark leaves.

Brunti walked back to the fork.

There were three roots across the path where she remembered two.

She looked again at the notebook. The sketch had not changed. It was the ground that refused to answer it.

A violent crack of white light split the trees, throwing sharp, fleeting shadows over the trunks. The air held the pungent scent of lightning.

She dropped behind a low bank.

An enormous panther stood among the trunks, its sleek obsidian coat shining in the brief brilliance. Tentacles writhed along its spine. Energy leapt between their tips, lifting the dark hairs of its fur.

The air hummed.

The animal's electric eyes turned toward the clearing.

Then its jaws opened.

The roar traveled through the ground with such force that Brunti felt the lowest part of her body answer it. Dead leaves sprang from the soil. Above the animal's lowered shoulders, its enormous paws spread against the earth. The raised tentacles became strangely still as the last thunder faded, and a brilliant blue-white point began gathering between their tips.

Brunti kept her weight low.

A second flash threw the forest into pale relief. She saw a line of stone near the streambed and a narrow space between two roots. She moved through it while the panther's attention remained on the opposite trees.

She did not learn whether the animal had hunted something or merely warned the forest of its presence.

The stone path carried her clear.

When the light stopped cracking behind her, she found that her hands were trembling with the effort of holding their shape.

She rested until the mineral joints grew steady.

Farther down the slope, a different sound came through the fog.

Wood creaked, then snapped with a sound like a dry branch breaking in two.

A twisted limb emerged from the growth, planted itself, and took the weight of a body that bent like a damaged tree. Deep cracks ran through its splintered bark as it lurched forward, one elongated arm tearing shallow furrows through the leaves. The Timberclaw Wretch lifted a bark-like face toward the open ground. Light burned inside the knots where its eyes sat.

Sap-like ichor stretched from its open mouth and broke, falling among the leaves.

With its next laboring step, the joints complained again.

One arm reached ahead. Five splintered claws unfolded, scraped a low branch until it broke, and briefly held the severed wood before closing around nothing. The creature dragged its other limb through the brush, its movements slow and painfully uneven.

Brunti remembered the Gobbl whose arm had risen against his will.

The Wretch was not that person, and she knew almost nothing of what it had been. Yet she recognized another body moving under a condition that did not look freely chosen.

She stayed behind an old stump until the creature passed.

A narrow trail led to a pond.

The water lay stagnant between knotted roots, its surface carrying a faint gold shimmer. A small goblin-like figure stood within it, almost perfectly still.

Gold light gathered along its skin.

Threads of golden light climbed from the dimming water along the Auraglow Mireling's slender legs, gathering beneath its skin. The pond darkened as the creature drew its radiance closer, until the crooked little body gleamed with a brightness the moon could not explain. Near the bank, a clump of reeds trembled. Ripples reached the bank. The creature shifted its gaze toward the clearing without moving its feet.

Then its weight passed forward.

The light tightened around its bending legs.

Brunti had no way to know whether it was drinking, feeding, healing or changing.

She moved along higher ground, leaving the pond untouched. The golden reflection stayed at the edge of her vision until the bank turned and the water disappeared.

A guttural noise came from the brush ahead.

Another answered from a nearer patch of darkness—so near, and so soon, that it seemed to arrive before the first creature could have closed its jaws.

The Echowraith Skulker appeared in pieces: a glowing eye between leaves, a claw edged with spectral light, a shoulder that jerked suddenly into place.

The rest of the creature followed with a fluency that seemed to defy its own broken motion.

It moved smoothly, stopped in a sharp lurch, then lowered itself against the dark growth. One arm snapped upward and remained suspended halfway through the motion, spectral light glimmering along the unfolded claws. A rough sound caught in its throat. The creature tilted its head, seeming to listen to the distorted echo as its wavering outline drew darker and more solid.

Brunti waited beneath a fallen trunk.

The creature's luminous eyes held on a patch of open ground. Its outline wavered while the rest of the body seemed to settle for another sudden movement.

She counted the length of the pause.

On the third breath she slipped through the narrow space beneath the trunk, letting wet leaves cover the sound of her passage.

The Skulker did not follow.

At least, she never heard it do so.

When she stopped to mark the route, Brunti found that she could not remember whether the pond had been north or east of the fallen trunk. The light of the Mireling belonged to the same fading hour, yet she had lost the order of two bends.

She drew the places separately.

She left the space between them blank.

***

The second day carried her into country where the trees had begun to look more like remains than growth.

An enormous footstep pressed the leaf litter flat ahead.

Brunti climbed onto a low stone and looked through the branches.

A giant figure moved across the dim ground. Pallid flesh hung from an Ogru-like frame, exposing bone and dark sinew in places. Its empty eye sockets faced forward without following the branches that struck its face.

One arm caught against the underbrush.

The body kept walking until the branch broke.

The sound brought no change to its expression.

The Lumbering Necrobrute advanced in pauses, each ponderous step separated from the next by a silence long enough for Brunti to think it had stopped. Then another foot rose, leaving a deep impression behind.

She stayed on the high stone until it passed.

The resemblance to an Ogru was visible. The identity of whoever that body might once have been was not. No story of captors, raiders or ancient bloodlines could be made from its appearance alone.

Brunti descended on the far side.

Smoke met her before the next danger became visible.

A rotten tree burst into flame. Its hollow trunk burned upward and collapsed in a shower of sparks. Beyond it drifted a woman whose charred skin was split by glowing cracks.

The Emberlost Wraith moved through the smoke without haste. Fire ran from her body into dead growth, and another rotten trunk began to smolder. Her eyes burned dully. One arm lifted in a motion that did not follow her gaze, scattering sparks toward dry leaves untouched by flame.

Brunti retreated from the path of the embers.

The forest's corrupted air thickened with smoke. She found a stream cutting through the dead ground and followed its damp margin until the burning wood was behind her.

An uneven tune rose from the haze ahead.

Another woman crossed the grey earth, humming in fragments that wandered away from the expected notes. Small fires appeared beneath her bare footsteps. Her burning hand touched a brittle branch, and it caught instantly.

The Flamecrazed Wanderer interrupted her melody with a short, meaningless sound.

Then she began again.

Her hand rose toward another branch, paused, and moved in time with the broken tune while flame ran through the wood beside her.

Brunti stood on the opposite side of the shallow stream.

Two fire-bearing figures, within a single day's travel, among damaged trees.

The temptation came quickly.

*One origin.*

*One pattern.*

*One cause beneath all the others.*

She thought of Kaldric's unfinished gesture. She thought of the two memorial voices occupying one wall.

It would be so much easier if resemblance proved kinship.

She wrote in her notebook: *Two separate observations. No verified connection.*

The Wanderer moved farther through the smoke, leaving the stream untouched.

Brunti continued along its bank.

The water ran into a thicket of withered wood where a tall shape stood motionless among the dead trunks.

Two purple eyes opened.

The Gloomthorn Sentinel had jagged horns and a body from which something of Verdani form still remained. Dark smoke curled around its limbs. A thin tendril brushed a green sprig near the roots.

The sprig shriveled at once.

The eyes moved toward the sound of a stone slipping beneath Brunti's foot.

The creature lurched forward with startling speed.

Brunti stepped behind a broad trunk as the smoky tendrils swept through the underbrush. The last green patches beside it curled inward, their color draining into the dark. She backed along the wet channel and stayed beyond its reach.

Sira's people were living water held together by memory and care. This altered creature retained something of their silhouette, but Brunti could not know whose life lay beneath the corruption or whether it could ever be restored.

She moved away without striking it.

The storm arrived before the smoke cleared.

A shriek rose inside a knot of dark cloud trapped beneath the canopy. Claw-like hands swept from its center, bending the air around them. Lightning briefly revealed an altered Aevis shape.

Then the cloud closed.

A violent gust struck the trees. Branches bent, leaves tore free, and a trunk split with a report like breaking stone. Thunder followed the falling wood.

The Stormshriek Harbinger turned within its own tempest, drawing fragments upward around its body. One clawed hand emerged again, fingers spread against the dark. The debris rose with it.

Brunti pressed herself into the hollow beneath the streambank.

Air pushed into every seam of her mineral body. She held her limbs close and waited while the wind carried broken branches across the open ground.

The sheltering bank trembled. Something heavy struck the earth above her.

For a little while she could not hear anything but the storm.

Then the wind moved away among the trees, and the hollow became still.

Brunti crawled out.

The stream had changed its course.

Where she had followed running water into the thicket, a mound of newly fallen wood now blocked the channel. Water spread into three shallow cuts, none of them marked on her map.

She found the last page of her notebook.

One corner had torn away.

She tried to remember whether she had noted the western branch before or after seeing the Sentinel.

The answer did not come.

***

At twilight she reached a grove where the trees had fallen long ago.

Rotting stumps stood among the wet ground, their edges softened by mist. Faint lights drifted between them, vanishing whenever the fog thickened and returning farther away.

Each reappearance changed the shadows.

A stump that had seemed sharply carved now looked like an ordinary mass of rot. Gnarled branches interlaced above the clearing. Their outlines shifted when the lights passed, though the wood itself remained still.

A whisper came through the fog.

No leaves moved with it.

The lights gathered near a hollow trunk, then separated. Its opening became dark again.

Brunti stood among the decaying wood and listened.

A nearly audible sound came from beyond the nearest stump. It did not become a word. It did not resemble the distinct old voices preserved at home.

Still, her chest filled with a sudden expectation that was almost painful.

She took out the sketch of the memorial's two old seats. Damp had softened its edges. The little lines marking their separate positions remained precise.

A light moved along the drawing.

For a heartbeat it seemed to trace the same interval she remembered from the damaged wall.

Brunti turned the page away.

The light followed the motion of her hand across the fog.

Not an echo.

A moving source of light, casting a moving shadow.

She knew that.

She kept looking anyway.

When she tried to leave the Twilight Whisper Grove, the path she had entered by no longer appeared between the stumps. She found a different opening, then another, and returned to a patch of ground whose rotting leaves she thought she recognized.

The sound came again.

This time it almost resembled her own name.

She did not answer.

She followed the higher, drier edge of the grove until the drifting lights dwindled behind her.

There was no certainty in the direction she chose.

***

Morning brought a trail ending in blue-white light.

The path narrowed between trees whose forms emerged and vanished beneath fog. Ahead, an opening shone as though a clear passage lay beyond it.

Brunti approached carefully.

A side route appeared between two trunks. Its ground seemed firm. Then a dark shadow crossed it, though she could find no source above the trees. Beyond, a second opening repeated the same bend.

The blue-white glow remained visible through both.

She tried the left branch first.

After a few dozen steps the fog closed behind her, and the light ahead held the same distance. She turned back to the original trail, then found that both bends resembled the route she had just left.

She stopped and marked a root with a small piece of chalk.

When she reached the next visible fork, she could no longer see the mark.

She waited until the light changed.

It did not.

Enigma's End withheld its destination without ever seeming to recede.

Brunti turned away.

For the first time she could remember, choosing not to follow a light felt like the harder act.

The path beyond it ran between dead trees onto grey, barren ground.

A pale shape lay across the trail. From one angle it resembled a rib cage. As the thin light shifted, one curved length continued upward into a dead root.

Other remains lay farther along. Some were unmistakably skeletal. Others were wood bent into the shapes of bones, or bones caught so deeply among roots that the two could not be separated without disturbing the ground.

No green interrupted the view.

The air barely moved.

Brunti walked through the Path of Despair slowly, keeping her attention on the stable soil beneath her feet. The distance ahead looked grey and empty. Every branch-shadow lengthened into another pale shape until the ground seemed filled with remains whether she looked directly at them or not.

She tried to recall the rescued child's small hands holding a walking stick.

For an instant the memory felt distant, as though it belonged to a story someone had told her long ago.

She stopped.

The child had been real.

So had the broken cot at Elda's shelter. So had the careful hinge, the warm lantern, the injured woman's request to see the door.

Brunti named each thing in her mind.

When the weight of the grey path eased a little, she continued.

The next landscape smelled of decay before it appeared.

Colossal dead roots rose from the ground, folded into narrow corridors and overhung the low places like broken arches. A foul vapor seeped from the earth between them, collecting in sheltered channels.

One knot resembled an open mouth.

A split in another root looked like a face until the vapor crossed it.

The stench turned sweet for an instant, then sharp again.

Brunti knew enough of poisoned air not to trust a change in scent as evidence of safety. She tied a damp cloth over the openings of her face and followed the highest visible line of ground, away from the hollows where the gas gathered.

The Gloomroot Expanse offered corridors that looked passable until more roots folded across them. A slow exhalation rose from the earth and filled one opening entirely.

She turned back to the last junction.

The way she had entered seemed lower now, closer to the vapor.

She waited until a faint breeze moved the gas toward an empty channel, then climbed over a broad root where the air remained clear.

Her metal joints ached from the effort.

The map in her notebook had become a series of isolated shapes.

A knot like a mouth.

A face in the wood.

Three passages, two crossed out.

None could be connected confidently to the last reliable road.

She had not turned around because of pride.

She had not turned around because she had already gone too far.

Brunti recognized both truths and disliked how little they changed her direction.

***

She reached the Wraithbone Pathway under dense fog.

A dying bough creaked above the narrow trail. Beneath it rested a skull half buried in leaves. More bones lay along both sides of the way, some grouped close enough to suggest fallen warriors, others scattered among the roots and dead growth.

Light barely reached the ground.

The bough bent again. Its shadow passed across an exposed hand and slipped between its fingers.

Brunti did not touch the remains.

She thought of Darrik preserving the wall at home without pretending it belonged in a resting chamber that could no longer safely hold it. Here the dead lay in a forest that had no steward, no record keeper, no clear way to ask what should be moved and what left undisturbed.

Her fingers hovered above the notebook.

She could record only that bones lay beside this trail.

Not who had died.

Not why.

Not whose work had been left unfinished.

The fog thickened.

Another pale fragment appeared beyond the last clear patch of ground.

Brunti went on.

The trail narrowed into a corridor of jagged stone.

Hollows in the rocks caught a little light, turning them into skull-like faces. More shapes lined the pass, their dark cavities facing the narrow way between dead trees.

A repugnant smell lingered beneath the fog.

Above the path, cracked branches hung near their joints. Loose soil filled the pockets between rocks. A small collapse of earth exposed a deeper recess beneath one stone.

Something glimmered within.

Brunti stopped beside the opening.

The accounts called Skullstone Pass a place of hidden treasures. There might indeed be objects buried beneath the heights and cursed ground. The glimmer could have been metal. It could have been wet stone. The ground above it had already shifted once.

She looked up at the broken branch scraping against the rock.

Then she moved past the recess.

A little farther on, she tried to draw the line of the pass.

Her hand stopped.

She could remember the skull-shaped hollows and the slipping soil. She could remember the smell. But she could not say which of the last two turnings had led to the route beneath the dead bough.

She had been using her notebook to prove where she had gone.

Now it proved only what she had once been able to see.

***

The third night found her near a stand of trees whose bark had been torn away in long strips.

A raw, prolonged roar rose among them.

Brunti dropped beneath the shelter of a stone ridge.

A huge bear reared beyond the undergrowth, its light-brown fur matted and bristling around an unnatural hump. One elongated claw swept across a trunk and peeled away a fresh length of bark. The other struck low enough to score the stone beneath it.

The Roarback Mauler's head turned sharply.

Its eyes searched the broken growth, wild and unsettled.

Another breath caught in its throat, a sound closer to pain than triumph. Its massive weight shifted upon its hind legs. The long claws lifted again, trembling at their tips.

Fresh bark settled over older scars.

Brunti had no reason to go closer.

She watched until the bear moved toward a different stand of trees, then waited longer still. The ridge offered a narrow line of stone along which she could pass without crossing the ground the animal had disturbed.

She followed it as the last light faded.

The ridge ended above an enormous hollow.

Roots rose and fell across the ground like the surface of a frozen dark sea. Some were as wide as bridges. Others plunged into soil that shifted faintly with their weight.

Two green eyes shone high among them.

Brunti stopped breathing.

Beneath the eyes, dark tentacles tightened around one another. A vast body lifted from the root tangle that anchored it to the blighted trees. Its head held no other visible feature. The lidless gaze remained steady while sinewy lengths slid into new positions below.

A limb drew free and hung above the ground.

The air thickened with dark magic.

Another tentacle loosened.

Then another.

The trees at its base creaked beneath the changing weight.

The Gloomweaver Colossus turned its eyes slowly toward the hollow below.

Brunti backed against the ridge, keeping her steps on the stone rather than the living roots. She could feel a low tremor entering the soles of her mineral feet. It was not a voice. It was weight moving through wood and ground, so immense that her own body could scarcely distinguish one pressure from the next.

The eyes moved nearer.

She lowered herself into a narrow fissure and held still.

Above, a root groaned. Something heavy passed across the opening, blocking the dim light entirely.

For a time she heard nothing but the slow movement of wood against wood.

The silence after it passed was worse.

She waited until the green light no longer touched the stone around her. Then she climbed down the far side of the ridge and followed a shallow cut between rocks, away from the roots.

She had not defeated the creature.

She had been small enough, patient enough, and fortunate enough to remain unnoticed.

That was all.

***

Later, Brunti could not say whether another night passed before the ground began to rise.

The fog remained thick. Water tasted of mineral grit and bitter leaves, and the prepared salts in her pack had dwindled. A fine tremor ran along the seams of her wrists whenever she set down a heavy load.

She stopped often.

At each rest she checked the small things she still knew.

The hammer was at her belt.

Her notebook was wrapped against damp.

The letter to Darrik had been sent from Everwinter, though she did not know whether he had received it.

The child with the walking stick had reached a safe shelter.

Sira and Velen had taken the southbound wagon.

None of those facts depended upon the sounds that followed her through Keldor.

Yet the forest had begun to offer them back to her in unfamiliar forms.

A branch tapped with the same interval as the two voices in the memorial wall.

Water struck a hollow stone in a rhythm that almost resembled a name.

At dusk a whisper moved through the branches, and for a moment Brunti heard what sounded like Darrik calling her home.

She closed her eyes.

The sound passed.

When she opened them, she was standing beside a cliff whose dark rock rose almost straight out of the ground.

There were no familiar route marks at its base.

She looked for the southern turn, the path back toward Elda's gate, the high stone where she had waited for the Necrobrute to pass.

None appeared.

The cliff continued beyond the fog in both directions.

Brunti remembered the accounts of a wooden door set high in remote Keldor stone. She could not yet see it.

She also remembered Sira's answer at the shelter.

*The search we promised is finished.*

The path home had been open when she chose to leave it.

That decision had been hers.

Now she stood beneath a place she could not locate on her own map, with no reliable way to tell whether the next step would carry her closer to the thing she sought or farther from everyone who had tried to help her.

A faint sound came from the rock above.

Not a voice.

Not yet.

Only something shifting where the cliff disappeared into fog.
