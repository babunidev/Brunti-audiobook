# Chapter Four — Letters Without a Word

The first letter was almost lost beneath a flower.

Brunti had left the caravan road to take water at a little spring when she saw the weathered stone standing at the edge of the Arcadian Hills. It rose only as high as her knee. Wildflowers pressed against its face, their fine stems trembling where the wind crossed the slope.

She would have walked past it if a blossom had not lifted at that moment.

Beneath the petals, a deep straight cut caught the sun.

Brunti knelt.

A.

The letter was large, though the stone itself was modest. Tiny roots had settled into its grooves. Earth covered much of the lower face, and a narrow edge continued beneath the grass as if the marker had once stood taller. Its upper surface was warm from the afternoon light.

She brushed away a loose leaf, careful not to pull at the roots.

Nothing moved beneath her hand. No second note sounded from the stone. There was only the soft scrape of grass against her boots and the distant creak of Eda's cart.

“A border marker,” the guide said when she came up the slope.

“Of what?”

“That's a very good question.”

Brunti measured the depth of the cut with the blunt end of her pencil.

“Someone made this on purpose.”

“So they did.”

“Would a boundary stone bear only one letter?”

“Some would. Some wouldn't.” Eda looked out across the green hills. “There are old accounts of treasures in these folds, too. And stories that the land is kinder to people who don't tear it apart looking for them.”

Brunti drew the letter and the flowers around it. She was tempted to uncover the stone's buried edge, to see whether another mark lay below the soil, but the fine roots had woven themselves through every exposed seam.

She put her pencil away.

Eda had been watching.

“That's the first time I've seen you leave an unanswered stone in peace,” she remarked.

“I have left many stones in peace.”

“You've written about many stones in peace.”

Brunti gave her a look. Eda laughed and turned toward the road.

A little farther on the cart track divided, one branch following the main trade route toward Aetherion, the other curving among smaller farms and garden paths. Eda's next delivery belonged to the busier road.

Brunti looked back at the marker.

“Do the smaller paths meet the road again?”

“Most do. Some take their time.”

“I want to see the hills.”

“I suspected you might.”

Eda gave her directions to the next market shelter, told her where water could be found, and warned her not to mistake every narrow track for a shortcut. They parted without ceremony. The cart wheels went on with their familiar wooden clatter, growing quieter between the grass-covered rises.

For the first time since she had descended from the Skyland, Brunti stood alone on a road that did not belong to her people.

She found that she liked the silence.

Then she opened her notebook again.

***

The Enchanted Gardens reached their brightest hour as the daylight left them.

Rock formations rose from beds of wildflowers, their dark faces warmed at the edges by the last sun. Fragrance thickened in the cooler air. Something called between the stones—a low trill answered by a clicking sound, followed by several notes Brunti could not place.

She stopped beside a cluster of tall flowers.

A thread of pale light crackled across the gap between two rocks.

It was brief, no larger than a handspan. Another followed and vanished behind the stems.

Brunti had seen the effects of concentrated magic before. In a Skyland workshop, such a flicker would have sent everyone looking for the seam that carried it. Here the wildflowers stood unharmed. Small creatures called from places she could not see.

She traced one crackle with her eyes, then another.

A carving brightened on a rough stone face.

H.

She crossed the clearing and touched the edge of the letter. It was worn but deliberate. The mark stood where the last light could still reach it, and when another faint spark passed among the stones, its grooves shone for an instant before the surrounding rock sank into shadow.

Brunti waited.

The chorus among the flowers grew quiet. From somewhere deeper between the rocks, a solitary note rose into the dusk, so soft that its last trace disappeared before she could judge the distance.

The letter did not change.

She sat beside the stone and drew it before the light failed completely. Beneath the sketch she added the first letter she had found that morning.

A. H.

Two marks in two different places.

She knew better than to call them a word.

Still, she tried their shapes together in her mind as the evening sounds passed over the garden. A might begin an inscription. H might be the name of a maker. They might belong to separate languages, separate ages, separate reasons for carving stone.

Her hand moved to the little strip of bent metal in her pocket.

Somewhere overhead, a bird gave three quick cries. The answer from the stones was nothing like them.

Brunti did not sleep in the gardens. She found a travel shelter near the main path and spent the night among merchants who had no interest at all in carved letters.

Their talk concerned the price of salt, a missing pack strap and which road would be passable after rain.

By morning, those questions seemed as real as her own.

***

The third letter was cut deep into a boulder in the Mystical Vale.

Clouds had covered the fields since dawn, laying soft shadows over the emerald grass. As Brunti crossed the low meadow, the sky opened briefly above her. A broad shaft of sunlight moved across the fields and climbed the face of the great stone.

K.

Its grooves were deep enough to hold shadow while the surrounding rock brightened. Wildflowers gathered at its base. A fresh scent of grass rose after the earlier coolness, and birds called from the trees beyond the hill.

The boulder cast a long shadow across the grass. Another cloud passed above it; the meadow grew green and quiet again, but the deep-cut letter remained distinct.

Brunti drew the K while the light remained.

Then she stood back.

Three stones. Three letters. No names attached to them.

She copied them onto one line.

A H K.

The letters sat beside each other in her notebook with a certainty they did not possess in the landscape. They looked as though someone had intended them to be read together.

She closed the book.

When she opened it again a short while later, the marks had not become more truthful for being close.

She considered turning back to the main road immediately. Aetherion had the records she needed. Darrik was preparing a safe replacement for the memorial's old support. She had already spent several days following scenic paths and listening to things that might have nothing to do with her work.

But another traveler at the shelter had spoken of a low stone in the Bucolic Dale.

Brunti told herself that if it bore an ordinary maker's sign, she would leave the matter.

She took the path toward the dale.

Two little creatures tumbled through the flowers when she arrived, chasing one another so quickly that the stems shook behind them. Farther up the hill, a grazing animal lifted its head at the commotion, then returned to its meal.

One creature scrambled onto a weathered stone half buried in the meadow. Its whiskers twitched. Below it, the other disappeared into the grass.

Beneath the little paws, a letter had been cut into the stone.

J.

The creature sprang away, sending loose petals into the evening air. As shadows deepened along the grooves, the sky above the dale turned rose and orange. The pair chased each other over the slope until only a wavering line of stems showed where they had passed.

Brunti knelt, brushed loose dust away with the side of her hand and made her fourth drawing.

A H K J.

She read the letters aloud.

Nothing answered.

A little animal emerged from the flowers beside her boot, paused with its nose raised, and darted back into hiding.

“That is a more useful opinion than most,” Brunti told it.

The animal offered no reply.

She laughed despite herself.

***

The fifth letter was not where Brunti expected it.

The Captivating Downs lay open beneath a high bright sky. Tall grasses shone along their narrow edges, and flowers nodded after the wind had dropped. Brunti spent much of the morning following a ridge from which she could see the lower paths twisting among green slopes.

She had almost given up searching when she noticed an oddly smooth curve on a stone near the trail.

O.

Unlike the sharp angles of the earlier marks, the letter followed the rounded face of its rock. Shadow rested inside the cut. Around its base the grass stirred while the higher stems remained still.

Brunti laid a hand on the stone.

A faint pressure moved through the ground beneath it. Not a clear note. Not the Skyland's familiar hum. Something steadier than the wind, though she could not tell how far it reached.

She waited for the old desire to explain it.

The desire arrived promptly.

A boundary. A line of stones. A message for travelers. A direction to a lost place. Perhaps a sequence arranged to be heard rather than read.

Her thoughts assembled themselves with the speed of an experienced craftsperson fitting a support to an uncertain joint.

Brunti took her hand away.

She looked down the slopes at all the paths she had crossed without marking them properly. If the stones formed a sequence, she had no proof that she had found them in its intended order. If there was an intended order at all.

The afternoon sun moved. A breeze rose, and the tall grasses came back into step with it.

She wrote the letter.

Then, beside it, she wrote a question mark.

The mark looked rather lonely.

At the Radiant Heath, she found the sixth.

Warm golden light lay across an expanse of untended grass and wildflowers. Insects passed through the brightness and disappeared among the stems. The wind brought a dry rustle from the open land and a sweeter scent from a sheltered patch near a rock.

M.

The letter had been carved large enough to recognize from a few paces away. Shadows gathered in its strokes, and the trembling shadow of a flower touched its final line.

Brunti stood over it without taking out her notebook.

The stone did not ask to be drawn. The heath was full of sound: insects, stems, an unseen bird farther up the slope. None of it seemed arranged for her.

At last she sat in the grass and ate a small portion of the food she had brought from the shelter. She had prepared it badly. The mineral mixture was too coarse, and the grain had dried around the edges. Darrik would have said the cook needed a better understanding of heat.

She ate it anyway.

She thought of Nemi's hammer. The child had repaired it by learning where the binding was wrong, not by insisting that the first binding ought to have held. And Brunti had come down into the Vale carrying a piece of metal that proved the cost of that same insistence.

When she finished, she wrote M on a clean page.

She left room below it.

***

The hills beyond the heath rose in long green folds.

At the Mesmerizing Hillsides, sunlight and cloud moved across opposite slopes so slowly that Brunti could watch one patch of flowers appear while another disappeared into shade. Wind crossed the hollow with a sudden rush. A small animal passed down the hillside, parting the grass for a moment before the stems closed again.

Brunti sat against a warm rock and studied the landscape.

The hills held no letter that she could find.

For a while she was annoyed by that.

Then the cloud moved, and the shadowed slope brightened little by little, from the hollow to the crest. The tiny flowers that had seemed dull and distant emerged in violet and white.

It was beautiful in a way that did not improve with recording.

Brunti put down her pencil.

She had spent much of the journey with her eyes turned toward stones. She had missed, she realized, how much of the land was changing between them.

By the time she reached the Magical Glens, the afternoon light had thinned beneath the trees.

A pale shaft of sunlight rested in a clearing, brighter than the gap in the canopy seemed able to allow. Flowers turned slowly in the breeze. Their leaves brushed together with a soft sound like a conversation held too far away to hear.

At the edge of the glen stood a rock bordered by moss.

On its face was the final letter.

A.

Brunti touched the smooth edge of the carving. Through the ground came a faint steady vibration, too low to gather into a proper note. Farther into the trees, another patch of light appeared though no branch had moved above it.

She did not follow the light.

Instead she opened the notebook and copied the last mark beneath the others.

A H K J O M A.

Seven letters in seven different places. She had crossed the hills and gardens of the Vale to find them, and each had given her a new shape without an explanation.

They might be parts of a map. They might be boundary marks made by different hands. They might once have belonged to a language no one nearby spoke. The world was old enough to leave many things behind without arranging them into a message for one passing traveler.

Brunti tried to sound the letters as a word.

It came out awkwardly, a string of hard beginnings and uncertain vowels.

She tried again.

A bird answered from the far side of the glen, sounding distinctly unimpressed.

Brunti closed the book.

“I agree,” she told it.

She sat beneath the trees until the light grew too dim to draw.

***

At the next shelter, a traveling record-keeper was copying accounts from merchants into a stack of narrow folios.

Brunti showed him the seven letters, with their places and the circumstances of their discovery. He examined the page, turning it sideways once, then handed it back.

“Do you know what they mean?” she asked.

“No.”

“Have you seen letters like them elsewhere?”

“Letters? Certainly. This sequence? Not that I can recall.”

He was an older fellow with ink-stained fingers and a habit of pressing his thumb against the edge of each page before turning it. A few visitors waited nearby with stories to dictate. Brunti was aware that she had delayed his work.

“There is an object in one of your records,” she said. “A cube. Symbols crossing its edges. Have you copied an account of it?”

The record-keeper hesitated, then searched through a bundle of older sheets.

“This is a traveler’s description,” he said. “Not a drawing made from the thing itself.”

He spread a worn page on the table.

The account spoke of a perfectly formed cube whose smooth faces met without visible seams. Delicate symbols covered it, some crossing the edges from one side to another. Inside the translucent material, cloudy light gathered and dispersed. When the cube pulsed, several distant markings brightened together, giving the impression of a pattern extending through the whole object.

Brunti read it twice.

The account described a humming sound. A resonance. A shape that seemed to join separate marks.

It would have been easy to make it belong to her discoveries.

“Can you tell me where the traveler saw it?” she asked.

“The copy doesn't say.”

“Who wrote the first account?”

“Not on this page.”

“Was the cube connected to the marked stones?”

The record-keeper looked at the letters.

“No one has written that here.”

Brunti ran her finger beside the passage, not touching the ink.

The temptation returned. Seven scattered marks might be the edges of some greater design, and a perfect cube might somehow carry the key to reading them. She could imagine the moment when the cloudy heart of the object cleared and all its symbols became legible.

The image pleased her.

That was precisely why she distrusted it.

She copied the description and noted that it came from a secondhand folio with no identified witness. Beside her seven letters she added: No established relation to the cube.

The record-keeper watched her write.

“An unusual note to keep,” he said.

“Not as unusual as I would like.”

When Brunti returned the folio, she found the ink had dried on the edge of her thumb. She rubbed at it until only a small dark trace remained.

***

The Ethereal Glades lay beyond the shelter, where the trees thinned around a quiet opening in the greenery.

Clouds had covered the sun, but the flowers below remained bright. Their colors held in the shadow as though daylight had settled among the petals and forgotten to leave. Faint shimmer rose from the grass. At the clearing's edge, leaves rustled from branch to branch and then became still.

Brunti followed a narrow opening through the foliage.

Beyond it stood another glade beneath the same covered sky. Fallen leaves rested on the ground, illuminated by a soft radiance gathering near the roots of the flowers.

She had seen many strange lights since leaving her home.

At first she had wanted to compare every one of them with the old memorial's second note. Here, among the flowers, she could find no useful comparison at all.

She sat on a flat stone and took the bent brace from her pack.

Its shape had not changed. One edge was twisted where it had caught beneath the memorial cover. It was a small failure, made of a material she knew well, and she could explain precisely how it had happened.

The letters had no such explanation.

Neither did the cube.

She returned the brace to its sleeve.

A little farther down the path, someone was singing.

The voice came and went among the trees, too soft at first to distinguish from the rustle of leaves. Then a clear low melody rose over the grass, followed by the light sound of footsteps.

Brunti stood.

A young Verdani woman moved along the winding path, turning with the song. She wore a deep-blue robe whose hem brushed the grass before lifting with each step. Flowers were woven among the horns that curved from her head, and more blossoms rested in her hair. Where she moved, a faint shimmer like water caught the light, mingling with other subtle motions that vanished as quickly as they appeared.

She was not alone in her dance. The breeze moved the flowers, and the flowers seemed to answer her.

The woman noticed Brunti and slowed.

“Care for a dance?”

Brunti glanced behind her, certain the invitation must be meant for someone else.

There was no one there.

“The ground is uneven,” she said.

“Then we can take small steps.”

“You ask strangers often?”

“Only the ones who stop to listen.”

Brunti had no ready answer.

The young woman smiled and stepped aside, making room on the path.

Brunti looked down at her feet. They were broad, fitted for stone and uncertain ground, not the quick turning steps of the stranger before her. She had danced at Skyland convergence festivals, of course, but those dances had been measured things, full of weight and shared rhythm.

This song slipped between the beats she expected.

“I may be poor company,” she said.

“Then we can begin badly and improve.”

Brunti hesitated.

The woman's smile did not change. She was waiting without urging.

Brunti moved one foot.

The Verdani turned, blue cloth following the movement a moment later. Brunti followed with a second step, then a third. The path was narrow, and at first her motions were stiff. Her boots pressed shallow marks into the soil. She thought of how heavy she must seem beside the dancer's light turns.

Then the melody changed.

The Verdani slowed, giving Brunti room to find it. Brunti shifted her weight, let her shoulders follow, and made a smaller turn.

They came almost face to face.

“That was better,” the woman said.

“You changed the song.”

“I did.”

“To fit me?”

“To make room.”

Brunti looked at her.

For a moment she remembered the memorial passage: two notes, one late and low, caught beneath a brace she had forced into place.

She might have told the stranger about it. She might have opened her notebook and shown her the seven letters, the cube, the copied ledger mark. She might have asked whether the song carried some hidden meaning she was meant to hear.

Instead she tried the next step.

The Verdani laughed softly, and Brunti found herself laughing too.

They stopped beside a patch of tall grass. The last fold of blue cloth settled while the melody continued for another moment.

“My name is Sira,” the woman said.

“Brunti.”

“Are you traveling alone?”

“For now.”

“Toward Aetherion?”

“Yes.”

“So am I.”

Brunti looked at her with fresh attention.

“Do you know the city's archives?”

“I know the people who carry messages to them. Why?”

“I need to find a drawing of an old repair.”

Sira nodded slowly. “That sounds like a sensible thing to look for.”

“It is.”

“More sensible than dancing with strangers?”

Brunti considered.

“Perhaps not.”

Sira reached for a small traveling bag resting beside the path. A few bundles of letters and folded cloth were fastened to it. When she lifted it, the flowers among her horns stirred in the breeze.

“I have messages to deliver,” she said. “Some written. Some spoken. The spoken ones are harder to carry if the person listening expects them to mean something else.”

Brunti thought of the seven letters in her notebook.

“I may have that problem.”

“Most people do.”

They began walking.

Sira's pace was unhurried, though she stepped lightly over the roots that crossed the path. Brunti had to shorten her stride once, then again. Neither spoke for a while.

The glades opened gradually toward a road used by travelers bound for the city. Evening light rested on the grass. Somewhere behind them, the flowers retained their faint brightness beneath the clouds.

Brunti listened to Sira humming as they walked.

She did not ask what the melody meant.

Not yet.
