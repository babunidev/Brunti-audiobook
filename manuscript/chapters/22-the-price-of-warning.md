# Chapter Twenty-Two — The Price of Warning

The first warning cost them a button.

It was a perfectly ordinary button, carved from grey stone, with two holes drilled through the middle. Brunti had carried it in her repair pouch since leaving home. She had meant to replace a missing fastening on her travel cloak, but in the months since then the cloak had acquired three different fastenings, none of which quite matched the others.

The old merchant examined the button in his palm.

“An heirloom,” he declared.

“No,” Brunti said.

“Prehistoric, perhaps.”

“I made it last winter.”

“A remarkably well-preserved specimen.”

He tucked it away before she could decide whether he was joking.

They had found him at the edge of Keldor's outer trade path, crouching in a gloomy recess between two trees. A moth-eaten cloth lay spread before him. Small skull talismans, bones, old amulets and objects too corroded to name occupied untidy piles. Above them drooped a battered wizard's hat, its brim so low that only the merchant's watchful eyes were visible.

A dry clatter came from the merchandise when he shifted his knee.

“Now,” he said. “What did you want?”

“A report of the old market road,” Sira replied.

He picked up a charm that looked like two bent nails wrapped in string.

“This will protect you from its darker turns. Very ancient. Very rare.”

The cord tangled around his knuckles.

Velen waited while he muttered at it. Brunti watched the string wind around his thumb, then tighten as he tried to pull it free. The merchant swore with considerable invention.

“Would you like help?” Brunti asked.

“Certainly not.”

He twisted the charm the other way. The cord loosened and fell into his lap.

“An exceptionally difficult enchantment,” he said.

“It was a knot,” Velen observed.

“Everything is a knot to people who have no imagination.”

Varun, who had led them from the last safe wayhouse, stood by a crooked oak with the look of a man who had heard this exchange many times.

“We need the road that goes past the old stone culvert,” he said. “Is it still in use?”

The merchant's eyes flicked toward him.

“Depends who is asking.”

“People looking for missing travelers.”

“Then it's certainly dangerous.”

Brunti leaned forward.

“You've seen the danger?”

“I've sold things to people who have.”

“What did you see yourself?”

The old man looked offended, as though she had asked him to distinguish one kind of coin from another.

“Yesterday I saw two fellows going west with sacks over their shoulders. One had a broken boot. Neither would pay my asking price for a perfectly serviceable charm.”

“Did they have a child with them?”

“No.”

“Did either carry a walking stick?”

“One carried a stick. It was a branch. People do that in forests.”

Velen opened their notebook.

“Where did you see them?”

The merchant pointed along the path.

“Beyond the split oak. They came out from behind the old culvert, where the stone channel runs dry in summer.”

“Did you recognize them?”

“One of them buys food for people who work at a shelter clearing farther west.”

“How do you know that?”

“Because he said so.”

“Did you sell him food?”

“No. I sell rarities.”

Brunti looked over the cloth. A spoon lay beneath one of the skull talismans. Its handle was bent and its bowl clean enough to reflect a sliver of grey sky.

“You sell spoons,” she said.

“An ancient table implement.”

“How much?”

“Two silver pieces.”

“For that?”

“You may have the button back if you're going to be difficult.”

Brunti held out her hand.

He regarded her steadily, then gave the button back with a small sigh.

“The old culvert is real,” he said. “The shelter clearing is real, too. I have seen its people come this way. That is what I can tell you.”

“And the charms?”

“Not for you, apparently.”

Sira placed a few ordinary trading coins on the cloth.

“For the information,” she said.

He did not refuse them.

As they turned away, the merchant lifted the tangled charm and began working its cord free again.

“Mind the rooster,” he called after them.

Brunti stopped.

“What rooster?”

“The expensive kind. Belongs to people who dislike visitors.”

“Have you seen it?”

“Yes,” the merchant said. “I've been trying to sell it a charm for months. The beast hasn't a coin to its name.”

***

The road west began as a pair of wheel ruts. Before long, grass and leaf litter covered one of them. Roots crossed the other at irregular angles, requiring the pack animals to step carefully.

They had brought no cart. Varun carried a small bundle of provisions and a length of cord. Sira had packed clean water, bandages and a few folded pieces of cloth used by the relief stations to mark safe resting places. Velen carried copies of the witness reports. Brunti bore the heavier tools and supplies.

It was the first morning of the two-day search they had agreed upon.

No one had brought a magical key.

The old merchant's warning kept its place among the things they had heard, though Brunti wondered how seriously a traveler ought to take a man who tried to sell a spoon as a relic.

“His price was outrageous,” Velen said.

“You were tempted,” Sira replied.

“By the spoon?”

“By the story around it.”

“It's an interesting spoon.”

“You can buy a better one at the relief station.”

“One with fewer mysteries.”

“That's usually an advantage when you're eating,” Brunti said.

The path dipped toward a line of broken stones. A channel had once carried water under the road, but its upper bank had collapsed and left a low, dry opening. Moss covered the remaining masonry. An old cart wheel lay half buried among the ferns.

Varun examined the far side.

“The culvert,” he said. “Just as the merchant told us.”

Velen marked the location.

“His story contains one verified place,” they said.

“It may contain several,” Sira answered. “We don't know yet.”

Varun pointed toward a line of narrow prints in the damp ground near the opening.

“People have passed here.”

Brunti knelt. The marks showed the edge of a worn boot and the scrape of something heavy being dragged across the stone.

“Not a child,” she said.

“Not enough to tell,” Varun replied.

The print was wide. It might have belonged to someone carrying supplies, or someone with feet too large for the small traveler described in Sira's letter. There was no reason to force it into either account.

They followed the channel for a short distance before returning to the higher track.

Birdsong was absent beneath the dense canopy. The old stones made a narrow pale line among the roots, and from somewhere beyond them came a sudden burst of laughter.

It was harsh, broken, and ended in a guttural snarl.

Varun raised his hand.

***

The figure crouched beside a fallen tree.

He was small and hunched, with ragged clothing hanging unevenly from his body. One shoulder rose higher than the other. Deep scars marked his crooked face, and a sickly green light flickered in his restless eyes.

A Gobbl.

His long fingers scraped across the bark of the fallen trunk, leaving thin grooves. He muttered something Brunti could not understand. Uneven yellow teeth flashed between a grin and a grimace.

His right arm jerked upward.

The movement was so sudden that Brunti nearly stepped forward to catch him. Before she moved, he seized the raised arm with his other hand and held it against his chest. His whole body trembled with the effort.

For an instant he became still.

Then his head turned sharply toward the opening where the travelers stood. The grin vanished. His features drew into a snarl.

Sira stopped beside Brunti.

“Don't approach,” Varun whispered.

The Gobbl released his arm, slowly, as though expecting it to betray him again. Both clawed hands spread wide. His attention fixed on the patch of open ground between the fallen tree and the road.

Brunti kept her hands where he could see them.

In the Vale she had known Gobbls who traded, quarreled, boasted and laughed with the same variety of reasons as anyone else. This being's shape and behavior were different. The green light in his eyes did not make all his people dangerous. It made him a person they did not know how to approach safely.

His left arm twitched.

He flinched from his own movement.

Brunti felt a terrible, familiar urge to fix something.

Sira's voice came very softly.

“He may be frightened of us.”

“He may,” Brunti said.

“Or not know where he is.”

“We don't know.”

The Gobbl took one uneven step. His fingers curled, then tightened against his palm. The snarl returned, sharp enough to make Velen retreat against the tree behind them.

Varun guided them away from the opening and along a higher bank where the creature did not have to watch them pass directly before him.

Brunti looked back once.

The Gobbl had lowered himself again beside the fallen trunk. He held his right arm against his body. For a little while his eyes remained fixed on the empty space they had left.

They did not touch him.

They did not call him a monster.

At the next marker, Sira wrote down the location so that the forest wardens could decide whether anyone equipped to help could approach without worsening his condition.

Velen hesitated before putting the account in their book.

“Unhinged Gobbl,” they wrote.

Brunti saw the phrase.

“That's the name people use for him?”

“It's the name in the reports.”

“Then write that it's a name they use.”

Velen added a line beneath it.

*Condition observed. Cause unknown.*

They continued along the raised path.

***

The next warning came from a woman carrying clean linen.

She emerged from a side track where two trees leaned together above the road. Her boots were muddy, and a bundle of rolled cloth rested against her shoulder. When she saw Varun, she lowered it with relief.

“I thought the main track was closed,” she said.

“Only the lower crossing,” he replied. “Are you coming from the western shelter?”

“Yes. They asked me to fetch more cloth. We have people there who need clean bindings.”

Sira stepped forward.

“Have you seen a young Verdani with a long walking stick?”

The woman frowned.

“A child?”

“We're trying to find out. A small traveler was seen near the outer routes, but the reports don't agree.”

“I haven't seen a child walking alone,” the woman said. “Not today.”

Her name was Ivera. She knew the shelter clearing that the grumpy merchant had mentioned, though she approached it from a different track. It was tended by people who stayed in the forest to help travelers injured or confused by its changes.

“There's a safer way around the low roots,” she said. “But keep away from the old hollow north of the path.”

“Why?” Brunti asked.

Ivera shifted the linen on her shoulder.

“Something lives there.”

“What have you seen?”

“Not much. I saw the leaves move when there was no wind. Long thin limbs came out of a tangle of dead plants. The body looked as if it was made of rotting leaves and corrupted flesh.”

Varun's expression hardened.

“Fearfeaster?”

“That's what the wardens call it.”

Ivera described narrow joints bent at strange angles and talons thin as needles gripping the roots. Its grin had been impossibly wide, teeth exposed even when it stood still. Small eyes had moved rapidly from shadow to shadow.

“What did it do?” Sira asked.

“It dragged one long claw along the bark. Such a sound.” Ivera touched the edge of her sleeve. “Then it stopped and listened. I thought it could hear me being afraid.”

“Did it come toward you?”

“Not before I left.”

“Did it hurt anyone you know?”

“There's a story about someone who went into that hollow and didn't return. I never knew the person.”

Velen wrote the distinction down.

“People say it feeds on fear,” Ivera continued. “I can't prove that. But its grin got wider when I froze. I still remember that.”

Brunti glanced toward the dark trees.

Fear could make distance feel short and sounds feel close. Whether the creature fed upon that fear, sensed movement, or simply watched its surroundings was more than Ivera had seen.

“What route did you use to leave?” Brunti asked.

“The creek bed. It leads back to the outer road before the hollow.”

“Would you show us the turning?”

Ivera nodded.

She led them to a fork marked by two stones stacked one above the other. The right-hand trail entered a stand of dead trees. The left followed a shallow, damp channel beneath ferns.

“Take the creek,” she said. “It joins the old service way near a fallen bridge.”

Velen copied the route.

Sira gave Ivera a portion of their clean cloth in exchange for one of the marked strips the western shelter used to identify its messengers. They had not made a bargain for secrets; they had swapped supplies each group could spare.

As the woman started back toward the eastern road, she paused.

“You're looking for someone small?”

“Yes,” Sira said.

“Then mind which stories you choose. Children hear enough fear without us adding to it.”

When she had gone, Brunti studied the two paths.

“We could search the hollow,” she said.

Varun looked at her.

“Do we have a reason to think our child went there?”

“No.”

“Then why would we?”

She watched a leaf roll slowly across the entrance to the darker path.

“Because it's there,” she admitted.

Sira took her hand briefly, then let it go.

“The creek,” she said.

They followed the water.

***

By late afternoon the forest had changed its sound again.

The creek ran shallow beneath a greenish canopy. Its water smelled of wet stone and decaying leaves. Farther on, the channel narrowed into a culvert built under an old supply path. Velen checked the position against Ivera's directions, and Varun tested the footing.

At the other end of the channel, the air carried the smell of spilled grain.

Brunti heard a sharp, indignant cluck.

On the forest floor beyond the culvert stood a black rooster.

He was magnificent in the way a small creature can be when convinced the world is watching. His glossy feathers shimmered as he strutted between the roots, tall comb swaying above a fierce little face. Wicked spurs lifted through the leaf litter with every deliberate step.

He stopped beside a scattering of discarded food.

One peck.

Then another.

His head rose with the expression of a critic disappointed by a particularly careless kitchen.

Sira pressed her lips together.

“Is that the dangerous rooster?”

Varun nodded without smiling.

“Firecrest Bloodbeak.”

Velen stared.

“The one the merchant warned us about?”

“The one,” Varun said. “The bandits reward him with scraps and shelter. He makes enough noise for the whole forest when strangers come near.”

Brunti looked past the bird.

On the far side of the old supply path stood the remains of a low shed. A few pieces of cloth hung from a branch. A small fire had been covered with earth, but smoke still escaped in thin threads. Whoever had used the place might not be far away.

The rooster turned his head.

One bright eye fixed on the narrow opening beneath the culvert.

Brunti lowered herself behind the masonry. Velen crouched beside her. Sira remained motionless, looking at the bird with the strained concentration of someone trying not to laugh.

The rooster forgot the morsel at his feet.

He straightened to his full height, spread his wings just a little, and drew a breath.

For a heartbeat the whole forest seemed to listen.

Then he crowed.

It was a great, furious sound.

The note tore through the trees and ended in a series of abrupt, demanding cries. Brunti felt the vibration through the stone where her palm rested.

Somewhere beyond the shed, a branch cracked.

Varun signaled them to remain still.

A voice answered from the trees.

“What now?”

Another voice said something Brunti could not make out.

The rooster crowed again.

Velen looked down at their paper case. One brass clasp had caught against a stone. They freed it slowly, keeping the metal from scraping.

Brunti could have thrown a pebble to distract the bird. She could have rushed out and frightened it into flight. The thought passed and went.

The bird had already sounded the alarm. Changing his mind would not recall the noise.

They waited beneath the culvert while two figures moved along the far edge of the clearing. One carried a short staff. The other lifted a cloth bundle from the shed and looked toward the place where the rooster stood.

Neither came close enough for Brunti to see a face.

The figures spoke briefly and then went back among the trees.

Varun leaned near Brunti's shoulder.

“Those are the people I won't ask you to meet.”

“Bandits?”

“Likely. I know the shed is used by people who demand payments from travelers. I can't name the two who just passed.”

The distinction mattered.

The rooster strutted over the ground again, apparently satisfied that he had fulfilled his responsibilities. He found a better scrap beneath the leaves and began pecking at it.

Sira whispered, “He has a terrible job.”

“An excellent job,” Velen whispered back. “Someone feeds him for shouting.”

Brunti nearly laughed.

Then she looked at the figures' fading path.

“Does the western shelter trade with these people?”

Varun shook his head.

“Not willingly. There's a second route around the shed. We can take it when the light changes.”

They waited until the last movement beyond the clearing had stopped. Then Varun led them back along the creek and through a stand of low alders, keeping the culvert between them and the shed.

They did not harm the rooster.

They did not discover whether he would have welcomed a charm.

***

A different bird watched the next stretch of path.

At first Brunti noticed only a pair of blue points high among the branches. They were too steady to be reflected water and too sharp to belong to the pale flowers growing beneath the trees.

A raven sat on a gnarled limb.

Its feathers lay rough along its body. Some patches moved independently, rising and settling as though the plumage could not agree on the direction of the breeze. The bird's blue eyes fixed upon a small movement in the roots below.

A caw broke against the trunks.

Harsh.

Oddly doubled.

Velen flinched.

The raven turned its head abruptly. Its body followed after a slight delay, making the motion look poorly joined. Curved talons tightened against the bark.

“Blightwatcher,” Varun breathed.

Brunti studied the creature.

It lowered itself along the branch, jagged feathers lifting from its shoulders. Their shadows trembled in the slanting moonlight that had begun to reach through the canopy. Another caw started, stopped short, and left an uncomfortable quiet.

Its wings opened just enough to show the dark space beneath them.

Its grip loosened.

Sira stepped back from the open ground.

The raven watched something beneath the leaves. A small creature moved there, scarcely visible except for the trembling of a fern. The raven's eyes followed it with an attention that made the reason for its curved talons plain.

The forest wardens' accounts described Blightwatchers as birds whose corruption had turned them toward living flesh. Brunti could see the restless plumage and abnormal eyes. She could see the way the bird watched the ground.

She could not see what it would do next.

A gust passed through the canopy.

The raven gave another fractured cry and leapt to a higher branch. Its rough feathers scattered a few dark fragments into the air. It vanished behind a mass of twisted leaves before the travelers could decide whether it had pursued its prey.

Sira let out a slow breath.

“Another warning.”

“Not the same kind,” Brunti replied.

Velen looked back along the route they had come.

“The rooster warned someone about us. The raven might not have noticed us at all.”

Varun nodded.

“That's why I don't treat every cry in this forest as a signal. Sometimes a creature is simply making the noise it makes.”

Brunti looked at the crooked line of the path.

“And sometimes the sound tells us something about the creature, not about the road.”

“Exactly,” Varun said.

They moved on, more quietly than before.

***

At the next crossing the path split three ways.

A strip of faded cloth hung from a low branch. Another was tied around a stone farther down the left-hand way. Both carried the same simple mark that Ivera had given Sira: two short blue lines sewn beside one green.

The western shelter's sign.

Velen examined the first strip.

“Is it recent?”

“The stitching is cleaner than the cloth,” Sira said.

“That doesn't tell us when it was tied here.”

“No. But it tells us the people who work at the clearing have a way of marking their approach.”

Varun knelt beside a shallow cart groove.

“Supplies have come through recently.”

“Perhaps the two sacks the merchant saw?” Brunti asked.

“Perhaps,” Varun said. “I won't swear they were the same people.”

The right-hand track bent toward thicker fog. The center rose onto a root-covered ridge and disappeared behind leaning trunks. The left followed the cloth markers toward what looked, from the edge of the clearing, like a line of old garden stones.

Sira touched the green stitching.

“These are the signs Ivera trusted.”

“Then we'll follow them,” Brunti said.

They moved slowly, keeping the return markers visible. Brunti carried the heavier bundle that had begun to drag at Velen's shoulder. Sira counted the strips, and Varun checked that the path had not been blocked since the last cart passed.

The forest remained difficult.

Roots crossed the ground without warning. Branches bent into shapes that looked like doorways and then proved to be nothing but branches. Twice the party stopped because a sound came from the wrong direction. Once it was water striking a hollow stone; once, a loose length of wood tapping against a trunk.

Brunti found herself wanting to ask every noise whether it meant danger.

Instead she listened for the sound of people at work.

Eventually a faint knocking reached them.

Not the urgent clatter of the merchant's bones. Not the Gobbl's broken laughter. Not the rooster's alarm or the raven's double caw.

Someone was striking wood with a small hammer.

A second sound followed: the scrape of a bucket against stone.

Varun lowered his hand.

“That's the clearing.”

***

A low hedge of woven branches surrounded a patch of ground where the trees had been thinned rather than felled. Beyond it stood a small roof made from layered timber and repaired canvas. A lantern burned beside its entrance, casting a warm stripe across the damp earth.

Two figures were moving in the light.

One carried a bucket from a narrow channel. The other held a length of timber against a damaged support while someone inside the shelter tapped a wooden peg into place.

The work was slow, practical and unmistakably alive.

Brunti stopped at the outer marker.

She had spent the day hearing warnings from people and creatures who each knew only a portion of the forest. Some had tried to sell certainty. Others had spoken cautiously, admitting what they could not prove. The warnings had not agreed, but they had kept her from treating every possible path as one she was entitled to take.

The person with the bucket saw them first.

“Travelers?”

Varun raised his empty hands.

“From the outer road. We have supplies and a question.”

The figure set the bucket down, then approached only far enough to see them clearly.

“Are you hurt?”

“No,” Sira replied. “We're looking for someone who may be.”

The person glanced at the supplies on Brunti's shoulder.

“Then come to the gate. We have room to listen.”

Velen opened their notebook, but Brunti touched the edge of it lightly.

“First,” she said, “we should ask what they need.”

Sira looked at her and smiled.

Beyond the woven hedge, the small hammer sounded again. A measured tap, followed by another. Someone kept working while strangers approached, and a lantern burned steadily above the open gate.

For the moment, there was enough light to see where the path led.
