# Chapter Fifteen — Beneath the Reeds

The first thing Karrhu took from Brunti was a boot.

Not the whole boot. Only the left one, which sank between two submerged roots when she stepped off the ferry landing. The swamp gripped it with such determination that her foot came free while the boot remained buried to the ankle.

She stood with one bare mineral foot in black water.

Sira looked at her.

Velen looked at the boot.

The ferryman, who had warned them twice about stepping beyond the boards, said nothing.

“It's still there,” Brunti observed.

“Yes,” Sira said.

“Would someone hold my pack?”

Velen took it without comment. Brunti bent, placed one hand against a broad root for balance and drew the boot carefully out of the mud. It emerged with a deep, reluctant sound and a fringe of green weed hanging from the buckle.

The ferryman began to laugh.

“I did tell you,” he said.

“You did,” Brunti admitted.

She emptied the boot, rinsed it in the shallower water at the landing, and stepped onto the boards. The ferryman was an elderly Verdani with the shifting translucence of river water beneath his weathered traveling clothes. His name was Calen. He had brought the three companions through the last of the raised causeways and across the widening black channels, after the caravan guides from Ohgar had delivered them to the western wetland stations.

They had spent days passing from dry scrub to damp woodland, then into country where the earth no longer trusted a road to remain in one place.

Karrhu was not merely wet.

Water occupied the hollows between enormous roots. Reeds rose above dark pools, some taller than Brunti. Fallen wood grew moss, and moss gathered new growth upon itself. Somewhere beneath the near bank something made a low bubbling sound; elsewhere a branch snapped without anything visible moving above the surface.

The smell of rain was gone. Here the air held damp bark, stagnant water and the deep, warm odor of things returning slowly to the earth.

Calen pushed the ferry pole into the mud and examined the sky.

“We have enough light to reach the reed station,” he said. “If no one insists upon visiting every interesting pool.”

Brunti refastened her muddy boot.

“That depends upon what the pools contain.”

“Mostly water.”

“Mostly?”

He grinned and turned along the boardwalk.

***

The duck stood on a mound of mud as though it owned the swamp.

It was a small bird, scarcely reaching Brunti's knee, with feathers that turned from oily green to copper as it shifted beneath the broken daylight. Ember-red flashed along the edges of its wings. Its bright eyes fixed upon the approaching travelers.

A sharp hiss came from its beak.

Sira stopped.

Calen had stopped two strides earlier.

“What's wrong?” Brunti asked.

“Nothing yet,” the ferryman answered.

Steam curled faintly from the duck's beak. Around its thin feet the water trembled in little rings that crossed one another among the rotting reeds.

Brunti crouched.

“An extraordinary thing.”

“Brunti,” Sira said.

“The colors—”

The duck opened its beak.

A short burst of fire struck the damp air.

Flame swept across the mud mound, scattering sparks that vanished in wet grass. A few stalks blackened. The smell of scorched marsh plants rose sharply above the swamp's rot.

Brunti recoiled. One spark landed on the end of her scarf before she could pull it away. Sira struck the cloth with a quick ribbon of water, leaving the corner soaked and smoking.

The duck gave a harsh rasping cry, shook its iridescent feathers and stood perfectly still.

Brunti stared at it.

“It is a duck.”

“Yes,” Calen said.

“It has just set fire to the swamp.”

“Only a little of it.”

Sira wrung water from the edge of Brunti's scarf.

“Did you see the steam before the flame?”

“I did.”

“And when I stopped?”

“I thought you were looking at the colors.”

“I was looking at its beak.”

Brunti examined the scorched patch where her scarf had been.

“Perhaps I should have looked there.”

Calen made a small sound that might have been another laugh.

Sira moved between Brunti and the bird.

“We're going around it.”

The duck hissed again.

No one argued.

They backed along the boardwalk and took a narrow side path where the roots rose close enough together to support old planks. Behind them another brief flare illuminated the reeds, followed by the bird's offended cry.

Velen looked over their shoulder.

“Does anyone know why it can do that?”

“Someone once blamed a sorcerer,” Calen said. “Someone else blamed an ancestor who argued with a fire spirit. I have never known a duck to confirm either tale.”

Sira glanced at Brunti.

“Perhaps the answer is that we should not ask it.”

Brunti looked back at the little smoky mound.

The bird was preening.

***

The Aradia occupied a place that looked peaceful enough to be mistaken for a rest stop.

The boardwalk widened around a sheltered pool. Thin shafts of sun reached the murky water through the canopy, making the surface shine beneath the shadows of hanging moss. A pale perch rose from the bank, its upper length worn smooth by years of weather.

Something silver glimmered above it.

At first Brunti saw only fragments of light.

Then the wings moved.

The Aradia dragonfly rested on the perch with its long body suspended above the water. Silver flecks caught the sultry daylight along its wings, spreading quick reflections across the dark surface below. A thin buzz rose as it vibrated, soft but sharp enough to separate from the insects calling among the reeds.

Its elongated body ended in a sting angled outward.

A bead glistened on the point.

Calen raised one hand.

“Stay behind the marker.”

Brunti looked down.

A strip of faded red cloth had been tied around a low post at the edge of the walk. Water and years had dulled the color, but the warning remained visible.

Velen stopped beside it.

“Is that venom?”

“Yes,” Calen said. “The Aradia's sting is a bad thing to carry home.”

Sira studied the insect, its wings almost still while their reflections continued to tremble on the water.

“Could we pass without disturbing it?”

“Perhaps. On a better day.”

“What makes this one worse?”

“The walkway is narrow beyond the perch. Your friend is made of stone. She will feel the boards shift, and so will the dragonfly.”

Brunti looked at the boards. Several had swollen and lifted at their ends. Even walking lightly, she would make the wood answer beneath her.

“We could wait.”

“We could,” Calen said. “Or use the longer channel. That one has an old stone crossing.”

“How much longer?”

“Long enough for you to become annoyed about it.”

Brunti folded her arms.

“I have already been annoyed about a boot.”

“Then you're well practiced.”

The Aradia lifted slightly. Its silver wings trembled, and the bead at its stinger flashed in a shaft of sunlight. It turned toward the open water.

Sira stepped backward first.

“This way,” she said.

Velen followed. Brunti gave the perch one last look and went with them.

The detour took them through a flooded stretch where a low stone causeway remained just visible beneath the water. Brunti tested each step before placing her weight. It was slow going. Twice Sira had to wait while Velen secured their papers against a sudden spray of water.

By the time they reached the next landing, the afternoon had begun to fade.

Calen examined his ferry pole.

“The reed station is farther than I'd like in this light.”

“We can continue,” Brunti said.

“Perhaps,” he answered. “But the lower walk floods when the evening tide of the channels meets the rain runoff. We may need to stop at the roots.”

Sira looked along the darkening water.

“Then we stop where it is safe.”

Brunti was about to object that they had already lost half a day.

A small voice came from the trees.

“Is anyone carrying bread?”

***

The child stood among the roots of a leaning tree.

She was Verdani, very young, with the fine branching horns of a child and a narrow face that seemed almost translucent in the damp light. Droplets clung to her hair. Her clothing was woven from leaves, but the folds had torn and darkened with mud.

She held a small loaf in her hands.

It was made of earth.

The little girl turned it carefully, smoothing the sides with her fingers before the shape broke against her palm. She looked at the pieces, then at the travelers, her emerald eyes dulled by hunger.

“I asked for bread,” she said, barely louder than the dripping water.

Sira knelt a few paces away.

“We have food. What is your name?”

“Leta.”

“Where have you come from, Leta?”

“The little house beyond the tall reeds.”

“Is someone there with you?”

“My aunt. She went to fetch the ferry when the water rose. I waited on the boardwalk, but the boards went under.”

Calen looked at the bank beyond her. A narrow row of posts emerged from the water. The missing planks between them were not visible.

“That's the old reed route,” he said quietly. “It was flooded after yesterday's rain.”

Leta brushed the mud from her fingers.

“I tried to make cakes. Auntie said we would eat when she came back.”

Brunti lowered her pack to the dry root beside her.

She had food. Dried roots, a little grain, mineral salt for herself and a soft parcel of bread Sira had bought at the last station.

She took out the bread.

“Would this help?”

The girl stared.

“Is it real?”

Brunti broke off a piece and held it out.

Leta did not reach for it immediately.

“Will you need it?”

“We have more food for our journey,” Sira said. “And we can sit while you eat.”

The child accepted it with both hands.

She took one careful bite, then another, her shoulders loosening as though she had been holding them up by effort alone. Sira filled a small cup with clean water from their own store and waited while Leta drank.

Brunti watched the mud cakes crumble beside the root.

“Have you eaten today?” she asked.

Leta shook her head.

“Yesterday?”

“A little.”

Calen rested his pole against the tree.

“She needs a safe way back to her aunt. The old route will not carry her until the water falls.”

“Where is the reed station?” Sira asked.

“Beyond that channel. There are people there who know her family.”

Brunti looked toward the darkening pools.

“We could carry her.”

“Over what?” Calen asked.

Brunti followed his gaze.

Between them and the station lay a stretch of water broken by submerged roots and strips of mud. It was not a path for a tired child. Even a Grimnir could lose footing there while carrying someone.

Sira turned to Leta.

“Do you want to stay here while Calen finds help, or would you rather come with us if we can make the crossing safely?”

Leta took another bite and considered.

“Can I have the rest of the bread first?”

“Of course,” Sira said.

Brunti smiled.

It was a much easier answer to give than one about the route.

***

Calen knew of a stone crossing farther upstream, where an old root wall held the mud in place. It was passable when the water stood low.

They would need to see whether the evening runoff had reached it.

He went ahead with the pole while Brunti gathered the child's damp belongings into a cloth. There was little to carry: a leaf-woven bag, a broken wooden comb, and a small string of smooth beads.

Leta watched Brunti wrap them.

“Don't lose the blue one,” she said.

Brunti examined the beads. Only one was blue, a little stone flecked with pale silver.

“I won't.”

The child rose when she had finished eating. Her knees shook. Sira offered a hand, and they walked slowly along the roots while Velen kept the dry side of the path clear.

By the time they reached the crossing, light had drained from the water. The old stones formed a narrow line across a shallow channel, their upper surfaces slick with green growth. Beyond them the roots rose into a dry island.

Calen tested the first stone.

“Still holding.”

Brunti took the child's bag.

“I can carry her if she wishes.”

Leta looked at the dark water.

“Will you drop me?”

“I will do everything I can not to.”

“That is not the same as no.”

“No,” Brunti said. “It isn't.”

Leta considered the answer, then held out her arms.

Brunti lifted her carefully. The Verdani child's body was lighter than she expected, with a cool shifting quality beneath the damp leaf-woven clothes. Leta rested one hand against Brunti's crystal shoulder and held the other around the blue-beaded string.

They crossed one stone at a time.

Sira went before them, testing each surface with a shallow ribbon of water. Calen stayed behind, ready to brace the pole if Brunti slipped. Velen carried both document cases across the first half of the ford and waited at the next safe place.

Near the middle, a loose stone tilted.

Brunti stopped.

She felt the shift through her foot before it became visible. The stone was settling toward a hollow underneath.

“Don't step,” Sira said.

“I wasn't going to.”

Leta gripped Brunti's shoulder.

“Are we stuck?”

“Only for a moment.”

Calen slid the pole beneath the edge of the stone and steadied it while Brunti moved her weight to the next support. Sira held out a hand, not to pull the child away, but to keep her attention on the safe landing.

They reached the island as the first true darkness settled among the trees.

Leta looked at Brunti.

“You did not drop me.”

“No.”

“Good.”

She sounded so satisfied that Velen began to laugh.

Across the channel, a lamp answered from the reed station.

***

The woman who came to meet them was not Leta's aunt.

She was a keeper named Olri, with tall horns adorned in thin river grass and a broad cloak against the evening damp. She listened while Calen explained the flooded walkway, the child at the roots and the route they had taken.

Then she looked at Leta.

“Your aunt came through at dusk looking for you.”

Leta's face changed.

“Where is she?”

“Upstairs, keeping a lamp by the window.”

The little girl ran the last few steps.

A door opened above the landing. Someone called her name. The sound that followed was too full of relief to be anything like a proper sentence.

Brunti stood beside the ferry pole, watching the figures meet beneath the lantern.

Olri returned after escorting them inside.

“Thank you,” she said.

“Is the route marked as flooded?” Calen asked.

“It was, at the last station. The notice never reached this side after the boards washed out.”

“Then we should fix that before more people use it.”

Olri looked toward the half-submerged posts.

“We'll send a runner at first light. I can have the western landing closed tonight.”

Velen opened their notebook.

“May I write the warning for the relay?”

“Please do.”

Brunti offered to help with a simple marker.

Olri pointed to a spare red board resting beside the landing.

“Painted sign, two posts, crossbar. Make it low enough the fog doesn't hide it, high enough the water won't carry it away.”

Brunti admired the clarity of the instructions.

“Now that,” she said, “is a drawing I understand.”

They worked beneath the lantern while Sira brought warm food to the child and her aunt. When the sign was finished, Calen took it to the western landing with another station worker, and Velen sent the closure notice through the keeper's message pouch.

No one declared the swamp safe.

One route had simply become less likely to mislead the next traveler.

***

The buzzing woke Brunti before dawn.

It reached the station through the boards beneath her resting slab, a low restless pulse that seemed to travel through the wet ground. The sound was not like a wasp near an ear. It was too deep, too even, and threaded with the scrape of something dry against stone.

Velen stood by the open shutter.

“Do you hear it?”

“Yes.”

“Calen says it comes from the grotto to the east.”

Brunti sat up.

“Is it close to the route?”

“Closer than the flooded crossing.”

Olri entered before they could leave.

“The lower reed path is closed,” she said. “Something was stirring at the old grotto in the night. A fisher heard the buzzing from the western bank.”

“Has anyone been hurt?” Sira asked.

“Not today. But the old markers are gone. A stranger might take the turn for the shelter path.”

Brunti rose and reached for her hammer.

Olri looked at it.

“I asked for a sign.”

Brunti paused.

“Yes.”

“Not a battle.”

She put the hammer back.

They went with Calen and two local workers to the mouth of the old grotto. The path lay above shallow black water, where roots clung to a bank of slick stone. The air grew sour before the entrance came into view.

Then the buzzing deepened.

Wet rock framed a low cavern opening. Slime clung to the walls, and dark muck shone beneath dripping ledges. Beneath the rot lay a sweetness already turning foul, thick enough to catch in the throat.

A great fly clung to a mound of stone inside.

It was as broad as a small horse. Bristled hairs stood along its swollen body. Torn wings twitched in dry, papery tremors. Its broad red eyes caught the dim light and held it without blinking.

For a moment no one moved.

The creature's proboscis began to uncurl.

The buzz rose until Brunti felt it along the metal joints of her hammer.

Calen stepped backward.

“That's enough.”

One worker whispered, “Is it coming?”

“Not yet,” Olri answered.

Sira had already moved to the rear of the group.

“Back to the dry branch,” she said.

Brunti looked once at the creature's wet red eyes, then turned with the others. They did not run while the path was narrow. The workers retreated in a line, leaving the grotto unchallenged.

At the dry branch they found a stone post with two old holes where a warning board had once been fastened. One fixing had rotted. The other had been torn away.

Brunti examined the wood.

“It needs a new frame,” she said.

Olri nodded.

“And a route notice at the station.”

They assembled a bright board from the supplies they had brought. Velen wrote the warning in the road script used by traders, and Calen added the local waterway sign for danger ahead. Brunti fitted the frame to a sound stone base, away from the moving water. Sira marked the safer route with a strip of blue cloth.

The work took less than an hour.

When the signs were in place, they stood back.

The buzzing remained inside the grotto. It rose and fell behind the wet stone, no less strange than before.

“It will still be there tomorrow,” Brunti said.

“Yes,” Olri replied.

“Then what have we changed?”

“Whether a traveler walks into it without knowing.”

Brunti looked along the new line of markers.

That seemed worth the effort.

***

They spent the afternoon at the reed station waiting for a safe passage east.

A boatman brought a load of reed fiber for mending roofs. Two merchants arrived on the upper route, complaining of insects and praising the shelter's hot food in the same breath. A courier asked Velen to copy a damaged address from an old packet. Sira helped Olri sort the messages for the eastern relay.

Leta appeared carrying a real loaf of bread.

She walked directly to Brunti.

“This is for you,” she said.

Brunti accepted it.

“Did you make it?”

“My aunt did. I helped.”

“How?”

“I put the seeds on top.”

The loaf was small, browned along the edges and thick with herbs. A little pattern of seeds formed a crooked circle across its crust.

“It is excellent work,” Brunti said.

Leta beamed.

“It's not mud.”

“No,” Brunti said solemnly. “I checked.”

Sira covered her mouth to hide a laugh.

The child sat beside them while Brunti broke the bread into pieces. Velen accepted a small share, then asked Leta about the blue bead she had carried through the swamp.

“My aunt gave it to me,” the girl said. “It was from the old river.”

“Do you know which river?”

“No.”

Velen looked as though they might ask a second question, then stopped.

“Is it important to you?”

Leta nodded.

“Then keep it safely,” Velen said.

She tucked the bead beneath her collar and returned to the kitchen.

Brunti watched her go.

“You almost asked for its history,” she said.

“Yes,” Velen replied.

“Why didn't you?”

“Because she was giving us bread.”

***

The silver pool lay beyond the station's eastern channel, hidden among knotted roots where the old swamp grew deep.

Calen had called it the Quicksilver Quagmire. He spoke of it as one speaks of a place that is beautiful, dangerous and not especially interested in company.

“No one should walk there alone,” he told them. “The bank looks firmer than it is.”

Their eastern guide, a quiet Verdani woman named Hessa, knew a marked route past the pool toward the next crossing. They reached it near nightfall, when the sky had cleared above a lattice of branches.

The water was a perfect circle of silver.

No breeze disturbed it. The moon rested upon its surface without a ripple, and beyond the moon lay stars Brunti did not recognize. The pattern seemed deeper than the shallow pool could possibly hold. Delicate shapes gathered and dissolved within the reflection, like memories seen only from their edges.

Insects hummed among the surrounding reeds, yet none crossed the luminous circle.

Brunti stopped.

Hessa had warned them not to stray from the path. But the pool was close enough to the boardwalk that Brunti could see a gnarled root reaching down toward it, its tip almost touching the bright surface.

She knelt to look.

The stars shifted beneath the silver.

Brunti knew the night sky from her Skyland. She knew the great routes by which the stars had guided travelers through generations of motion. The constellations below her belonged to none of them.

A faint pattern formed beneath the root.

It seemed to show two shapes held near one another, then changed into something like a path branching through dark water.

Brunti felt the familiar pressure of a question.

Could the pool reveal a hidden possibility? Something within her she had not yet understood? A way to set the memorial's two stones without making either one silent?

Velen stopped beside her.

“Do you see those stars?” Brunti asked.

“Yes.”

“Do you know them?”

“No.”

“Then perhaps the water is showing another sky.”

“Perhaps it is reflecting something we do not understand.”

Brunti leaned closer.

A piece of the bank broke beneath her boot.

The sound was small, barely more than a wet click. But the root beside her moved, and a thin line of mud slid toward the pool.

Hessa caught the back of Brunti's pack.

“Not there.”

Brunti shifted her weight away from the edge. The ground where she had been kneeling sagged, and black water seeped into the footprint she had left.

Sira came beside them.

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Then come back to the boards.”

Brunti rose.

For a moment she looked again at the silver circle. The reflected stars had changed. The two shapes she thought she had seen were gone, replaced by another faint lattice of light.

She could have stayed until the pool offered a pattern she preferred.

Instead she stepped onto the safe path.

Velen remained long enough to mark the pool's location in their field notes.

“What did it show?” Sira asked as they walked.

Brunti considered.

“Stars that may not be stars.”

“Anything else?”

“Something I wanted to recognize.”

Sira did not press her.

Behind them the silver water held its perfect circle while the reeds stirred in the dark.

***

They reached the eastern ferry house by lantern light.

It stood on raised piles above a broad black channel, with a dry upper room for travelers and a lower deck for mooring boats. The air smelled of smoked herbs, wet rope and warm grain. Hessa reported the damaged signs to the keeper, who promised to send word along the route.

Sira spread her damp messages beside the hearth to dry.

Velen laid out their notes from Ohgar, the swamp crossings and the damaged grotto, keeping each account separate. Brunti set the wrapped archive sketch safely inside her pack. Her left boot was finally dry, though a trace of green mud remained in the buckle.

Outside, water struck the piles in an uneven rhythm.

A traveler near the fire was humming.

It was a gentle tune, hardly more than a handful of notes. Sira turned toward it at once.

“Where did you hear that?”

The traveler was a broad-shouldered woman carrying woven baskets of herbs. She looked up, surprised.

“In the Alluring Fens,” she said.

“Near here?”

“No. Farther back in the central lands, where the mist gathers among the old pools. I took the road through them on the way toward the Vale.”

Brunti watched Sira listen.

The woman described shallow black water beneath trees heavy with moss. Tiny fireflies drifted through pale fog. The air had smelled of wet wood and earth, and the reeds whispered whenever a breeze crossed the pools.

“One night I heard a few notes,” she said. “Not a whole song. Just enough to make me think someone was singing beyond the nearest trees.”

“Did you see anyone?” Sira asked.

“No. The melody moved. When I thought it was close, the next phrase came from farther away.”

“Did you follow it?”

The traveler smiled.

“Only as far as the dry path went. The mist was thick, and I had no reason to leave a good road for a sound.”

She hummed the notes again.

They were sweet and unfamiliar. Sira listened until the final tone faded into the ferry house.

“That is not a song I know,” she said.

“Nor I,” the traveler replied. “I remember it because it never finished.”

Velen opened their notebook.

“Would you mind telling me exactly where you were?”

The traveler named two waystations and the route between them. Velen wrote the locations down, marking the music as something heard by another person, not a sound the three companions had witnessed in Karrhu.

Brunti watched the ink dry.

The fens were elsewhere. The music had been heard at another time by another traveler. There was no reason to make it the voice of the Quicksilver pool or the buzzing fly or the night insects outside the house.

The traveler resumed her place by the fire.

Sira sat beside Brunti.

“Do you want to hear how she sang it?” Brunti asked.

“Yes.”

Brunti tried to repeat the notes.

She got the first two right and the third badly wrong. Sira laughed, then showed her the turn in the melody. Brunti tried again.

It sounded a little better.

The song still did not finish.

***

A message arrived before dawn.

The courier had come by the northern crossing, wet to the waist and grateful for a dry place by the fire. He carried packets from Aetherion and from the smaller stations along the southern trade roads. One was addressed to Velen by the Archive of Echoes. Another held a relay list for Sira's Evergrove contacts.

Velen read their packet first.

“More descriptions of altered creatures,” they said. “The archive has copied the public records I asked for. Some are dated. Several are not.”

Brunti looked at the stack.

“Anything from the northern workshops?”

“Not in this packet. Those collections will have to be examined farther along the route.”

Sira unfolded her own message.

She read it once, then again.

“From Alen?” Brunti asked.

“From two stations that received her letters. They have heard of some of the displaced families.”

“Is the child with the walking stick among them?”

“Not by name.”

Sira looked at the page.

“One party reached the southern gardens. Another went north after the crossing reopened. The lists still do not agree.”

Brunti waited.

Sira folded the letter.

“I need to ask questions in places you may not be going.”

It was not a declaration of departure. Not yet.

“Where?” Brunti asked.

“The relay houses along the western channels. There may be people there who remember which cart carried whom.”

“We can look at the routes,” Velen said. “There may be a crossing that serves both journeys for a while.”

Sira nodded.

“For a while.”

Outside, the rain began again.

It struck the ferry roof in thin, uneven taps, then grew steady. Beneath the house the dark channel carried the sound away, joining it to all the other movements of the swamp.

Brunti set her cup beside the hearth.

The old memorial drawing waited in her pack. Velen's records waited on the table. Sira's messages rested in her hands.

Three different kinds of unfinished work.

The ferry keeper opened the door to look at the weather. Beyond the threshold the reeds bent under the rain, and the black water continued along its unseen courses.

No one chose a road before morning.
