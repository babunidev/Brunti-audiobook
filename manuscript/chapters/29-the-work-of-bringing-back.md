# Chapter Twenty-Nine — The Work of Bringing Back

The Skyland came out of the morning cloud with its lower anchors shining.

Brunti stood beside the lift's railing, her sound hand wrapped around the worn timber. Above her, tier after tier of worked stone and dark metal moved through the pale air. The city was close enough for her to distinguish the old maintenance bridge and the row of narrow windows where the northern workshops kept their lamps burning after dusk.

She knew those windows.

She had watched them disappear behind her when she left.

Now several were dark. One had been widened, and a length of new metalwork ran beneath the bridge. Brunti recognized a careful support fitted where a patch of outer stone had once looked worn.

Someone had done the work while she was gone.

The lift climbed.

Velen stood beside her with their document case held close against the wind. They had tied the loose pages inside, then tied the case itself, then asked the attendant whether the upper landing was exposed to rain.

The attendant had answered no.

Velen had tied another cord anyway.

“Is that the workshop?” they asked.

“The one near the lower bridge. Mine is farther in.”

“It looks like a city that knows how to hold its own weight.”

“It does.”

Brunti watched an anchor crystal pass overhead, its deep hum traveling along the lift's frame. The note was familiar enough to settle through her body before she consciously recognized it.

There were many sounds in the Skyland.

The low resonance of its stabilizing lattice. The slight complaint of a bridge as it took the weight of a moving cart. Voices from open windows. A bell at the landing calling workers to the next shift.

And beneath them, a silence.

The familiar memorial note no longer traveled along the inner court.

Darrik's isolation had held.

***

Nemi met them on the landing with a hammer too large for her hand.

She had grown into the beginnings of a broad-shouldered Grimnir craftswoman, though she still held the hammer as if the tool might change its mind about obeying her. A new mineral band shone along one forearm.

Brunti stopped.

Nemi stared at the brace on her wrist.

“That's not a decoration.”

“No.”

“Did you lose a fight?”

“Several kinds.”

Nemi considered this.

“Did you win any?”

“I came home.”

“Darrik says that's not the same thing.”

“Darrik has probably said a great deal since I left.”

Nemi looked at the travel case.

“Are those the answers?”

“Some records. A drawing.”

“Answers would be lighter.”

Velen made a small sound behind her.

Brunti laughed.

The sound surprised her.

She had expected the Skyland's first greeting to make her feel as though she were still standing before the council, trying to explain the damage in the resting chamber. Instead, there was Nemi holding a badly balanced hammer and asking questions she had every right to ask.

“Where's Darrik?” Brunti said.

“Working.”

“Of course.”

“He said you weren't to go straight to the memorial court.”

“Because of my wrist?”

“Because you'd get in the way of the inspection.”

Brunti looked down at the brace.

“That too, I imagine.”

Nemi shifted the hammer against her shoulder.

“There's food waiting at the workshop. Someone thought you might remember to eat it if it was put in the place you always leave your tools.”

“That sounds like Darrik.”

“Actually it was the caretaker.”

Brunti's smile faded a little.

She remembered the sharp cry from the resting chamber, and the woman kneeling beside an older Grimnir whose body had been disturbed by the harmful resonance.

“How is the resident?” she asked.

“Walking again. Slowly. He still sleeps in the outer room when the night turns cold.”

Brunti closed her eyes for a moment.

Then she opened them.

“Thank you.”

Nemi gave her a puzzled look.

“For what?”

“For telling me how he is before I had to ask twice.”

***

The workshop smelled of warm metal and ground mineral dust.

Brunti knew the place in such detail that for several breaths she could do nothing but look.

Her bench stood beneath the eastern window. A few tools had been moved to different hooks. Someone had polished the measuring stones and labeled the drawers she habitually left untidy. The little practice block on which Nemi had once managed five hammer strikes rested beside a new one bearing a much cleaner row of marks.

On the table, a covered bowl waited.

Brunti lifted the cloth.

Grain, roots, and prepared minerals, warmed and seasoned the way she liked them.

She sat.

Velen placed the travel case on the driest shelf and opened it long enough to confirm the paper inside had survived the passage.

“Nothing missing,” they said.

“Except the part of the drawing that was missing before.”

“That was not going to grow back on the road.”

Brunti tasted the food.

Someone had ground the minerals fine enough that her weakened body did not have to work hard to take them in. The flavor was familiar, a little sharper than she remembered.

She ate slowly.

Darrik arrived before the bowl was empty.

He stood at the doorway without his tool case, which was almost more unsettling than if he had entered with a cart of stones. The seams of his hands were polished smooth. A thin layer of dust lay across his dark shoulders.

He studied her face, then her wrist.

“You shouldn't be carrying that hammer,” he said.

Brunti glanced at her belt.

It was the old hammer she had carried all the way through Morkahja. She had forgotten it was there.

“It's not heavy.”

“That isn't what I said.”

“No.”

He approached the bench.

For a moment neither spoke.

Then Darrik placed his palm against the uninjured side of her forearm. His touch carried a familiar, steady vibration through the mineral beneath her skin.

Brunti answered it without thinking.

The two rhythms met.

He withdrew his hand.

“You came home,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

She had expected another question.

He asked it after she finished the meal.

“Can you give an account of what happened?”

“Yes.”

“Can you give it without trying to repair the account while you tell it?”

Brunti looked at him.

“I think so.”

Darrik nodded.

“The council is ready to hear you after the middle shift. Until then, rest. The affected passage remains closed.”

“May I see the work?”

“After the account. We have kept it accessible for examination, not for wandering.”

Brunti wanted to protest that she knew the passage better than most of the people now working there.

Her wrist ached where it rested against the table.

“Then I'll wait,” she said.

Darrik looked at her for a long moment.

“That,” he said, “is a change.”

***

The council hall had the same broad table and the same low windows that overlooked the city’s moving terraces. The view beyond them had changed; a distant range of mountains lay beneath the Skyland now, its ridges pale with morning cloud.

The council had kept working while Brunti traveled.

A new noticeboard listed the descent stations still waiting for supplies. Beside it hung an account of bridge repairs and a diagram of the convergence moorings. Someone had made room on the long table for Brunti's papers.

Orven, the Skykeeper who had brought witness accounts at the beginning of her journey, sat near the end with a set of narrow tablets.

He nodded as Brunti entered.

She was glad to see him.

It meant she would not have to explain why the absence of a conclusion could still be part of an honest record.

The caretaker from the resting chamber sat beside an older memory-keeper whose hands had been worn smooth by touching the names carved in the memorial court. Darrik stood beside the prepared measurements of the damaged panel.

Velen carried the Aetherion sketch to the table and laid it in its protective folder.

Brunti remained standing until the council invited her to sit.

Her wrist was supported on a folded pad.

The first question came from the older caretaker.

“Do you understand why the room was closed?”

Brunti looked at her.

“Because I fitted a metal brace to a joint I hadn't inspected from the other side. The added pressure carried an uneven vibration into the communal resting chamber. Someone was hurt.”

“Disturbed and injured,” the caretaker said. “Not merely inconvenienced.”

“Yes.”

“You removed the brace.”

“Yes.”

“And Darrik separated the memorial's resonance from the living room afterward.”

“Yes.”

The caretaker settled back.

“Then tell us what you brought back.”

Brunti unfolded the sketch.

***

She began with the city of Aetherion.

She told them about the Archive of Echoes, where Velen had found an old repair drawing filed among copies brought along former Skyland trade routes. The original remained in the archive. The sheet they had was a measured copy, and the missing portions had been left blank.

She showed the surviving lines.

A shared cover.

Two shallow beds beneath it.

Separate fixing points.

A narrow gap between the beds, hidden from a viewer looking only at the outer panel.

“Does the drawing prove this is our wall?” a councilor asked.

“No,” Brunti said.

The answer surprised the room enough to make several people look up.

“It is similar to the construction beneath our casing,” she continued. “The transport mark is incomplete. The lower curve resembles a mark in our own ledger, but the surviving lines are not identical. The drawing gives us a way to test what we have. It cannot give us the names of the people beneath the cover.”

Velen turned the page so the council could see the torn margin.

“Nor can we responsibly supply the missing half of the workshop seal,” they added. “We would then be comparing our own invention to the wall.”

Orven copied the distinction onto a tablet.

Darrik made no comment.

Brunti described her journey through Everwinter, where broken histories and incomplete inscriptions had not become whole merely because she wished them to.

Then she reached Keldor.

She told the council about the displaced people, the damaged communities and the work Sira had done with the others to bring survivors toward safety.

“The rescue was completed as far as we could carry it,” she said. “The people were transferred to staffed shelters. Some later outcomes are still unknown.”

“Then you went into Blackwood,” Darrik said.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Brunti rested her sound hand on the table.

“I hoped it would teach me how to distinguish one remembered voice from another. I had no evidence that it could. Velen told me so. Sira told me the journey was unnecessary. I went anyway.”

The hall remained quiet.

“Did you find a method?” a councilor asked.

“No.”

“An object?”

“No.”

“A record concerning the Skyland?”

“No.”

“Then what happened?”

Brunti took out the notebook whose pages Velen had kept from becoming a map of things she had not seen.

She told them what she remembered.

The wooden door high in the cliff.

The gaps in her memory.

The roots.

The hanging trees with mouths that sang.

The mire whose water showed faces that did not match her own.

The endless procession of hooded figures carrying lanterns without light.

The throne inside a cathedral of rootwood, where an enormous pulse had seemed to travel through the stone.

She stopped whenever she reached something she could not account for.

She did not call the keeper a god.

She did not claim the faces had belonged to the dead.

She did not say the procession had shown her future.

At the end she showed them the brace on her wrist.

“I woke under a Verdani healer's care, at Keldor's border. Two wardens found me beside an old boundary marker and carried me to a ridge post; a supply wagon brought me to the lazaret. I cannot tell you how I came from the hall I remember to the place where they found me. The injury was real. My impressions may have been real, but they have not been checked by another witness.”

Orven finished writing and looked at the last tablet.

“No verified link to the memorial,” he said.

“None.”

The older memory-keeper studied Brunti.

“Do you regret going?”

“Yes.”

“Because you were hurt?”

“That too.”

Brunti looked toward the caretaker.

“Because the people here had to keep doing the work while I chased an answer I did not need.” She looked at the caretaker again. “And because I was more afraid of coming back with an unfinished record than of making you wait for me. I should have known the difference.”

***

The council did not turn the meeting into a ceremony.

They asked about the original panel, the deadline and the conditions under which a safe inspection could begin. One of the structural workers reported that the temporary dampening pads had remained stable through several ordinary shifts of resonance. The resting rooms had an independent support line, inspected daily. No new disturbance had been measured there.

The caretaker confirmed the residents had been able to sleep without the old knocking returning. The elder whose rest Brunti had disturbed was using the same shaped bed again, though he still asked to be warned before work began near the chamber.

“Did anyone warn him this morning?” Brunti asked.

“I did,” the caretaker said. “He wanted to know the hour, not the story.”

Brunti nodded. The hour was something they could give him.

She felt a relief so plain that it was almost embarrassing.

She had spent months imagining the wall waiting for her.

It had not.

People had protected one another.

Darrik laid out the work schedule.

A new support had been prepared and checked in a separate bay. The remaining materials and safe access to the outer wall would come with the approaching convergence, just as Darrik had promised before Brunti left. Until then, the old panel would stay isolated from the resting chambers. A temporary cradle was ready to hold the two stones when their casing could finally be lifted.

“The transfer can proceed whether or not we identify the old inscriptions,” he said.

Brunti nodded.

“I agree.”

Darrik looked up.

“And their destination?”

“A protected archive,” he said. “That has always been the plan.”

The older memory-keeper spoke for the first time since Brunti's account.

“Protected from whom?”

“From weather, vibration, accidental damage.”

“From those of us who came to hear them?”

Darrik's hands stilled over the diagram.

“No.”

“Then where would we go to listen?”

He hesitated.

A locked room near the old ledger stores would have been secure. It would also have been difficult for an elder with unsteady feet to reach, and it would have required asking a keeper to open a door each time someone wished to hear the memories.

Brunti recognized the flaw because she had feared it from the beginning.

She also recognized that fear had not justified leaving the old wall where it could harm the living.

“Could the archive have a listening space on the public passage?” she asked.

Darrik looked at her.

“Not sharing an acoustic support with the resting chamber.”

“Of course not.”

“The stones can't remain pressed together under the same old cover.”

“Then they shouldn't.”

“Can you bear the thought of them sounding separately?”

Brunti looked at the drawing.

“Yes.”

The memory-keeper leaned forward.

“Can it be reached without climbing?”

Darrik studied the floor plan again.

“There is a side court beside the archive. The structure there is independent of the resting chambers. We could examine it.”

“Before we decide?” Brunti asked.

“Before we decide.”

The council agreed to inspect the place after the stones had been made safe.

No one was asked to applaud.

***

The convergence came several days later. Temporary bridges of stone and light joined the neighboring Skylands, and the remaining fittings passed into the craft stores. A service platform was secured beside the outer wall. Only after Darrik's crew checked the access and the separate supports of the resting chambers did he permit the permanent work to begin.

The following morning, Brunti arrived at the memorial court with her wrist still in its brace and a written list of tasks she could perform without bearing a heavy load. Darrik had assigned the lifting to a crew who knew the old stone's weight. Velen stood near the worktable with the Aetherion drawing and the original Skyland ledger copy.

The caretaker waited at the far end of the corridor.

She did not enter the active work zone.

Brunti was glad.

The affected wall looked smaller than she remembered.

The high court still received light through its roof. The names carved across the neighboring memorial stones caught it in small, bright lines. Only one panel was covered by the isolation frame, its dark metal edges marked with chalk lines showing where the supports had been tested.

Brunti approached without touching.

She could see where she had once seated the careless brace.

The narrow mark remained.

Her hammer hung at her side.

She left it there.

Darrik opened the inspection record.

“First, confirm the resting room is disconnected.”

A second craft worker took readings from the independent line behind the court. The caretaker reported the conditions in the neighboring chamber. The workers checked the dampening pads, the temporary cradle and the supports beneath the outer casing.

Only when their measurements agreed did Darrik authorize the cover to be opened.

Brunti watched the first fastening lift free.

No sudden revelation followed.

A screw resisted.

The worker changed tools.

Another fastening had been set at an awkward angle by an earlier repair. The crew protected the surrounding stone and eased it loose with a patient turn.

When the cover began to move, Brunti watched the narrow gap beneath it.

Darrik raised one hand.

“Wait.”

The lifting stopped.

A thin seam of dust fell from the upper edge.

“There's pressure on the lower corner,” he said. “Set the cradle higher.”

The crew adjusted the support.

Brunti could see the problem from where she stood.

A former version of herself would have reached for a lever before he finished speaking.

Now she watched until the load settled.

Darrik gave the signal.

The old cover moved away.

***

Two stones lay beneath it.

Not the suggestion of two.

Not two possible positions in a torn drawing.

Two distinct memorial bodies rested in separate shallow beds, each with its own fixing points. Between them ran a narrow channel hidden by the old outer casing.

The stones were different.

The left one was pale, with fine metallic threads passing across its surface in a pattern that had softened over years of use. The right stone was darker, its broad mineral face crossed by a few bright crystal seams. Their edges did not meet. A narrow spacer held them apart.

The old cover had concealed both.

The later support had worn unevenly and allowed part of the assembly to settle. The failing join was material and visible, not a mystical thread hidden beneath the entire world.

Brunti bent near the cradle.

“Two,” she whispered.

Velen looked at the copied drawing.

“Two,” they agreed.

Darrik did not let either of them proceed.

“The shapes agree in a general way,” he said. “Record the dimensions before we name the work.”

They measured the widths.

The distance between the fixing holes.

The position of the spacer.

The depth of each separate bed.

Another worker made independent measurements without looking at Brunti's notes.

When they compared the results, the differences were small enough to fall within the tools' ordinary tolerances. One fixing hole stood slightly lower than the Aetherion drawing suggested. Darrik marked the discrepancy.

“Does that disprove the drawing?” Brunti asked.

“It proves the drawing is not a perfect image of this wall.”

Velen smiled.

“Something I have been saying for some time.”

“You may enjoy being right,” Brunti told them, “but you needn't enjoy it so visibly.”

They did not promise to stop.

***

The first inscription appeared beneath a layer of old dust near the upper edge of the left stone.

The characters had been cut shallowly and later hidden by the shared cover. The surface was protected from touch, but it had not been untouched by time. Several letters were faint; one had been damaged where an old fastening crossed the edge.

The crew cleared the dust with soft brushes.

Brunti did not touch the carving.

A broad line emerged, then a curved stroke beside it. As more of the surface became legible, a name appeared.

**Tavra.**

The shallow strokes near the upper fastening resembled marks Brunti had noticed before her journey. Here they ended beside the pale memorial, where no cover concealed them. It was a trace of workmanship, not yet a story about the person named by the stone.

The memory-keeper, watching from behind the work boundary, made a small sound.

Brunti looked toward her.

“Do you know the name?”

“From the older sky-travel accounts,” the elder said. “A Skykeeper. Not a queen. Not a warrior who conquered anything. She carried the words of other people between settlements.”

Velen found the name in a small index of copied Skyland records, where a Tavra had been associated with several former routes. A surviving entry concerned the correction of a family's name in a carried account; the earlier spelling remained on the page with the correction beside it. Velen read the two forms aloud before showing the inscription to the memory-keeper. The entry alone could not prove the stone belonged to that person, but its period and craft marks were consistent with the exposed inscription.

Darrik read the name aloud for the independent witness.

The witness copied it.

Then the crew examined the right stone.

Its upper edge carried only a few indistinct marks.

Brunti's hope fell.

She had spent the entire journey fearing that the second name had disappeared forever, that there would always be one memorial with a place in the record and another known only by its sound.

Darrik studied the support beneath the darker stone.

“Look at the lower lip,” he said.

A line of carving lay partly obscured by the old mounting strip.

The strip was no longer carrying weight. Once its fastening had been documented and a temporary support fitted beneath the separate stone, the crew lifted it clear.

More letters came into view.

Not a whole clean sentence.

Enough of a name to read without inventing the missing strokes.

**Korrum.**

The lower inscription bore a different tool hand from Tavra's. It was heavier, its cuts suited to darker mineral and the kind of durable lettering used in workshops rather than formal memorial courts. The broad squared marks beside the old lower fastening followed the same practical style, though the original fitter had left no name.

Brunti leaned closer.

Her sound hand trembled.

She wanted to read the name before anyone else could.

She stopped herself.

“Does the second witness see it?” she asked.

The other worker examined the stone from a different angle.

“Korrum,” they said.

Darrik asked for a rubbing and a measured copy of the exposed letters before the strip was moved elsewhere.

Velen recorded which strokes were intact and which were inferred only from the surviving parts of the name. Another archive record associated a Korrum with the maintenance of inhabited chambers in an earlier period of the Skyland's history. One entry listed a landing widened so residents could turn without leaning against a narrow rail; its measurements were ordinary, and the name beside them had nearly been worn away. The name and the old craft marks strengthened one another; neither record had required a fabricated conclusion.

The inscription belonged to a separate stone.

The stone occupied a separate bed.

Brunti looked between them.

At last, the names were no longer part of a theory she carried alone.

***

The resonance test was quiet.

Darrik removed the worn outer support completely and asked the crew to set the stones in temporary independent cradles. Each cradle bore its own weight. Neither touched the support that led toward the inhabited resting chamber.

He struck the left stone once with a small testing implement.

A soft, short note moved through the pale mineral.

Brunti felt its contour in the fine crystal beneath her sternum.

Then Darrik tested the right stone.

A lower resonance followed, slower to rise and slower to fade.

The two did not answer one another like singers in conversation. They carried traces of lives already lived, held in mineral and craft.

For years the shared casing had pressed their tones together. Later settling had made the overlap troublesome. Brunti's brace had carried the resulting discord into a room where it could do harm.

No god had placed the notes there to teach her a lesson.

No abyss had preserved the missing name.

A hand had repaired an old wall.

Time had worn a support.

Other hands could repair it again.

Darrik set the testing implement down.

“Now we know what was built,” he said.

Brunti looked at the two stones.

“And what we broke.”

“Not all of it,” he replied. “The records survived.”

Her wrist throbbed against the brace.

She thought of all the places where she had found a likeness and wanted it to be enough.

Here, at last, enough was made of things another person could measure.

***

The new wall was installed before the old stones left the court.

Darrik insisted on that order. The living chamber's safe support came first. Its load was carried by a line tested independently of the memorial's new setting, with fresh fittings designed for the weight they would actually bear.

Brunti assisted where her wrist allowed.

She checked measured distances, compared parts against the prepared diagram and recorded the adjustments made when an old stone edge failed to meet the new work exactly.

Sometimes she had to stand aside.

A heavy plate required more hands than hers.

An anchor needed to be held steady while a worker below it tightened a fastening she could not reach without placing weight through her injured joint.

She had known how to perform both tasks before the journey.

Knowing did not entitle her to do them now.

Darrik noticed when she moved away from the lifting frame.

“Does it trouble you?” he asked.

“Of course.”

“Good.”

Brunti gave him a dark look.

He almost smiled.

“It means you haven't mistaken healing for a thing that can be forced by pride.”

She looked at his old mineral insets, the places where his own body had needed careful repair.

“You've had practice.”

“Enough to dislike it properly.”

When the new wall was secured, the caretaker came to inspect the adjoining resting chamber.

She placed a hand against the nearest support.

No unwanted pulse traveled through it.

An older resident rested on the shaped stone beyond, his eyes closed and the mineral seams of his body settled into a low, even hum. The caretaker watched for a while before giving her report.

“The chamber is quiet.”

Darrik did not accept the statement as a final test.

They repeated their measurements through another shift of the Skyland's ordinary movement and checked the resting surface under use. The results remained stable.

Only then did the council authorize the old panel to be transferred.

***

The archive's side court was smaller than the memorial passage, and the light entered it differently.

A narrow opening above the outer wall admitted morning air. Two sheltered benches stood beneath a low roof of worked stone. The space had once been used for copying records, but its tables had been moved years earlier, leaving a broad path that a person with unsteady feet could cross without climbing.

Darrik and the memory-keeper inspected the room together.

The elder tried the approach with one hand resting lightly against the wall.

“No steps,” she said.

“None,” Darrik replied.

“And the stones?”

“In independent mounts,” he said. “The supports beneath them will not be part of the resonance line serving the resting rooms.”

“Will I hear them from the bench?”

“That is what we must test.”

Brunti had expected Darrik to prefer the old locked storehouse. He would have kept the stones safe there, in darkness and quiet, away from accidental touch.

Instead, he stood measuring a public alcove.

She approached him after the elder had gone.

“Why this place?”

“The memory-keeper asked where she would sit. The caretaker asked how someone could reach the court without crossing the room where people rested. The locked storehouse answered neither question, so I had the old copying tables moved.”

“You listened.”

“I have been known to do that.”

Brunti looked at him.

“You were right about moving the panel.”

“Yes.”

“And I was right that people needed a place to hear it.”

“You were right that simply storing a memorial does not finish the work of caring for it.”

She lowered her gaze.

“That isn't the same as being right to leave it in the resting wall.”

“No,” he said. “It isn't.”

The distinction remained between them.

Neither tried to close it with praise.

***

The transfer took most of the afternoon.

The crew carried the two memorial stones in separate padded frames, each secured against impact and kept in its recorded orientation. Brunti walked beside the lighter frame, reading the measurements aloud while another worker checked that the support remained level.

She did not lift.

She did not need to.

At the new alcove, both stones were seated in independent mounts with a visible space between them. The gap was narrow enough for a listener to stand near both, wide enough to prevent the old casing from forcing them into one shared support.

Darrik adjusted the dampening beneath the left mount.

Brunti checked the right.

The little sounds of their tools filled the court.

Then the workers stepped away.

The memory-keeper sat on the nearest bench.

“Can I hear them?” she asked.

Darrik nodded.

Brunti moved to the wall and placed one sound fingertip against the stone beside the two mounts.

The left memory gave its short, bright resonance.

The right answered in its slower, lower contour.

There was a pause between them.

A real pause.

Brunti could hear both without their sounds being pressed into a false single note. The old unstable knocking did not return. Nothing traveled along the independent supports toward the resting chamber.

The elder listened for a long time.

“I knew Tavra's name,” she said softly. “Korrum's had been lost from the part we could see.”

“Korrum's inscription remained underneath,” Velen replied.

“And now?”

“Both are recorded.”

The elder looked at the stones.

“Will people be allowed to sit here after the workers leave?”

Darrik stepped forward.

“That is why we made the benches.”

The old Grimnir nodded.

She did not ask anyone to explain what the memory stones said. The echoes were fixed, older than the people listening, and could not answer questions about what had happened after their makers died.

Still, two names had come back into the light.

That was enough for the afternoon.

***

The Skyland held a modest gathering when the work was accepted.

It was not a great victory feast. The city's people had other tasks, and the larger work of the convergence continued beyond the archive court. But the workers brought warm food, the memory-keeper invited those who had tended the old passage, and the caretaker from the resting chamber came for a short while after her shift.

Brunti sat with Velen near the side wall.

Her wrist was aching, and she had learned to regard that as a reason to remain still.

Nemi approached with a plate.

“You didn't lift anything,” she said.

“Not much.”

“Darrik said you would try.”

“He was wrong.”

Nemi studied her.

“Are you sure?”

“Not entirely.”

She held out her sound hand.

Nemi gave her the plate.

The food was simple, prepared with mineral grains and warm roots. Brunti ate while listening to ordinary conversation.

Some residents spoke about Tavra's journeys. Others remembered stories of Korrum's work, particularly a bridge repair that had been repeated so often no one could now agree on how high the original bridge had stood.

Velen listened until the elder who told the bridge story had finished, then asked whether she wished her name recorded beside the telling. The elder agreed, but requested that the height of the bridge remain uncertain. Velen wrote that down before accepting the next account.

Brunti smiled into her bowl.

Across the court, Darrik was talking with the caretaker about the resting chamber's next inspection.

No one asked him whether he had learned a lesson.

He had simply kept the chamber safe.

Brunti looked at the two memorial stones in their separate mounts.

The name markers were not yet finished. Temporary slips identified the stones for the workers and the few listeners who came that evening. Permanent markers would be cut after the rubbings and inscriptions had been checked against the final record.

Brunti could have begun shaping them at once.

She wanted to.

Instead, she finished the meal and rested her injured hand.

***

Near the end of the gathering, one of the workers came to Darrik with a problem.

A narrow rail along the archive's outer passage had loosened where the new traffic made it necessary for people to lean against it. The fastening was old, and the new stonework beside it had been fitted too closely for the original plate to be lifted cleanly.

Brunti heard the explanation.

Her tools were in the workshop.

She knew how to repair the rail.

The old desire rose: to be the first to offer her skill, to make a small fault vanish while the others still spoke of the larger repair.

She stood.

Then she walked to the memory-keeper, who was preparing to leave.

“Will you need that rail tomorrow?” Brunti asked.

The elder looked toward the passage.

“Yes. It helps me keep my balance on the turn.”

“Then we'll make sure it holds.”

The elder nodded.

“Thank you.”

Brunti went to Darrik.

“Can we arrange a safe support there before the morning shift?”

“We can,” he said. “The worker has already set a temporary brace.”

“May I inspect the fastening with someone whose wrists work?”

Darrik looked at her injured arm.

“That's a reasonable request.”

“I've been practicing.”

“So I have noticed.”

He asked one of the younger workers to meet Brunti at the passage.

They examined the loose rail together. The plate beneath it had shifted where the new stone joined the old, leaving a gap that would take a different fastening than the one originally fitted.

The worker tested the support while Brunti measured the opening with her sound hand.

They wrote down the dimensions.

The repair would be done in the morning, when the right material could be brought from the workshop.

Brunti could see exactly how it should be fixed.

The knowledge was satisfying.

It did not make the work urgent enough to do badly in the dark.

***

When the last visitors had gone, Brunti returned briefly to the listening court.

The two stones stood beneath their separate sheltered mounts. A faint light from the corridor reached the pale one, catching a thread of metal near its upper edge. The darker stone held a few points of crystal that answered the same light more slowly.

The temporary name slips remained in place.

Tavra.

Korrum.

The names had been found beneath actual old fittings, copied by witnesses whose hands had held the tools, and recorded against a sketch that was still incomplete.

There were things the sketch could never tell them.

There were things Blackwood might have shown her that she would never be able to prove.

The work at home had required neither.

Brunti stood without touching the stones.

From the adjoining passage came the steady sound of someone walking toward the resting room. The footfalls faded through the independently supported wall. No wrong vibration followed them.

The Skyland moved on.

Brunti took the rolled measurements for the rail and placed them in a drawer marked for the morning crew. She left a note naming the worker who had tested the temporary brace and the elder who depended upon it.

Then she closed the drawer.

There was still work to do.

For the first time, she was content to leave the right part of it for another day.
