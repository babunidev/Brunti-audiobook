# Chapter Twenty-Eight — A Story About the End

The first road Brunti traveled after Blackwood was scarcely wide enough for a wagon.

It followed the edge of Keldor through trees that still carried the forest's damp mist but no longer seemed to lean across the path when she looked away. The trunks stood in ordinary soil. A pair of birds argued in a thorn bush beside the ditch. Water ran beneath a little stone bridge whose missing corner someone had repaired with a block of different color.

Brunti noticed that block immediately.

It had been fitted neatly, though the join was not quite flush with the older stone. The builder had left a small space for water to escape rather than sealing the corner entirely.

She could have told the builder how to smooth the face.

She had no reason to.

The bridge held the wagon.

The driver took them across at a careful pace while Sira walked beside the front wheel and Velen kept the travel records dry beneath the cover. Brunti sat on a padded bench with her left wrist braced against her chest.

The jolting of the wheels traveled through her mineral body. The healer had warned her that a long road could strain fractures even without a blow. They had therefore divided the journey into short stages, each ending at a staffed shelter where she could rest.

There had been no argument about that division.

Brunti had expected to resent the slowness.

Instead, she found herself measuring the distance between stops with an unfamiliar gratitude. There would be water at the next station. Someone had already sent word of the supplies her body required. The people guiding her were not asking her to prove she could manage alone.

For now, that was enough.

***

At the first turning, the trees opened toward a strip of pale grass.

Keldor remained visible behind them as a dark seam beneath the mountains. Mist lay along its canopy, concealing the deep places where Brunti had walked without being able to remember all the paths between them.

She watched the forest until the wagon turned again.

Sira had climbed onto the bench opposite her. She carried a bundle of messages gathered from the shelters where recovered travelers had been received. Some belonged to families who wanted news of their own people. Others were requests for supplies, names and tools that should be sent before the next rains made the lower tracks difficult.

Brunti knew what each packet represented.

People waiting for answers that could be checked.

Sira noticed her looking.

“The child with the long stick was taken onward from the western station,” she said.

“Did she reach her family?”

“Not in the last report I received. They're working on it.”

Brunti nodded.

She wanted the story to end with a door opening and a pair of arms waiting inside.

The report did not contain that ending.

She did not ask Sira to supply it.

“The boy?” she asked.

“Still under care at Elda's shelter when I left. He was helping stack the cups, which Elda claims is a dangerous assignment.”

“Because of him?”

“Because of the cups. They're old.”

Brunti almost smiled.

Sira went back to arranging the messages in her lap.

A little later she said, “You could have told me before you left for the cliff.”

“I did tell you.”

“You told me you were going.”

Brunti looked at her.

“That isn't the same thing,” Sira said.

“No.”

“I wanted you to tell me what you were afraid would happen if you didn't go.”

Brunti watched the road pass beneath the wagon.

“I thought I'd be going home empty-handed.”

“And?”

“I thought everyone would see it.”

Sira waited.

Brunti looked down at the brace.

“I can see it now.”

Sira held the messages against a sudden gust.

“Then you can be seen coming home.”

They sat with that for a while.

Neither called it forgiveness.

***

By evening the wagon reached a roadside house with a sloping roof and a yard full of weathered carts.

The proprietor had cleared a quiet room for Brunti and set a broad resting stone near the hearth. It was not a proper Grimnir resonance bed, but the surface was strong, and a shallow mineral bowl had been fitted beneath it to carry warmth into the stone.

Brunti tested the support with her sound hand.

It was better than the bed she had imagined she would find.

The proprietor looked embarrassed when she said so.

“An old traveler taught me how to set it,” he explained. “We don't have many Grimnir staying in this part of the road.”

“You set the bowl where the weight spreads,” Brunti said. “That's the difficult part.”

He examined the stone as though the compliment had revealed a defect.

“Is it safe?”

“For me to rest on? Yes.”

“Then I'd rather not change it.”

“Good.”

Velen, standing behind Brunti, made a small sound of pleasure.

“What?” she asked.

“Nothing. A lesson in restraint, freely given.”

Brunti narrowed her eyes.

“You may put that in the book.”

“I already have.”

***

The house sheltered more travelers than its small dining room could hold.

Some ate beneath the eaves. Others sat around a low stove built into a stone trough near the yard. The air smelled of roasted roots, wet leather and the faint metallic tang of rain approaching from the hills.

Brunti came outside after resting.

She carried a cup of mineral broth prepared for her by the proprietor. It was too hot at first, so she held it between her sound hand and the arm of the bench, allowing the warmth to settle through the fine channels at her fingers.

Sira sat farther along the table with a small group of people discussing the missing and recovered travelers from Keldor. She was doing the work she had intended to do when Brunti first met her.

Velen had found another archivist by some mysterious instinct common to people who carried books through rain. The two were comparing the names of waystations with several maps spread beneath a lantern.

Brunti chose a place at the edge of the stove.

An elderly traveler sat there already.

His cloak was plain, repaired at the elbows with fabric of a different color. A narrow wooden case rested against his boot. He kept one hand upon it even while eating.

“Mind if I sit?” Brunti asked.

“Not if you intend to remain on your side of the bench.”

“I'm not planning to cross it.”

“Then we've settled the first question.”

She sat.

The traveler looked at the brace on her wrist, then away.

He did not ask how she had been hurt.

Brunti warmed her cup.

“Are you going north?” she asked.

“Far enough to take the high road, if the weather holds.”

“So are we.”

“Then perhaps we'll share more bridges.”

He took a spoonful of food.

For some time they listened to the people around the stove.

The traveler was called Tarnel. He had been carrying a small casket of household keepsakes toward the family of a woman who had died on a long journey. She had been his sister.

He mentioned her only when someone came to ask whether a late wagon had brought another parcel from the lower stations.

“Not yet,” the proprietor told him. “I'll keep a place for it.”

Tarnel thanked him and returned to the bench.

The wooden case remained beneath his hand.

Brunti recognized the careful way he held it.

Not because it contained something precious in the usual sense.

Because its contents belonged to someone who was no longer there to decide where they should be kept.

***

The next morning rain held them at the house.

A slow wash of water crossed the yard, gathering at the edges of the stone trough and pouring into a channel beyond the gate. The roof creaked in the damp. Somewhere in the stable a horse stamped a hoof and complained at its keeper.

Velen joined Brunti beside the stove with a dry notebook and a question.

“Would you be willing to read what I wrote after the lazaret?”

Brunti looked at the pages.

“Do you want me to correct it?”

“I want you to tell me if I've made your uncertainty sound more certain than you meant.”

“That seems unlikely.”

Velen raised an eyebrow.

“It's an archivist's favorite mistake.”

She read.

The account began with the wooden door high in the cliff. It listed the places Brunti remembered after that: the tangled roots, the suspended trees, the black water, the procession, the enormous hall. Beside each observation Velen had kept a narrow column for things she could not establish.

The space under the first door was blank.

So was the space after the Blackwood Guardian.

So was the route out of the throne chamber.

Brunti reached the final line.

*Recovered under circumstances not independently documented.*

She handed the notebook back.

“It's accurate.”

“Are you sure?”

“It doesn't say what I want it to say.”

“What would that be?”

“That I found a place where the dead remember us.”

Velen closed the book.

They did not try to convince her she had not found it.

They did not pretend the longing had no weight.

After a moment Velen said, “That would be a different kind of record.”

***

Tarnel came inside as the rain strengthened.

He shook water from the hem of his cloak, set the wooden case beneath the table, and asked for warm tea. Sira returned from the covered yard, carrying a packet of names she had just received.

All four found themselves at the same table.

Tarnel noticed Velen's open notebook.

“Are you writing a history?”

“Trying to,” Velen said.

“Of the forest?”

“Of one traveler who went too far into it.”

Brunti gave them a sharp glance.

Velen did not look apologetic.

“That's accurate too,” they said.

Tarnel studied her without staring.

“My sister used to tell stories about that forest. Not because she had been inside it. She liked a place that could keep a mystery.”

“What did she think happened to people who vanished there?” Brunti asked.

“She thought people went missing.”

“That sounds practical.”

“She was. More than I am.”

He turned the empty cup in his hands.

“Toward the end, when she was ill, she began telling me stories about Atranor. She said it was a place where nothing a soul had done could be lost from sight.”

Velen's expression changed.

“Atranor?”

Tarnel nodded.

“That's what she called it.”

Sira set down her messages.

“Did she say she'd seen it?”

“No. She was very clear on that. Her grandmother had told her, and someone had told the grandmother before her.”

“Then it's a story with a long road behind it,” Sira said.

Tarnel smiled faintly.

“Yes. Perhaps longer than the one we're walking.”

***

Tarnel's story began with a plain of black stone.

Not black earth or a field darkened by fire, but a polished surface without visible edge, smooth as obsidian under thin rainlight. It reflected a silver sky though no sun or moon shone above it.

The soul, so the story said, arrived without a road.

The plain was simply there, and the soul was there upon it, carrying everything that had made a life.

Brunti listened.

She did not picture herself standing on the stone.

Tarnel had told them from the beginning that he was repeating a story, not opening a window.

“In my sister's telling,” he said, “there was no wind. Yet clothes and hair moved as though the whole realm breathed very slowly.”

He lifted a hand to show the movement.

“Overhead the constellations drifted. They didn't keep their ordinary places. Some were gold-white, some red, some blue. Rivers of light passed between them, long shining currents across a sky without a shore.”

The rain tapped steadily on the roof.

Tarnel paused to warm his hands around his cup.

“In the distance stood pillars larger than mountains. Above them floated pieces of impossible country. A forest hung upside down with its roots open to the heavens. Waterfalls rose instead of falling. Islands drifted wrapped in fire and loose stone. And among all that were dragons.”

Velen leaned forward.

“The divine forms?”

“That's how the story names them. Not flying, not descending in judgment. Resting.”

Tarnel looked toward the rain.

“One lay along a ruined stairway. Another seemed almost part of a temple built so long ago that no one remembered its makers. Others watched from cliffs, their scales carrying different colors, like living fire, crystal, shadow and starlight.”

Brunti felt the old temptation to connect the image to the huge root beneath Blackwood.

There too she had seen a structure like a body and a presence that seemed to watch.

She stopped the thought before it became a statement.

Two stories could share an image without sharing an origin.

Tarnel went on.

“The soul walked, though there was nowhere it needed to reach. Then the stone itself remembered a path. The path led toward a round platform, and above it sat the great assembly.”

“The Pantheon,” Velen said.

“That's what my sister called them.”

“Did she name the judges?”

“She did. Aerisara, Sylvanus, and the god whose final gift had kept that soul alive.”

Brunti knew that teaching.

The last sustaining fragment of divine essence returned to its deity when a mortal life ended. The soul passed on as itself, carrying memory and choice. Which deity had given the last fragment differed from person to person.

Tarnel told the next part slowly.

“Every kindness, every injury, every moment of cowardice and courage was there. Nothing could be hidden. The judges didn't need to accuse the soul. Its own life stood before it.”

Velen looked down at their notebook.

“That part is often described as the realm revealing memory,” they said.

Tarnel shrugged.

“Perhaps. The way my sister said it, the plain didn't ask a question. It showed the answer to questions a person might never have known how to ask.”

Brunti watched his hand upon the cup.

She wondered whether he was thinking of his sister's last hours.

He did not say.

***

The story ended at the circular platform.

Tarnel paused there.

“And what happened next?” Sira asked.

“That depends which telling you hear.”

Velen's pencil was already poised.

Tarnel saw it and laughed softly.

“You should have met my sister. She would have been delighted to argue with an archivist.”

“What did she believe?”

“That the gods might offer a soul the chance to return to the same life. Not because it had won a contest, or because its mistakes had been small enough, but because at least one god chose to give a living fragment again.”

“That is resurrection,” Velen said. “A return to the life already lived, memories intact.”

“Yes.”

“Not reincarnation.”

“No. She corrected me on that distinction more than once.”

Tarnel turned the wooden case beneath the table until its latch faced him.

“In the old teaching, if a soul isn't returned to its former life, it enters the inner realm of the god whose final essence sustained it. It remains itself there, though it joins that god's element. The soul can stay as long as it wishes.”

“And leave?” Brunti asked.

“Choose another life. The gods do not keep it there against its will.”

Tarnel spoke the next words with unusual firmness.

“Every soul has that right.”

Brunti knew the law too. The divine teaching did not make a new life a reward for a pleasing judgment. Reincarnation belonged to the soul's own choice. A soul could choose a new form, even among a people different from the one it had known, and several gods would give the essence to sustain it.

But the new life would not arrive with the old life laid out like an open book. Conscious memory of former lives and of the divine realm would be gone.

Brunti looked at the little case.

“Your sister believed she'd remember everything when she arrived?”

Tarnel shook his head.

“She believed a soul brought its life with it to Atranor. What it remembers in the gods' inner realms, and what any of us might know of those places, was another matter. She did not pretend to have returned with a report.”

He was quiet for a moment.

“She also knew she might choose another life someday, and that it would not be the same as coming back to our kitchen.”

The rain softened.

Brunti could hear drops falling from the eaves one by one.

“Did that comfort her?” she asked.

“Sometimes.”

“And you?”

Tarnel looked out at the road.

“Sometimes.”

***

Later the proprietor came to clear the cups.

Tarnel helped gather them, placing the empty ones together so they would not strike one another on the tray. His movements were practiced. Brunti suspected he had kept a kitchen of his own for many years.

When the proprietor had gone, he opened the wooden case.

Inside lay a handful of small objects.

A length of blue thread.

A worn thimble.

A brass clasp polished bright at the center.

A flat stone with an uneven rim.

Nothing looked like an artifact that could persuade a god.

Tarnel lifted the thimble.

“My sister used to wear this when she mended a coat. She had a habit of turning it so the dent faced her palm. Said it caught the needle better.”

Brunti examined the dent.

It had been made by repeated pressure, perhaps by someone pushing too hard with a tool.

“Did it?”

“I don't know. I never learned to sew properly.”

“Then perhaps you should have asked.”

“I did. She told me I was pressing the needle at the wrong angle.”

Brunti smiled.

Tarnel put the thimble back.

“That's how I remember her most clearly. Not from the story of Atranor. From all the little things she corrected.”

He closed the lid.

“My nephew asked whether there would be a sign if she chose to return in another life. Some mark that would tell us it was her.”

“What did you say?” Sira asked.

“I said the teachings allow affinities, instincts, unexplained feelings. But none of them would give us the right to point at a child and call that child our dead.”

Velen set the pencil down.

The answer had the same care they had shown at the ruined shrine in Everwinter, where the Shattered Seer's fragments offered gestures but no complete sentence.

Brunti remembered her own memorial wall.

Tavra and Korrum were dead. Their memory stones preserved echoes, not souls that could be asked to prove what happened after death.

The distinction mattered.

She had once treated an old cadence as a voice trying to tell her something new. She had fitted a brace to make the two rhythms agree and nearly made the resting chamber unsafe.

Tarnel had kept a thimble because he knew the shape of his sister's hand around it.

The thimble did not have to contain her.

***

When the rain stopped, the travelers left the house in separate groups.

Tarnel joined the same northbound caravan as Brunti, Sira and Velen. He rode on the wagon ahead, his case secured in a canvas bundle beneath the seat.

The road gradually left the edge of Keldor.

Dark trees gave way to open grass and small farms. Fields followed the contours of the land. At one crossing the path split around a stone outcrop whose face was carved with an old distance marker.

Brunti studied it as they passed.

The grooves were worn, but their meaning remained clear enough for the driver to choose the correct route.

No hidden name.

No moving runes.

A direction, carved by someone who wished the next traveler to arrive.

She liked it.

The caravan stopped at a mill beside a shallow river.

Brunti watched the wheel turn. The blades were patched with mismatched wood. Each revolution made a small creak at the same point, and a narrow strip of water spilled from a place where the boards no longer met perfectly.

She began to say something.

Then she stopped.

The miller was standing beside the wheel, listening to it himself.

He knew where it creaked.

He had set a bucket beneath the spill.

Brunti could ask if he wanted help, but she could not simply decide that the mill needed her repair.

“What?” Velen asked.

“Nothing.”

“You were looking at the wheel.”

“It's a wheel.”

“Remarkable observation.”

“Someone has been keeping it turning.”

Velen looked again.

The patches were visible from where they stood.

“So they have.”

Brunti let the wheel continue its work.

***

At the river station a message reached Velen.

It had traveled by several routes: from a highland courier to a staffed road post, from that post to the river crossing, then on with the ordinary packets carried by the caravan. The folded paper bore several marks of receipt, each smaller than the last.

Velen inspected the seal.

“It's from the Skyland.”

Brunti stopped eating.

Sira looked up.

Velen handed her the paper.

The writing belonged to Darrik.

Brunti knew it at once. His letters were narrow and firm, with a habit of leaving more space around the important words than most writers would think necessary.

She unfolded the page carefully with her sound hand.

He had received the copy of the Aetherion restoration sketch and the message about the two possible memorial seats. The original damaged panel remained isolated from the inhabited resting chambers, where temporary supports had been inspected and found safe for continued use.

The next convergence work was approaching.

The craft team was preparing a stable replacement for the affected wall. The old memorial casing would not be discarded. It would be moved intact to a protected space where its stones and original marks could be examined without placing the resting chamber at risk.

Darrik had not waited for Brunti's return to make the rooms safe.

He had done the work he could justify.

The message did not forbid further investigation. It did not promise that the old inscriptions would identify anyone.

Near the bottom, Darrik had written:

*Bring back the observations you can stand beside. Come back safely enough to help us test them.*

Brunti read that sentence twice.

She had expected a summons.

What arrived was an invitation to accountable work.

***

She showed the letter to Velen.

They read it in silence.

“He received the earlier record,” they said.

“Yes.”

“So the sketch wasn't lost.”

“No.”

“And the panel is still there.”

“Yes.”

Sira leaned forward.

“What about the people sleeping near it?”

“Darrik says the affected section remains isolated. The replacement is being prepared.”

Sira gave a small nod.

“That was the right thing to do.”

Brunti folded the paper.

“I know.”

She looked up when Sira did not answer.

“I mean it,” she added. “Not because I've come back with something grander to offer.”

Velen sat opposite her, hands resting on the notebook.

“The restoration sketch still shows two positions,” they said. “We can compare those positions with the old toolmarks when we reach home.”

“And if the marks don't agree?” Brunti asked.

“Then we report that.”

“And if they do?”

“Then we report that too.”

Brunti let herself smile.

“You've become very difficult to argue with.”

“An unexpected benefit of traveling with you.”

She placed the letter beside her cup.

They still had a journey to complete before anyone could test the wall.

The answer was not hiding in the next village.

It was waiting in a structure they had deliberately kept safe.

***

That evening Tarnel found Brunti beneath the shelter eaves.

The caravan had stopped early to avoid a road damaged by recent rain. A team of workers was filling a washout with compacted stone while the drivers prepared a temporary crossing farther uphill.

Brunti watched the repair.

Her left wrist rested in its brace. A few days earlier she would have stepped in without being asked, offered advice over someone's shoulder and lifted the largest stones to prove she could carry them.

Now she sat where she could see the work clearly.

Tarnel sat beside her.

“Your companions say you have news from home.”

“We have instructions. Work to do.”

“That sounds like home.”

He rested the wooden case between his knees.

“My sister lived farther north,” he said. “I may leave the caravan at the next crossing.”

“Will your family be there?”

“Some of them.”

“Do they know what you're bringing?”

“They know the case is hers. I haven't told them which things are inside.”

Brunti looked at the latch.

“Will you tell them her story about Atranor?”

“If they ask.”

“And if they don't?”

“Then we might talk about her thimble.”

They watched the workers lay another course of stone into the washed-out road.

One piece did not fit.

A laborer lifted it out, turned it and set it down again. It still rocked.

The foreman examined the base and shook his head.

The piece was set aside.

Another was chosen.

The wagon crossing would hold because someone was willing to stop making the wrong stone fit.

Tarnel followed Brunti's gaze.

“Do you think the gods know all that will happen to us?” he asked.

It was the kind of question a traveler might ask beside a dark road, when the work of returning could be felt in the body and the next day remained uncertain.

Brunti remembered the divine laws Velen had copied in Aetherion.

“No,” she said. “The future isn't written for them to see either.”

“Then my sister might make a choice I could never have expected.”

“Yes.”

“Even after death?”

“If the teachings are true, especially then.”

Tarnel was quiet.

“Good,” he said.

He did not explain what pleased him in the thought.

Brunti did not ask him to.

***

They left the river country by stages.

Some mornings the road carried them beneath trees where birds sang from the hedges. On others it ran across bare hills, the wind catching at cloaks and wagon covers. The nights grew colder as the route climbed. At each station Brunti rested on stone warmed through by careful mineral preparations.

The healer's instructions remained in her pack.

She read them when the wrist ached, even when she already knew what they said.

Sira traveled with them as far as a broad crossing where relief caravans turned toward settlements still waiting for messages from the border. The road there divided around a small shrine and a well.

Brunti found Sira beside the wagon, checking the names on a packet before passing it to the courier.

“You're leaving from here,” Brunti said.

“For a while.”

“I thought you might.”

“There are people expecting these messages. I can reach them before the next train of wagons.”

Brunti stood close enough to see the ink on the folded papers.

“Will you be safe?”

Sira gave her a look.

“I'll use the staffed routes. I'll tell people where I'm going. I'll stop when I'm tired.”

“That sounds familiar.”

“Does it?”

“More than you might think.”

Sira tied the bundle.

Brunti wanted to ask whether her friend would come to the Skyland for the convergence. She wanted to ask whether she would still be angry when they met again.

Instead she said, “Thank you for coming to the lazaret.”

Sira's hands stilled.

“You needed someone.”

“I did.”

“And I was already angry.”

“I know.”

“You needn't keep agreeing with everything.”

“I'm not.”

Sira laughed once, despite herself.

Then she looked at the brace.

“Don't lift anything heavy.”

“I won't.”

“That's a promise I intend to check.”

Brunti moved her sound hand toward Sira.

The Verdani took it.

Their grip was brief, warm in a way Brunti had learned to recognize through the differing nature of their bodies.

“Good road,” Sira said.

“You too.”

They parted at the well.

Brunti watched the relief wagon turn east until it disappeared behind the low hills.

There was an ache in the leaving.

It was not evidence that either of them had chosen wrongly.

***

Velen continued with Brunti.

They had been given space in a smaller caravan carrying grain, tools and copies of official notices to the upland landing stations. The roads there connected with the routes by which travelers reached the Grimnir Skylands when those great engineered cities passed near their known moorings.

Brunti had known those routes since childhood.

They had changed while she was gone.

A bridge had been rebuilt.

A station had moved its water trough.

A roadside marker had been replaced with one bearing cleaner lettering, though the names beneath it were the same.

She found she enjoyed these changes.

The world had continued its ordinary work without waiting for her to understand it.

At a high station the caravan stopped beneath an open sky.

Far above the hills, a pale band of cloud moved across the afternoon. Behind it, barely visible at first, lay a long structure of stone and metal suspended in the air.

A Skyland.

It was not her own.

The lower anchor crystals caught sunlight as it passed along its route, and faint bridges of light stretched between the floating city and the docking structures below.

Brunti watched it until the cloud covered it again.

Velen came to stand beside her.

“Does it feel like home?”

“Not that one.”

“Because the stones are different?”

“Because the people are.”

Velen nodded.

Brunti considered the answer.

“Also the stones are different.”

“That was more like you.”

They stood together until the light changed.

***

The last night before their planned passage toward Brunti's Skyland, Velen found her sorting the travel papers by lamplight.

She had arranged them in three groups.

One held the Aetherion restoration sketch and copies of surviving inscriptions.

Another held messages from Darrik about the old wall, its temporary isolation and the approaching convergence.

The third held her Blackwood notes.

That last group was thick with blank spaces.

Velen examined the arrangement.

“Do you want me to prepare the council copy?”

“Not yet.”

“Why not?”

“Because I need to decide what I can say without borrowing certainty from the pages beside it.”

They sat down.

Brunti opened the Blackwood notebook.

She read the first line about the wooden door.

Then the lines about the roots.

She described the distant singing, the reflections, the procession, the throne.

At several points she stopped.

Velen did not supply missing words.

When Brunti reached the end, she laid the page beside Darrik's letter.

“What can the council use?” she asked.

“From Blackwood?”

“Yes.”

Velen considered it.

“They can use the fact that you entered a dangerous region after the search was over. They can use the injuries you returned with. They can use your admission that you cannot verify the places or the cause of what you perceived.”

“And the throne?”

“They can listen to your account. That doesn't make it evidence about the memorial wall.”

Brunti nodded.

“What about the sketch?”

“That is different. We know where it was found. We know who copied it. We know which lines remain visible. We can compare it to the original.”

She put the sketch beside the letter.

“And my brace?”

“That is different too.”

“How?”

“We know you fitted it. We know the resting chamber became unsafe. We know it was removed.”

Brunti closed her eyes for a moment.

The list was plain.

Nothing in it excused her.

Nothing in it required her to be more monstrous than she had been.

The work of repair could begin from facts that people could test.

She opened her eyes.

“Then that's what I'll say.”

***

Before dawn a wind passed over the high station.

Brunti was awake when the lantern outside the shelter guttered and steadied. The door to the yard stood open. Beyond it, workers were preparing the boarding platform and checking the ropes that secured the lift to the upper landing.

She went outside with her wrist protected beneath her cloak.

A young attendant was having trouble with a crate.

Its bottom had settled against a loose corner of the loading frame, and each attempt to move it drew a harsh scrape across the timber.

Brunti watched him try once.

Then again.

The old instinct rose.

She could lift the crate with one hand if her wrist were sound. She could show him where the frame was poorly fitted. She could make the problem disappear before anyone noticed.

Her left wrist ached beneath the brace.

She went to the attendant.

“Do you want a second pair of hands?”

He looked at her damaged arm.

“Not if it hurts.”

“It will. I can look at the frame instead, if you want.”

He considered that.

“All right.”

Brunti crouched beside the loading platform, careful to keep her injured wrist free of weight. She pointed out where the timber had bowed beneath the crate. The attendant fetched a small wooden wedge and set it where she indicated.

The frame lifted just enough.

The crate slid.

He looked pleased.

“That's better.”

“Yes.”

“Thank you.”

“You did the lifting.”

“You knew where to put the wedge.”

Brunti stood.

Both statements were true.

She had not needed to carry the weight to be useful.

***

The horizon brightened.

Far above, a moving band of stone and metal passed between the clouds, its underside catching the first pale light of morning. It was not yet close enough for Brunti to recognize individual towers or the pattern of its anchor stones, but the sight made the fine crystal within her chest answer with a familiar, quiet hum.

Somewhere along that route her own Skyland was approaching the places where its people would gather for convergence.

Darrik would be inspecting supports.

The memorial panel would remain isolated.

The resting chamber would still be safe.

And within the old casing, two distinct stones would wait for the work of seeing them clearly.

Brunti did not have a new relic to bring.

She had no witness from Atranor, no answer from the Blackwood keeper and no repaired history that the world itself had handed her.

She carried a copied drawing with uncertain edges.

A letter from someone who had kept working.

A damaged wrist.

And a way of telling the truth that left room for another person to check it.

The attendant called that the lift was ready.

Velen came from the shelter with the travel case secured against their shoulder.

Brunti looked once toward the roads below, where Sira had gone to deliver the messages entrusted to her. Then she turned toward the platform.

The first step was low.

She took it carefully.

The lift began to rise, carrying the two travelers toward the work waiting above the clouds.
