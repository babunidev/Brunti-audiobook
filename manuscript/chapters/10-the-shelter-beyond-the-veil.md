# Chapter Ten — The Shelter Beyond the Veil

The smoke rose from the grass before Brunti saw the pilgrim.

It came in a dark, uneven thread, broken by the wind. No fire burned nearby. On either side of the road the summer meadows stretched toward low hills, and the only trees stood far off along a watercourse. A cart had passed less than an hour before, leaving narrow tracks in the dry earth.

Then something coughed.

Brunti stopped.

A Rokur woman stood beside the road with one hand raised near her mouth. Orange and purple light moved beneath the dark texture of her shoulders, but the brightness had sunk low, as though the flame within her had withdrawn to some deep place. She had a travel bundle over one arm. The other rested on a stone beside the path.

She tried to greet them and coughed again.

Black smoke escaped in a brief cloud. She waited for it to clear, head lowered, while a gust passed across the empty grass.

Sira was already moving.

“May we come closer?” she asked.

The pilgrim nodded, then caught her foot on a loose stone as she shifted her weight.

Brunti reached her before she fell, but did not take hold of her. She offered an arm. The Rokur used it to steady herself, her heat passing gently through the metal fastening on Brunti's sleeve.

“Thank you,” the woman said. “I am walking badly today.”

“Where are you going?” Brunti asked.

“To a place where I can stop.”

“That could be anywhere.”

“It has not been anywhere so far.”

The reply came with a weary attempt at a smile.

Her name was Aru. She had crossed the southern pilgrim roads and turned north through Ethera's Vale after hearing of an old sanctuary whose hearth never went dark. There she meant to rest before continuing toward relatives who lived beyond the central trade ways.

“I thought I could reach it yesterday,” she said.

“Did you get lost?” Sira asked.

“No. I knew where I was. That was part of the trouble.”

Brunti looked at the woman's travel bundle. It was lighter than her own pack, yet the strap had worn a deep groove across the cloth.

“Are you wounded?”

“Not in the way you mean.”

Aru sat on the roadside stone and closed her eyes for a moment. Her shoulders still glimmered, but each flicker came after a long pause.

“I have traveled too far without good rest,” she said. “I kept deciding I could manage one more stretch of road.”

Brunti recognized the sentence. She had used it herself on the longest training marches above the Skylands, when the proper rest station stood behind and the next one seemed nearly within reach.

Sira crouched beside the pilgrim.

“Would water help? Or a quiet place out of the wind?”

“Water, please. And company for a while.”

Brunti took a cup from her pack while Sira found a sheltered place beside the roadside bank. Aru accepted the water slowly. The cold made a faint hiss against the warmth of her hands, but she drank without trouble.

“Do you know the route to the hearth?” Brunti asked.

“I know the northern turn. Beyond it the stones stand close together. Travelers say the entrance is guarded by a seal.”

“Guarded against what?”

Aru opened her eyes.

“I have never been there. They only told me it was a refuge.”

Brunti could have insisted upon more. Instead she looked at the fine smoke still rising from Aru's shoulders.

The woman needed rest before directions.

They stayed beside her until the afternoon light had shifted across the road. No revelation came from the sky. The grass moved in the ordinary breeze, and the three travelers shared what nourishment they carried.

When Aru stood, she did so with greater care.

“I can walk,” she said. “Just not as quickly as I would like.”

Sira rose beside her.

“Then the road will have to bear our disappointment.”

***

They kept to the trade way until evening, taking the shorter stretches between wells and sheltered banks.

Brunti watched Aru's footing, though she tried not to make her watching obvious. Whenever the pilgrim stopped, she gave her room to choose when to begin again. Sira walked on Aru's other side, sometimes humming under her breath, sometimes speaking of the villages along the river routes.

The third traveler was less talkative. Brunti had brought Thalen's note out twice that morning and put it away without reading it. Aetherion was now a real destination, with an archive and a name to ask for. Every detour made the chance of returning before the Skyland's convergence a little less certain.

At the next crossroads they found a wagon with its rear wheel raised off the ground.

A pair of drivers were trying to set a new pin into the axle. The wood around the fitting had split, and the iron ring would not sit properly.

Brunti studied the damage.

“May I look?”

One driver wiped his hands on his coat.

“You know wheels?”

“I know what happens when a ring is forced over wood that has already begun to part.”

The driver hesitated, then stepped aside.

Brunti knelt. The wheel was heavy and the daylight was failing. She had the tools to cut a new wedge, and in her pack she carried a small piece of metal that could hold the ring while the wood was stabilized.

Her hands moved almost of their own accord.

Then she stopped.

“How much farther do you need it to travel?” she asked.

“To the next repair yard,” the driver said. “Not all the way to the city.”

“And how much weight?”

“Half a load. We can move the rest onto the other cart.”

Brunti examined the split again. That changed the work.

She fitted a temporary support to keep the ring from slipping, marked the damaged wood so the yard would see it, and told the driver exactly what the repair could bear.

“Not a full load,” she said. “And not a faster road.”

He tested the wheel, then offered her a little coin.

Brunti refused.

“Keep it for the proper repair.”

The driver thanked her, and Sira waited until they had resumed walking before speaking.

“You asked what the wheel was meant to do.”

“Yes.”

“You didn't ask that question in the memorial passage.”

Brunti looked ahead.

“I am aware.”

“I wasn't scolding you.”

“I am aware of that as well.”

Sira's mouth tightened.

For several strides neither spoke.

Then Aru, who had listened to the exchange, said, “I am very glad you did not try to repair me in the same way.”

Brunti stopped.

The pilgrim's voice was so dry that for a moment Brunti could not tell whether she was joking.

Sira began to laugh.

Aru joined her, softly enough to avoid another cough.

Brunti shook her head and followed them down the road.

***

The next trouble came from the grass.

A gust swept across the eastern meadow, flattening a broad circle around a lone figure. Her garments snapped sharply in the force of the air. Beyond her stood three bandits, their cloaks pulled close against the wind.

The figure in the circle was Aevis.

Brunti could see the shifting light along her form, the way the edges of her sleeves and hands moved as though pressure held them together. Her arms were lifted, drawing another current between herself and the nearest bandit. Each movement seemed more difficult than the last.

One bandit pressed forward with a short blade.

The Aevis turned toward him. The wind rose and struck his legs, forcing him backward.

A second bandit moved to her other side.

Sira caught Brunti's arm.

“She's being surrounded.”

Brunti had already seen it.

She lowered her pack and drew her hammer. The bandits' positions made a broad arc, with the Aevis at its center and a narrow gap toward the open road. Brunti could charge the nearest man, but the others would have a clear path to the woman while she was occupied.

“What do you need?” Brunti called.

The Aevis looked toward them.

“An opening!”

Her voice was thin under the rushing air.

Brunti looked again at the bandits. The one with the blade was recovering his footing. Another held a wooden staff. The third had moved behind a low rock where he could approach without crossing the strongest gust.

“Can you reach the road?” Brunti shouted.

“Not while they hold the sides!”

Brunti turned toward Sira.

“Take Aru back to the bank.”

Sira did not move.

“Aru can choose where to stand,” she said sharply.

Aru was already stepping away from the path. “I choose that bank. Sira, bring me the water.”

Brunti felt the sting of the correction. There was no time to answer it.

She moved toward the low rock.

The bandit behind it saw her coming and raised his weapon. Brunti caught the wooden shaft against the iron shoulder of her hammer. The impact rang sharply through her arms. She turned the weapon aside, stepped past the man's leading foot and put her body between him and the Aevis.

He stumbled backward.

The other bandits turned.

“What business is this of yours?” one shouted.

“Someone has asked for a road,” Brunti said. “Make one.”

The man with the blade lunged toward her.

Brunti set her hammer low and struck the hard earth at his feet. The blow broke the dry crust beside a shallow rut. Grit scattered across his boots, and he jumped back to keep his balance. Before he could recover, a gust from the Aevis caught his cloak and wrapped it around his arm.

He cursed and tore it free.

The opening toward the road widened.

“Now,” Brunti called.

The Aevis moved.

For a moment her form blurred, her sleeve trailing into a pale current. She had been holding too much pressure against the circling bandits. As she crossed the gap the wind around her thinned, and her next step faltered.

Sira came forward with Aru close behind.

“Can you follow me?” Sira asked the Aevis.

“Yes.”

“Then keep your eyes on the bank.”

They moved toward the roadside shelter. Brunti backed after them, hammer raised but not swinging.

The bandit with the blade considered another attack. His companion had lost the staff in the grass. The third was still struggling to regain his footing on the broken ground. Whatever they had hoped to take from a lone traveler now lay farther away behind a Grimnir warrior and two companions.

“Go,” Brunti said.

The leader spat into the dust. Then he signaled the others and withdrew across the meadow.

Brunti did not follow.

She waited until the last cloak disappeared beyond the far grass before lowering her hammer.

Only then did she turn.

The Aevis woman had reached the bank. Her hands trembled with faint pressure changes, and the air around her seemed reluctant to settle. Sira stood nearby, ready to help without closing in on her.

Aru had taken a place beside the woman and was speaking quietly.

Brunti came closer.

“Are you hurt?”

“Not cut,” the Aevis said. “Tired. The current is difficult to hold after being broken.”

“Can I do anything?”

“Keep the air calm for a little while.”

Brunti looked at the open field.

It was the kind of request she could obey without inventing a repair.

She returned to the road and stood watch.

***

The Aevis was named Tessan.

She was a courier, traveling between outer trade stations with several small packets wrapped inside a weatherproof case. The bandits had asked for her goods, then attempted to take the case when she refused. She had held them off with controlled gusts long enough that her own coherence began to waver.

“I can usually make a path through a crowd,” she said when she was able to speak more steadily. “Not when the crowd has decided to close around me.”

Aru had settled beside her in the lee of the bank.

“The road has a talent for giving the same lesson to different people,” the Rokur murmured.

Brunti glanced at Sira, expecting another remark.

Sira was looking at the place where Tessan's sleeve met her hand. The form had become clearer as the pressure around her settled. Her garments no longer snapped in the air.

“Can you travel?” Sira asked.

“Yes. Slowly.”

“That makes four of us.”

Brunti looked toward the eastern meadow.

“The bandits may return.”

“They might,” Tessan said. “But there are two guarded caravans behind us. I saw them before I turned off the main road.”

“Then we should meet the caravans.”

Aru shook her head.

“I would like to reach the northern shelter if there is a safe route. I cannot manage another crowded night beside the trade road.”

Brunti looked at her.

Then at Tessan.

The Aevis courier had a reason to continue, one different from Aru's. Brunti could not simply choose a destination for all four because it seemed the most defensible to her.

“What do you want to do?” she asked Tessan.

The Aevis considered.

“I need a place with stable air before I attempt another long stretch. If this refuge is near enough, I would go there. But the bandits will remember the road. I do not wish to draw them toward other travelers.”

Sira unfolded their route sketch.

“The northern turn is not far from the guarded way,” she said. “We can walk together until the road divides. Then decide again.”

Brunti nodded.

That was what they did.

Aru walked between them, setting the pace. Tessan kept to the sheltered side of the path, where the wind was gentler. Brunti carried the Aevis courier's heavier case only after Tessan asked her to. Sira walked ahead when the road narrowed and returned when it opened, making sure no one had been left behind.

They reached a travel station at dusk.

There, beneath a low awning, a woman with a basket of reeds was telling a story that had frightened half the village where she lived.

Brunti heard the word *Veilwalker* and stopped.

***

The woman said she had seen two enormous eyes high above the wildflowers, nearly level with the crowns of the nearby trees.

They hovered above the eastern meadow, lidless and steady, without any face she could distinguish behind them. A long tendril descended from the barely visible shape, feeling through the blossoms far below. Other tentacles drifted through the air without touching the ground. No clear body appeared.

“I thought it was looking for something,” she said.

“Did it see you?” Brunti asked.

The woman hesitated.

“The eyes turned toward the road. They held there so long I could not tell whether they had found me.”

“What did you do?”

“I left.”

“Did it follow?”

“No.”

Sira listened from the bench beside the basket.

“Why call it a Veilwalker?” she asked.

“That is the name people in the village use. Some say it comes from Keldor. Others say it has always been here, hidden in the mist.”

“Do you know either of those things?” Brunti asked.

“No,” the woman answered. “I know what I saw.”

Brunti wrote the account down.

She did not connect it to the dark creatures of Keldor, though the name and the tentacles would have made it easy to do so. The witness had observed eyes, tendrils, movement among flowers and no origin at all.

That was enough.

The woman asked whether Brunti believed her.

“I believe you saw something,” Brunti said.

“And the rest?”

“The rest I cannot know from here.”

The woman studied her, then nodded.

“I would rather that than be told it was nothing.”

Brunti closed the notebook.

Across the station, Tessan was checking the seals on her courier case. Aru had found a quiet corner and was resting with her head lowered. Sira was buying a little food for the next stretch of road.

The story remained with Brunti, but it did not need her to solve it tonight.

***

They left the station after a long rest and traveled northward over the following day.

The countryside changed gradually. Low slopes rose between the meadows, and the wind found narrow passages through the stones. The grass remained green, but the evening air held a cold edge that Brunti felt along the exposed crystal seams of her hands.

Aru walked more steadily than before, though she still stopped often.

Tessan had recovered enough to carry her own case. She spoke little of the attack. Once, when Brunti asked whether the bandits might have found another traveler, she answered without looking up.

“I do not know. I have given the station keeper a description of them. That is what I can do from here.”

Brunti accepted the limit, though it troubled her.

Near sunset they came upon a willow standing alone beside a shallow hollow.

A deep snore moved through its hanging branches.

Brunti stopped.

Beneath the tree lay a massive Ogru, grey and rough, so close in color to the earth that it might have been another boulder among the roots. Its chest rose slowly, paused, and fell. Each breath sent a low rumble through the hollow.

Beside its arm lay a weathered pouch.

Gold shone at the mouth of the bag.

Tessan saw it too.

“Someone has been careless,” she whispered.

“Perhaps,” Sira said.

The Ogru shifted. The pouch tilted, opening a little farther, and another bright edge caught the last sunlight.

Brunti watched the sleeping creature.

A few coins would have paid for the next station, and perhaps for a safe escort toward Aetherion. They had lost time and supplies on the road. She could step beneath the willow, take the pouch and return before the Ogru stirred.

The thought arrived so quickly that she disliked it.

Not because the pouch was difficult to reach.

Because the Ogru was asleep.

Aru had stopped a short way behind them. She stood with one hand against a stone, her emberlight faint but steady.

“Is there another path?” she asked.

Brunti looked up the bank. A narrow ridge ran behind the willow, away from the roots and the sleeping body. It would take longer and leave them exposed to the cold wind for a while.

“Yes,” she said.

“Then we will take it.”

No one argued.

They climbed the ridge in single file, careful not to dislodge stones into the hollow below.

Halfway up, Brunti looked back.

The Ogru had settled again. Its pouch lay open beside the roots, and the gold still caught the light. The great grey body breathed in the same slow rhythm, unaware of the travelers who had crossed its meadow.

Brunti turned away.

She did not feel noble.

She was simply glad that the creature could continue sleeping.

***

The stones of the northern refuge appeared at dusk.

They stood tall beside a low rise, their dark surfaces cut with old weathering. Between them a small entrance opened in the hillside. Hearthlight shone from within, golden and steady, but a pale veil filled the doorway.

The barrier moved like thin threads drawn across a loom.

They crossed and parted, gathered into bright knots, then unwound into new patterns without ever leaving an opening.

Aru took a step toward the light.

The veil shivered.

She stopped.

“Can we enter?” she asked.

Brunti approached the doorway. The cold air passed through the stones around it, but the warmth beyond did not escape. She could hear a muted crackle from the hearth. The fire was close enough to illuminate the threshold and far enough away to remain untouchable.

She reached toward the shimmering threads.

“Brunti,” Sira warned.

“I am only looking.”

Her hand stopped short of the barrier.

At its center several strands gathered into a brighter knot. The knot held for a moment, then loosened as the threads flowed back into their crossing pattern.

Brunti watched it happen again.

She thought of Thalen's bookbinding. A stitch made too tight would tear the pages; one made too loose would not hold them together. This barrier did something similar. Its strands held across the opening with a shifting tension, closing every space as soon as it appeared.

There was no safe gap to step through.

Tessan came beside her.

“The air is being turned away,” she said. “Not blocked like a wall. Guided around the entrance.”

“Can you change it?”

“Perhaps. Not by forcing another gust against it. That would only give it more pressure to resist.”

Aru leaned against the outer stone.

“It is a shelter,” she said. “Surely someone made it to be entered.”

Sira examined the worn rock beside the doorway.

At knee height, partly hidden behind a narrow ridge, a smooth circular fitting had been worked into the stone. Its center bore a shallow groove, and the edge had been polished by long use.

“Someone has touched this many times,” she said.

Brunti knelt.

The fitting moved a little beneath her fingers. Not freely. A quiet resistance held it at an angle.

She glanced up at the veil. The central knot brightened, loosened, and formed again.

“Let me see the cycle,” she said.

They waited.

Aru settled on the ground out of the wind. Sira kept the supplies near her. Tessan stood beside the doorway, studying how the air passed around the moving threads.

After several moments Brunti set her hand on the fitting.

“When the knot loosens,” she told Tessan, “does the pressure outside change?”

“Yes. Only a little.”

“Tell me when.”

The Aevis watched.

“Now.”

Brunti turned the fitting gently. It shifted a fraction, then stopped. The barrier drew tighter across the entrance.

She let go at once.

“That was wrong,” she said.

The veil returned to its former pattern.

No damage followed.

She tried again when Tessan gave the signal, this time turning the fitting in the opposite direction. The resistance eased. The bright knot at the center of the seal widened into a narrow oval, then closed.

“Again?” Sira asked.

Brunti hesitated.

She had learned enough to know that a moving mechanism could be broken by repeated force. But the fitting bore the polish of many hands, and it had answered when turned with the seal's own rhythm. It was not a hidden weakness she had found. It seemed more like a handle meant to be used.

“Help me count the turns,” she said.

Sira watched the light. Tessan marked each slight change in the air. Brunti moved the fitting only when the pattern loosened, never against the returning tension.

On the third cycle, the central threads gathered at the sides of the doorway instead of crossing its middle.

A narrow passage opened through the glow.

Warmth touched Brunti's face.

Aru looked up.

“Can I go through?”

“Yes,” Brunti said. “Slowly. We don't know how long it will remain open.”

Sira supported the pilgrim as she rose. Tessan passed through after them, keeping her courier case close to her body. Brunti held the fitting steady while the last threads quivered along the edge of the passage.

Then she followed.

The veil closed behind her with a sound like silk drawn across stone.

***

The chamber was small.

The rock curved inward around a modest hearth where an eternal fire burned with a quiet golden light. Its flames moved gently, throwing long shadows across walls that seemed untouched by the years. Earthen jars and clay vessels rested along the floor, their surfaces clean and unbroken. No dust lay upon them.

Beside the hearth stood a simple stone bench, smoothed by generations of use.

Aru sat first.

She closed her eyes, allowing the warmth and stillness to settle around her. The smoke that had troubled her on the road did not vanish at once. A thin trace rose from her shoulders, then faded as she rested without speaking.

Tessan chose a place near the doorway where the air moved gently. She laid her courier case beside her and let the pressure around her hands settle into a calmer pattern.

Sira set down the food and water they had brought.

Brunti remained standing.

The fire had no visible fuel. The jars showed no trace of age. Beyond the barrier the northern wind moved through the stones, yet inside there was only the quiet crackle of the flames.

She was tempted to examine the hearth closely, to discover how its fire endured without consuming the chamber.

Then Aru spoke.

“Could you bring me some water?”

Brunti turned at once.

“Of course.”

She took the cup Aru indicated, filled it from their own supplies, and brought it to the bench. The Rokur accepted it with both hands.

“Thank you,” she said.

Brunti sat beside her.

They remained there for a long while, listening to the hearth.

The chamber held no voices of ancient keepers, no instruction carved into its walls that Brunti could read. Whatever magic preserved the warmth and closed the threshold had been made for a practical purpose.

A place to rest.

Outside, night settled over the stones.

***

They stayed until morning.

Aru's emberlight grew steadier through the night, though she remained tired. Tessan slept only in brief intervals, waking whenever the air shifted near the doorway. Sira arranged their remaining food into small portions and noted which supplies they could spare without leaving themselves unable to continue.

Brunti spent the first part of the night beside the entrance.

She could hear the wind striking the outer stones. The veil held fast. Its woven threads did not seem strained by their passage, and the fitting she had turned lay silent again.

At one point she rose to examine the threshold more closely.

Then she stopped.

The mechanism was working. No one had asked her to improve it.

She returned to the bench.

In the morning Aru stood with greater ease.

“I can walk again,” she said.

“For how long?” Brunti asked.

“Long enough to reach the next place of rest if I choose it properly.”

“That is not an answer I can measure.”

Aru smiled.

“It is the answer I have.”

Tessan checked the seals on her case and thanked them for the escort. She intended to return to the guarded trade roads and report the bandits before continuing her courier route.

Sira offered to carry a message for her if she wished. Tessan considered, then wrote a brief account on a narrow strip of paper and sealed it herself.

“Only to the station warden,” she said.

Sira took it without opening it.

Brunti watched the exchange and thought of the letters Sira had carried from the river villages. Some messages were spoken. Some were written. All of them depended upon someone knowing where they belonged.

Before they left, the four travelers set aside a small parcel of supplies.

There was dried food, a little mineral salt, clean wrapping cloth and a note explaining that the parcel was for the next traveler in need. They placed it on a dry shelf near the bench, away from the old vessels. Nothing in the sanctuary was taken or rearranged.

Brunti looked around once more.

The fire burned with the same steady light it had held when they entered. The bench remained beneath the warm wall. The jars stood untouched.

“Someone made this place,” she said.

“Yes,” Sira replied.

“And they made it last.”

Aru rested one hand against the stone beside the doorway.

“Perhaps they also knew that travelers would sometimes arrive without enough strength to go farther.”

Brunti looked at the pale threads of the seal.

The barrier had protected the chamber from the weather and from those who would force it. Yet its maker had left a way for patient hands to open it. Not a broken place. An intended one.

She thought of the memorial wall on her Skyland.

A safe resting chamber. A protected archive. A place where memory could remain heard.

The words had begun to take new shapes in her mind.

She could still imagine bringing the old drawing home and showing Darrik that the memorial might be repaired. She could also imagine asking him where its voices could be heard without carrying their sound into a place meant for healing.

That question had not existed for her when she left.

***

They departed after the morning light touched the upper stones.

Brunti waited by the entrance until Aru and Tessan had passed through the veil. Sira followed. Brunti stepped out last, turning the small fitting back with the same care she had used to open it.

The threads gathered and crossed.

The pale barrier settled across the doorway, and the hearthlight remained visible beyond it, warm and undisturbed.

Tessan stood beneath the open sky for a moment, allowing the morning air to pass around her. Her outline held steady.

“Thank you,” she said to the others.

Aru bowed her head.

“I thought I needed to reach the hearth before I could rest,” she said. “It seems I needed to rest before I could reach it.”

Sira smiled.

Brunti regarded the northern road.

Aetherion still lay ahead. Aru tested her breath in the morning air and surprised herself with a little laugh. Tessan adjusted the fastening of her courier case; this time the strap rested against a steady shoulder.

At the next fork the travelers parted. Tessan turned toward the guarded road, carrying her own messages. Aru chose the slower pilgrim route, promising herself frequent stops. Brunti and Sira followed the way toward Aetherion.

They walked for a while without speaking.

Then Sira said, “You asked each of them what they wanted.”

“Yes.”

“Even when you were afraid.”

Brunti looked at her.

“I was very afraid.”

Sira nodded.

“I know.”

The admission felt strange in the open air. Brunti had faced bandits with her hammer and stood before an enchantment whose pattern she did not understand. She had thought of the Skyland's deadline all through the night. None of it had been pleasant.

But Sira did not turn the fear into praise.

She simply walked beside her.

In the distance the grasslands opened beneath a pale sky, and the public road curved among the low hills. Travelers moved along it in small groups, their carts and packs carrying ordinary things toward the great city.

Brunti adjusted the weight of her hammer and continued.

Somewhere behind them, hidden among tall stones in northern Ethera's Vale, the fire kept burning.
