# Chapter Twenty-Three — Those Who Still Tend the Forest

The woman at the gate did not ask Brunti what she was hunting.

She asked whether the bundles on her shoulders were dry.

“Mostly,” Brunti said.

“Then put them beside the east wall. The roof leaks over there, but the shelf doesn't.”

Beyond the woven hedge, a low building rested against the trunks of three living trees. Lanterns hung under a patched canvas roof. A bucket knocked softly against stone while a narrow channel carried water into a settling basin. The source of the tapping Brunti had heard from the road was an older man sitting on a bench, repairing the frame of a folding cot.

He looked up.

“More travelers?”

“More supplies,” the woman answered.

“Better.”

Sira set down the cloth she had brought from the relief station. Velen produced the route marks they had copied along the way. Varun spoke quietly to the gatekeeper, explaining which crossings remained passable and which tracks they had deliberately avoided.

The gatekeeper listened to him before turning to the three newcomers.

“My name is Elda. We keep the western shelter running when the other stations can't reach the people who are still out here. The forest makes some journeys longer than maps expect.”

“Do you know of a missing child?” Sira asked.

“Several.”

The answer arrived without drama.

Sira took out the note she had carried from the family. Elda read it beneath the lantern, then gave the paper back without copying the name.

“A child with a stick like that was reported beyond the western stream,” she said. “I can't say who she was. The messenger didn't stop long enough to learn.”

“Who brought the report?” Velen asked.

“A young scholar. She came here short of food and worried about what she'd seen on the animal trails. She went out again this morning to look for a safer route.”

“Alone?”

“She insisted she knew her work. I didn't have anyone free to go with her.”

A debate had been taking place when the travelers arrived. The carpenter wanted to keep the shelter's remaining workers close to the roof and water channel. Another caretaker wanted to send someone farther into the trees, where wounded travelers sometimes gathered near a clearing lit by fireflies.

“We can't do both,” Elda said.

Brunti looked at the damaged cot, the half-filled water jars and the folded cloth waiting to be made into clean bindings.

“We brought hands,” she said.

“You brought hands that need rest.”

“They can rest after doing something.”

Sira gave her a look.

Brunti reconsidered.

“They can rest first,” she said.

Elda smiled, barely.

“Now you sound like somebody who might be useful.”

***

The shelter offered one room dry enough for the travelers to sleep in. Its walls carried the close scent of wet timber, clean bandages and boiled roots. A small lamp burned until midnight while the people on the late watch sorted their notes into messages for the eastern stations.

Velen helped with the sorting. It was a better use for their trained hands than lifting the heavy roof beams, and they admitted as much without being asked.

Brunti slept against a timber support, where the floor was firm and the air did not press cold dampness against her mineral seams. When morning came, she woke to Sira speaking softly with Elda outside.

The argument from the night before had not gone away.

“We have a chance to find someone,” Sira said.

“And a shelter full of people who already depend on us,” Elda answered.

“Then we need to carry enough to help the ones we find, and leave enough here.”

“Who decides what's enough?”

“Not me alone.”

Brunti came out into the pale light.

They counted the supplies with the carpenter and the two people tending the settling basin. Water skins, cloth, mineral salts, food, lantern oil, rope and two litters were placed in separate piles. Elda kept what the shelter required for its residents and the next expected courier. The rest could accompany a small group along the marked forest routes.

Varun agreed to lead the way.

“I won't take you to the Blackwood,” he said.

“No one asked you to,” Brunti replied.

“People often don't ask until we're halfway there.”

Sira unfolded the note.

“We're looking for the last witness, not a door beneath the earth.”

Velen made a mark beside the route.

*Search limited to attended clearings and known return tracks.*

Brunti looked at the line for a moment.

Then she helped carry the supplies out. Before the group passed the gate, Brunti checked the straps on both litters and asked who would hold the rear of the party if they needed to turn back. She took that place herself. The skill had been taught to warriors for a reason: a person carrying someone injured must never have to be the last one watching the path.

***

The bright hut appeared where the forest should have been darkest.

At first it was only a narrow shaft of golden light among the branches. Then a patch of grass came into view, fresh and richly green, spreading around the base of a wooden building whose walls were covered with vigorous moss.

Keldor's oppressive dimness seemed to stop a few steps from its doorstep.

A bubbling chuckle escaped the open window.

Something rattled within.

A second laugh followed, louder and more delighted.

Varun raised a hand.

“That's the Enclave.”

Brunti had expected a guarded outpost, perhaps a healer's room or another storehouse. Instead the little house was crowded with painted boards, hanging herbs and shelves that seemed to occupy more space than the walls could reasonably hold.

An elderly hag stood behind a cluttered counter among crooked vessels, tarnished ornaments, odd glasswork and magical curiosities whose uses were not immediately apparent. A little light caught on a patch of bright green moss outside the doorway.

She was moving a precarious tower of curiosities from one end of a shelf to the other. The uppermost piece wobbled and began to fall. She caught it between two quick fingers, gave it a stern, suspicious look, steadied the whole arrangement with a practiced touch, and looked at the visitors as though they had arrived precisely in time to admire her achievement.

“Well,” she said, “that's saved us all from a very small catastrophe.”

A glass object behind her gave another ominous rattle.

She glanced at it.

“Not you. You can fall if you wish.”

Brunti smiled before she meant to.

The hag's shop smelled of old wood, drying herbs and a dozen other things too mixed to name. The light reached the farthest corners. Even the bundles beneath the counter cast no deep shadows.

“We're not shopping for a miracle,” Sira said.

“Then you're the first sensible people I've seen this week.”

The hag swept a hand over the clutter.

“I do sell ordinary goods, though it hurts my reputation.”

Varun told her about the child and the last report.

She listened until he finished.

“A small Verdani with a long stick? I've heard someone humming in the western growth. There are always children who hum. It tells you nothing about who they are.”

“Did you see the person?” Brunti asked.

“No.”

“Which way was the song?”

The hag pointed toward the low country beyond the stream.

“The way the light goes when the clouds move,” she said.

Velen glanced up.

“Which direction is that?”

“West, most days. Here, occasionally, the forest has different opinions.”

Brunti waited.

The hag sighed, took an old route tile from beneath the counter, and placed it before them.

“There's a path that meets Sageglow Clearing. The scholar there sometimes sees who comes through. Ask him before you follow the sound of every poor singer in the woods.”

“That sounds like real advice,” Sira said.

“I contain multitudes.”

A small jar slipped from the edge of a shelf.

Brunti caught it before it reached the floor.

The hag looked at the jar, then at Brunti's hands.

“Useful multitudes,” she amended.

Brunti returned the jar.

“Is there a price for the route?”

“Tell Elda I still have the hinges she wanted. And remind her that a house with a door should occasionally close it.”

Sira laughed.

“She's been saying that about this place?”

“Everyone has been saying it about this place. That's why nobody can find me when they need me.”

They left beneath the green moss and warm light. The hag was humming to herself while she set a trembling object into a safer position.

Nothing about her house explained the forest's curse.

Brunti found that she did not mind.

***

Sageglow Clearing lay farther along the attended path, beyond a stretch of tall undergrowth through which Varun moved with considerable caution.

Fireflies gathered above an open book.

Their lights drifted together and apart, brightening each line as though someone had carried tiny lanterns over the page. Beneath them sat a Verdani scholar, his delicate antlers outlined against the darker trees.

His finger followed a passage slowly.

A firefly settled beside the binding. The scholar moved his thumb to give it room. He waited until the light brightened, then lifted his head.

“Visitors,” he said.

“Travelers,” Varun corrected. “With questions.”

“That's what visitors generally bring.”

The scholar's smile made faint creases beside his eyes. The air was clearer here than beneath the surrounding canopy, and Brunti could smell dry pages beneath the forest damp.

Velen crouched beside the book.

“May I look?”

“Certainly. I haven't finished the line, but it will still be there.”

He shifted the tome so its open page faced outward, supporting the worn binding with one hand. Several fireflies moved toward the new angle. Light settled softly across the next passage.

Brunti watched Velen resist the urge to read the whole page.

“We're following reports of a child,” Sira began.

The scholar listened to the family description without interruption. When she mentioned the adult-sized walking stick, his face became serious.

“I saw a stick,” he said. “Not the child carrying it.”

“Where?”

“Near the western bend. A young Aevis woman came through at dawn and told me she had seen someone small moving beyond a place where the plants had begun to fail. She drew the stick in the margin of her field notes.”

“Do you have that drawing?”

“No. She kept her notes.”

“Did she give a name?” Velen asked.

“She called herself a Skylore Scholar. She was studying Keldor's changed creatures and looked tired enough to be carrying the forest on her shoulders.”

He pointed to the route tile from the hag.

“She went toward the guarded ridge, where two Aevis women have been holding back the worst of the growth. A man tending the plants has been working nearby. You'll find them separately; don't mistake their different work for one great spell.”

“Do you know whether the scholar saw the child herself?” Brunti asked.

“He said she had seen someone small. I wasn't there. You should ask her.”

Velen wrote down the distinction.

The Verdani reader closed his book.

“There's another young one who comes and goes near the lower glade,” he added. “A boy who sings when he's able. He needs company, and perhaps care, but I have never seen him carry a walking stick.”

Sira looked up quickly.

“A boy?”

“Yes.”

“Our missing child may be a girl.”

“Then do not make him into her merely because he needs the same help.”

The fireflies drifted toward the closed cover, where a final line of light lingered over the worn leather.

The scholar stood.

“I can send word to Elda if you find anyone. And I can help keep the marked path clear while you return.”

Brunti thanked him.

It was not a promise to solve the problem.

It was a promise to remain.

***

The guarded ridge announced itself with a soft, strained shimmer among the trees.

An armored Aevis woman stood beneath a canopy from which dark, root-like tendrils extended toward her. A feathered crown caught what little light reached the place. Her armor was finely worked, though its surfaces showed wear from a long vigil.

A thin radiance stood between her face and the approaching dark.

The tendrils pressed forward.

The light met them. They recoiled, gathered, and approached again.

The guardian placed one hand against her armor to steady herself.

When the travelers came within speaking distance, she greeted them with warmth that seemed almost out of place against the struggle at her back.

“Welcome. Keep to the open stones.”

Her shimmer weakened as she spoke.

Velen moved a little closer, then stopped where she had indicated.

“Do you need help?”

“Someone has to keep the lower passage clear,” she said. “If the growth reaches the road, the people bringing provisions will have nowhere to turn.”

“Could you leave for a while?” Brunti asked.

“I could. The passage would close after me.”

That was not pride. It was a fact she had been living with.

A feather trembled above her brow. She raised her hand again, holding the darkness a short distance from the stones.

Brunti studied the ground.

There were three places where old roots pressed through the roadbed, leaving a narrow channel for travelers. She could not mend the magical veil, but she could move a fallen block that forced people closer to the guardian's struggle.

“May I clear the stone?” she asked.

“Which one?”

“The broken one beside the passage. It pushes anyone carrying a litter toward your shield.”

The guardian glanced at it.

“Yes. But do not disturb the roots beneath.”

Brunti set down her pack and worked the block free with a short iron lever. The stone was heavier than it looked, its underside bound by a web of fine roots. She eased it upward only as far as the roots allowed, then shifted it onto a firm patch outside the path. When she finished, a wider space opened for anyone carrying someone who could not walk.

The guardian looked at the new passage.

“Thank you.”

“Does it make your work easier?”

“It means fewer people must come so close.”

A small improvement. Not freedom.

Sira offered her water and a portion of the clean supplies.

The guardian accepted the water, then pointed toward the next shelter marker.

“There's another Aevis traveler down the slope. Her protection is failing. If you're carrying bindings and rest, she may need you more urgently.”

Brunti looked at the thin shimmer still trembling before the guardian.

“We could stay.”

“You've made space for the path. Use it.”

They did.

***

Blue light flickered among the low trees below the ridge.

An Aevis woman stood alone in the shade. Finely woven garments moved around her though the leaves nearby were still. Her dark hair lifted from her face and settled again as though the air could not decide which direction to travel.

A fragile blue aura surrounded her shoulders.

She offered a low, musical greeting.

As the words faded, the light thinned and the forest's darkness drew nearer. She steadied herself without moving her feet.

One hand opened toward the travelers.

The fingers trembled.

“Skyheart Drifter?” Varun asked.

The woman nodded.

“I'm meant to reach the shelter,” she said. “The path keeps slipping away from me.”

“It's still here,” Sira replied. “We can walk beside you.”

The drifter's gaze moved toward the trees.

“When I lose the light, I lose the edge of the path.”

Velen took a strip of the blue-and-green shelter cloth and tied it to the nearest branch.

“We can keep one marker in sight until the next,” they said. “You don't have to hold the whole route in your head.”

Brunti moved to the woman's other side without touching her.

Sira asked whether she could help steady the aura.

“Not safely,” the drifter replied. “What I need is rest and air that doesn't press against my pattern.”

“Then we'll help you reach it,” Sira said.

The light along the drifter's wrist flared once, delicate against the undergrowth, and dimmed again. She followed the visible cloth marks, step by careful step, until they reached a small rest station near the ridge where another caretaker could remain with her.

Velen checked the time and route before they left.

Brunti looked back once. The woman's blue light was still faint, but she no longer stood alone.

***

The Skylore Scholar was found at a place where three animal trails crossed.

She was young, even by the long measures of her people. Twilight colors shifted through her skin and hair as she watched a disturbance moving through the low growth. Her head followed it, then stopped when the forest quieted.

She remained still until the movement had gone.

Only then did she turn to the visitors.

“Don't step into that patch,” she said. “Something has been nesting there. It isn't safe to guess what.”

Varun introduced them.

Sira explained the missing child and the walking stick, keeping the private family details folded inside her coat.

The scholar's expression changed.

“I saw a young person by the broken stream,” she said. “I could not tell whether they were traveling alone. I didn't approach; the animals nearby were moving strangely, and the ground between us was unsafe.”

“Did the child have a stick?” Brunti asked.

“Yes.”

“Longer than the person?”

“Yes. I sketched it.”

She opened a narrow field journal. Along one margin, between drawings of leaves and disturbed tracks, stood the outline of a long, crooked stick with a cluster of knots near its upper end.

Velen took the journal only when offered.

“May I copy the drawing?”

“Please. I need the original for my work.”

The sketch showed a split in the wood near the handle, repaired with a strip of binding.

Sira went very still.

“That's in the family's description,” she said.

“Then it may be the same stick,” Velen replied.

“Or another made in the same way,” Brunti added.

The scholar nodded.

“I know where I saw it. I don't know where the person went afterward.”

She marked the location on Varun's map.

Only then did Brunti notice the scholar's unsteady hands. Her packet of provisions hung open from her belt, half empty.

“Have you eaten?”

“Not enough. My food pouch tore when I crossed the stones yesterday.”

“How much do you have left?”

“Less than I planned. The research has taken longer than I thought.”

Brunti handed her a portion of the food Elda had approved for the search.

“Take this.”

“You need it for the people you find.”

“That's why we brought it.”

The young Aevis accepted the food with a look of relief so plain that Brunti felt ashamed she had nearly overlooked it.

A rustling came from the underbrush.

The scholar glanced toward it at once. This time her shoulders lowered.

“I won't follow that trail today,” she said.

“Good,” Varun replied. “Come back toward the marked path after you rest. We'll tell the western shelter where you were.”

The scholar made a note of the place and the name of the guide.

Their inquiry had given them something better than another rumor: a drawing made by a person who knew exactly where she had stood.

It was still not the child.

***

Beyond the broken stream, the undergrowth looked sick.

Leaves curled inward along their veins. Several plants had darkened from their edges, and the earth beneath them carried a stale smell that no ordinary decay quite explained.

An Aevis man stood beside a low, blighted shrub.

Scholarly insignia marked his robes. His skin and hair shifted in Keldor's muted light as he traced a careful pattern over the plant.

A faint magic gathered between his hands and descended toward the curled leaves.

He held the shape steady while his eyes turned toward a sound among the trees. His hands did not follow his gaze.

“Keep that path clear,” he called. “Please.”

Varun and Brunti moved toward the far side of the opening.

The envoy repeated the working, slower this time.

“Is there something we can do?” Velen asked.

“Watch the underbrush. I can't turn my hands away whenever it moves.”

They took positions where they could see the surrounding trail without stepping into the damaged soil. Brunti held her hammer low, not because she expected a battle but because the tool gave her a way to move broken wood without touching the blighted plants.

A branch stirred.

Nothing emerged.

The envoy continued.

The smallest leaf on the shrub uncurled a fraction. It was a modest change, but the color along its edge stopped darkening.

He lowered his hands, weary.

“Enough for now.”

“Did you heal it?” Sira asked.

“I stopped the change from spreading for a little while. That is not the same thing.”

Brunti liked the answer.

Velen showed him the scholar's drawing and asked about a young person with a long walking stick.

The envoy nodded.

“I saw a small figure farther west, toward the lower glade. I wasn't near enough to see a face. I remember the stick because it caught on the roots and made a distinct knocking sound.”

“How long ago?”

“This morning.”

The first current report.

The group checked the sun's position and compared the statement with the scholar's earlier account. If the figure had moved slowly, the two sightings could belong to the same journey. Neither witness claimed they had watched the child travel between them.

Sira drew a steady breath.

“Then we can look at the glade.”

The envoy pointed toward the safe side of the slope.

“Go by the standing stones. The direct path cuts through the worst growth. And if you find anyone, bring them back through the guarded ridge.”

“Will you still be here?” Brunti asked.

“For now.”

He raised his hands again and returned to the plant.

***

The first child was singing to no one.

A few notes drifted from a secluded glade, so faint that Sira stopped to hear whether they were a voice or water passing beneath the roots.

A young Verdani boy sat among the shadows. His delicate horns scarcely rose above the growth around him. His deer-like face was lowered toward hands folded tightly in his lap.

He hummed three notes, paused, and began again on a softer one.

Darkness gathered through the clearing, dimming the small brightness in his eyes. He tried to form the beginning of another phrase, but only a breath followed.

Sira crouched at the edge of the glade.

“May we sit here?”

The boy looked up.

His eyes moved from Sira to Brunti's broad mineral shoulders and then to Velen's folded papers.

“You can,” he whispered.

They did not hurry toward him.

Sira offered water. Brunti placed a bundle of food on a flat stone within his reach and moved away so he could take it or leave it. Velen asked his name only after he had taken a small portion and seemed able to listen.

He gave it quietly.

It was not the name on Sira's private list.

Brunti glanced at her.

Sira gave the slightest shake of her head.

The boy was not the one they had come to find.

He still needed help.

“Are you trying to reach the shelters?” Sira asked.

He nodded.

“Can you walk?”

“Yes. When the trees stop sounding like that.”

“What do they sound like?”

The boy looked toward the darker edge of the clearing.

“Like everyone is calling at once.”

Sira waited. She did not tell him what he meant.

After a while he began to hum again, holding one note in the space where the song had stopped. The faint rhythm seemed to help him remain steady.

Brunti knew the way a Grimnir memory stone could carry an old contour even when the full name of a life had been lost. This boy's song was not that sort of record. It was something he could still choose to make.

“We can help you reach a place with clearer air,” she said.

“Will you make me sing there?”

“No.”

He considered.

“Can I sing if I want to?”

“Yes.”

That answer seemed enough.

Varun took him toward a marked waiting point where the western shelter could send a caretaker. A short whistle agreed upon at the ridge would tell the others when the boy was safely met.

Brunti and Sira watched them go.

Velen remained beside the glade for a moment.

“Two missing young people,” they said.

“Two separate lives,” Sira replied.

“And one of them is still somewhere ahead.”

They followed the quieter path west.

***

The stick knocked against a root.

Then again.

Brunti heard it before she saw the child.

The path curved around a leaning tree whose exposed roots held a shallow pocket of damp earth. In its shadow a small Verdani girl was trying to free a long, knotted walking stick caught between two twisted ridges of wood.

She pulled once.

The stick did not move.

She shifted her grip and worked it free with both hands. Then she leaned against it, gathering herself before taking another step.

Her face was small and faun-like, framed by delicate horns. A whispered tune accompanied her movement, losing notes whenever the ground rose or a branch blocked the way.

At the next root she stopped.

The stick remained planted in the earth.

Her fingers tightened around the worn wood, and she looked from one dark passage to another as the last notes of her humming faded into breath.

Sira did not call the name from the letter.

She stepped into view slowly.

“Hello. The path behind us leads toward a shelter.”

The girl looked at her.

“Are you from the wagons?”

“From one of the relief routes. I'm Sira.”

The child glanced past her at Brunti and Velen.

“That's a large person.”

“I'm told,” Brunti said.

“Can you lift trees?”

“Some of them.”

The child looked at the tangled roots.

“Can you lift that one?”

“I don't know. It seems to be doing a good job of staying where it is.”

The girl almost smiled.

Sira stayed where she was.

“We're trying to help someone who was separated from a caravan,” she said. “May I ask you a few things?”

The girl's gaze moved toward the stick.

“Will you take this away?”

“No. Not unless you want someone to carry it.”

She kept her hold on the wood.

Sira asked which road she remembered last, what the wagons looked like, and whether she knew anyone at the shelters. Some answers came quickly. Others took longer, and for one or two the child shook her head.

“I was with a woman who tied blue cloth to the side of the cart,” she said. “The wood was split there. She didn't want anyone catching their fingers in it.”

Sira's hand moved toward the folded report.

Brunti waited.

“Do you remember her name?” Sira asked.

The child spoke it.

It matched the name recorded beside the family report, a detail Sira had not shared with the other travelers.

“And your own?”

The girl gave it in a small voice.

Sira read the written name to herself and looked up.

“Would you tell me where the stick came from?”

“It was my grandfather's,” the child said. “He made a line around the top so I could know which end was mine.”

She showed them the old binding near the handle. Beneath it the wood carried a shallow ring and a small split repaired by a strip of cloth.

The mark matched the drawing from the Skylore Scholar, and the private family description.

Velen was breathing very quietly.

Sira closed the paper.

“I know people who have been looking for you,” she told the child. “They asked me to help find you. They can come to a safe station to meet you.”

The girl did not move.

“Are they angry?”

“No,” Sira said. “They are worried.”

The girl lowered her head. Light moved beneath her translucent skin like water trying to pass through a narrow channel.

“I was supposed to stay with the wagon.”

Sira did not answer for her.

“I went to look for the woman with the blue cloth,” the child continued. “Then the road changed. I kept walking back, but it wasn't the same road.”

Brunti recognized the strain of trying to make a broken route behave as memory insisted it should.

“You're not there now,” she said.

The girl looked up.

“Where am I?”

“On a marked path toward people who can bring you to the caravan stations.”

“Can you carry my stick?”

“If you want.”

The girl considered the offer, then gave the wood to Brunti.

It was longer than it had looked from a distance, a little crooked and smooth where hands had held it for years. Brunti balanced it carefully against her shoulder.

Sira offered her hand.

The girl took it.

No door opened. No curse lifted from the surrounding trees. They had found a child whose family was searching for her, because people had written down where they had last seen a stick, checked the roads and kept a shelter open.

Brunti had never seen a better use for a map.

***

The child could walk, but not quickly.

She tired after the first bend and had difficulty keeping her tune steady when the forest grew darker. Sira found that she could help by speaking softly about ordinary things: the way the relief station arranged its water jars, the blanket colors, a small garden beside a wagon where herbs grew in boxes.

The girl asked whether the garden was still alive.

“Yes,” Sira said. “Someone has been watering it.”

That answer pleased her.

When they reached the lower fringe, a movement among the dead trees made Brunti stop.

A young Verdani woman was crouched beneath the brush. Her small horned head remained low, and a crude splint bound one injured ankle. The binding scraped against a root when she shifted.

Her face showed the strain of hunger.

“Don't come closer,” the woman said.

Brunti stopped at once.

“We're traveling to a shelter,” Sira replied. “There are people who can help with the splint.”

The woman's gaze moved from one traveler to another. She listened behind her, then looked toward the clearer ground beyond the trees.

“Are there Ogrus?”

“Not on the route we came from,” Varun's absence suddenly mattered to Brunti; she corrected herself. “We haven't seen any. I can't tell you what's elsewhere.”

The woman swallowed.

Her account came in short pieces. Ogru captors. A chance to escape. Running into the woods. The ankle injured along the way, and a splint fashioned from what she could find.

Brunti did not ask what had been done to her.

Sira offered clean water and let the woman choose whether to take it.

The injured Verdani drank slowly.

“Can you stand?” Brunti asked.

“Not far.”

“We have a carrying frame a little way back at the ridge,” Velen said. “The wardens can help us bring it. There's a shelter with people who know how to tend injuries.”

The woman looked at the small girl holding Sira's hand.

“Is she yours?”

“No,” Sira said. “We're helping her reach her family.”

The woman's face changed.

“She should not stay here.”

“No one should have to,” Brunti answered.

She set down the walking stick and examined the splint from where she stood.

“I can carry you,” she said, “but only if you agree, and only to the marked station.”

The woman looked toward the clearing beyond the dead trees.

“Would I be able to stop?”

“Whenever you say.”

“Then yes.”

Brunti asked where her ankle hurt most before lifting her. She supported the injured leg without pressing the rough splint into the wound. The woman's body was water held in living form, not fragile glass; its instability showed in the shifting lines near the ankle and the way she guarded it.

They moved slowly.

Velen went ahead with the route markers. Sira walked beside the child, who had taken back her stick and was now using it to keep a steady rhythm. Brunti followed with the injured woman in her arms.

Behind them, the forest kept its many sounds.

For once, none required interpretation.

***

They reached the guarded ridge after dusk.

The Skyshard Guardian still stood beneath her feathered crown, the thin light before her meeting the dark tendrils that pressed toward the path. She saw the injured woman and stepped aside as far as her work allowed, keeping the clear stones open.

The passage Brunti had widened earlier was just broad enough.

A caretaker waited on the far side with a litter and warm coverings. The woman with the splinted ankle asked to be lowered and was helped onto the frame, where she could rest without bearing her weight.

“There's a clear route to Elda's station,” the caretaker said. “The boy you sent earlier is there already.”

Sira looked relieved.

The little girl tightened her grip on the walking stick.

“Will I see the woman with the blue cloth?”

“We've sent word,” Sira replied. “She'll be told where you are. We won't promise when someone can arrive, but you're on the right road now.”

The child nodded.

Velen copied the station's marks into their book, leaving the private names out of the public page. They wrote the child's identity in a sealed message for the family rather than calling it out to every stranger along the path.

Brunti looked at the guardian.

“Can someone relieve you?”

“Some nights,” the Aevis woman said. “Not every night.”

She smiled a little.

“The people in the clearing keep trying.”

There was more road to cross before the safe station. Two figures stood farther along the route, their light visible between the trunks.

***

Fire traveled along a dead tree in a narrow line.

A Rokur woman in rugged leather armor stood beside it, one hand raised. Emberlight glowed through her volcanic features. She spoke a measured incantation and guided the heat through the corrupted wood, keeping it from reaching the living tree beside it.

Ash drifted around her feet.

The dead trunk gave a small crack.

She waited until it settled before lowering her hand.

“Hold there,” she called toward the path. “The roots are hot.”

Brunti paused with the group.

The woman's eyes moved across the darker growth beyond the controlled flame.

“We're moving people to the western shelter,” Sira said. “Is the lower path open?”

“Almost,” the Rokur replied. “That fallen limb has been carrying the blight across the track. I can burn the dead section without taking the healthy roots with it, but I need someone to watch the far side.”

Velen took a place beside the marker where the route remained visible. Brunti moved the little girl and the litter party to a safe patch of stone. The child watched the flame with an expression of fascination.

“Does it hurt the tree?” she asked.

“The dead part is already lost,” the Rokur woman said. “I'm trying to keep the rest.”

The fire moved another hand's width.

Then it stopped.

The woman lowered her arm. The last ember faded where the trunk met the living roots.

She walked toward them.

“I'm called a Flameheart Purifier by people who like titles,” she said. “You can call me whatever helps you remember not to stand in the ash.”

Brunti smiled.

“Where do we go?”

“Along the lower stones. The Cinderheart Vanguard is holding the next turn, though she's worn thin.”

“From what?” Velen asked.

“Too many nights keeping the undead away from people who can't defend themselves.”

The woman looked at the injured Verdani on the litter and then at the small girl beside Sira.

“Go. I'll finish securing the fire and follow when I can.”

No one asked her to burn the forest clear.

She had enough work preserving what remained alive.

***

The Vanguard stood beyond the turn with her weapon still raised.

She was young for a Rokur warrior. Battle scars crossed her traditional armor, and ash filled the seams. The light inside her burned low, barely enough to reach the edges of her shoulder plates.

She shifted the weapon from one hand to the other without lowering its point.

A tremor passed through her free arm.

Brunti could see how carefully she steadied her feet.

“Who comes?” the warrior asked.

“Relief party,” Varun called from farther up the route, where he had returned with the second escort. “Two young travelers and an injured woman.”

The Vanguard looked at the litter, then at the child.

“Keep the center of the stones. The ground beyond has been disturbed.”

“Have you seen anything?” Brunti asked.

“Not since the last watch. That doesn't mean there isn't anything.”

The light beneath the woman's armor strengthened for a brief moment, then settled back into a low glow.

Sira offered her a small packet of food and a cup of water from the clean store.

“Can someone take your post?”

“After the escort reaches the station,” she replied. “The next guard is coming from the other side.”

Brunti could have taken the weapon from her hands and stood there in her place. She knew what it was to hold a line, to keep a threat away through sheer strength.

But she did not know the ground, the watches or the people waiting on either side.

“Would you like me to hold the path while you sit?” she asked.

The Vanguard shook her head.

“Help them cross. That's what I need.”

Brunti nodded.

The group passed along the clear stones. As they went, Brunti looked back at the young warrior standing with the dim light of a tired hearth within her.

It seemed an especially quiet kind of courage to stay where another person's life depended upon an unglamorous watch.

***

Mist thickened near the last waystation.

A woman stepped out of it with an artifact gleaming in her open hand.

Her wild hair framed a composed face, and her worn, torn clothing carried patterns Brunti did not recognize. Several other objects glimmered faintly among the folds at her side. She seemed untroubled by the oppressive forest air.

“Travelers,” she said. “An uncommon hour for trading.”

“We're bringing people out,” Varun replied.

“Then you'll need things.”

She turned the offered object between her fingers. Its surface shone briefly with a power Brunti could feel at the edge of her mineral senses, though not enough to identify.

“Something to keep the darker paths from reaching you,” the woman said.

Sira looked at the object.

“Does it work?”

“It has worked for those who knew how to use it.”

“That isn't quite an answer.”

“It is the only one I'm selling.”

The woman named a price.

It was far more than the group could spare without taking food and supplies away from the people they were escorting.

Brunti considered the artifact.

Old instincts moved beneath the thought. Somewhere in the past, she would have wanted to examine it, determine its value, find out whether it might carry an answer to a question she had not asked properly.

Now the injured woman was resting on a litter behind her. A small child held a walking stick under Sira's hand. A boy waited safely at Elda's shelter. The night was growing colder.

“What else do you sell?” Brunti asked.

The mystic's expression sharpened.

“Other things of power.”

“Do you have dry cloth?”

The woman blinked.

“Cloth?”

“For a splint.”

“I have bindings,” she said at last. “Ordinary ones.”

“How much?”

The price she named was small.

Brunti paid it.

The mystic gave her a folded roll of clean material and watched with a faint, amused wonder as Sira passed it to the caretaker beside the litter.

“You came to the Shadowmarket Mystic for bandages?”

“We came to the road,” Brunti said. “You happened to be here.”

The woman laughed once.

“A distinction worth remembering.”

She stepped back into the mist, the bright artifact still held in her hand.

Brunti did not follow.

***

The return to Elda's shelter came with the first grey light of morning.

The roof was still leaking above the western wall. The carpenter was still working on the folding cot. A kettle had begun to warm near the hearth, and someone had hung clean blankets where the damp air could not reach them.

The small Verdani boy was sitting beneath the lantern. He looked up when the party entered, then returned to a tune he was humming.

The girl with the walking stick stopped beside him.

“Do you know that song?” she asked.

“A little.”

She sat on the bench a short distance away.

“Can you teach it to me?”

He thought for a while.

“Only the part I remember.”

“That's enough.”

Their voices rose together for a few notes, then separated into two different endings. Neither child seemed to mind.

The injured young woman was carried to a dry room where Elda's caretakers could examine her splint and help her rest. She asked for water and a place where she could see the door. Both were given.

Velen prepared the messages.

The child's family received a sealed account identifying her and naming the safe shelter where she could be reached. The other missing boy's circumstances were recorded separately, without turning him into the person Sira had come to find. The escapee's name and experience remained hers to share; the public route note recorded only that an injured traveler had been recovered.

By morning a courier had taken the messages toward the outer stations.

Brunti watched him go.

“Does this mean we're finished?” she asked Sira.

“The children are safe here,” Sira replied. “The family still has to come. The woman still needs treatment. These people will be working after we leave.”

“That's not quite what I asked.”

Sira looked at the two young Verdani beneath the lantern.

“For the search we promised? Yes.”

Brunti felt the answer settle slowly.

A missing person had been found without a prophecy, without a secret relic, without a battle that made the forest kneel. The people who lived at its edge had kept their lamps burning. They had written careful reports, mended the paths they could reach, and refused to call every frightening thing an enemy that must be destroyed.

The work was still unfinished.

It was enough.

Varun came from the gate with a map rolled beneath his arm.

“The southern road is open,” he said. “There's a cart leaving toward the Vale at midday. You can reach the next relay before night if you go with it.”

Velen checked the relay dates against the Skyland's expected gathering. A little more than a month remained. With the scheduled wagons and descent lifts, Brunti could reach home in time to stand beside Darrik when the work began. There was a place for her on this cart.

Velen looked at Brunti.

“We could leave.”

“We should,” Brunti replied, but the words came slowly.

“Yes,” she said.

Beyond the hedge, the forest stood dense and dark. Somewhere deep among the trunks, old roads bent toward places the world had never understood. Brunti had crossed enough of Morkahja to know that an unanswered place could tempt a person more than a finished task.

She folded Sira's family report and placed it among the papers bound for the safe roads, not among the old diagrams of stone and memory.

Then she walked to the shelter gate.

Outside, someone was repairing the hinges the hag had promised to send. A little patch of moss had grown beside the post, soft and bright beneath the early light.

Brunti stopped to hold the gate steady while the carpenter set the next pin.

The hammer sounded twice.

The gate swung freely.

Beyond it, the southern road waited.
