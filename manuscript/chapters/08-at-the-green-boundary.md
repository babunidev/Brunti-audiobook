# Chapter Eight — At the Green Boundary

At the edge of the oldest trees, the path ended without ending.

It was still there beneath Brunti's feet: a strip of bare earth worn by travelers, crossed now and then by roots and the small tracks of woodland creatures. But ahead, where the light turned green and the trunks stood close together, the path became difficult to distinguish from everything around it.

A few pale stones marked the last bend. Beyond them, ferns covered the ground without the breaks that ordinary footsteps should have left.

Brunti stood before the stones with the keeper's directions in her hand.

“This is not the public road,” Sira said.

“No.”

“We were meant to turn at the stream.”

“I know.”

“Then why are we here?”

Brunti folded the directions and put them away.

“Because the keeper said the old paths sometimes lead toward the Liani realm. They may have records older than anything carried by the trade routes.”

“May have,” Sira repeated.

“I only wanted to see the boundary.”

Sira looked at the tall trees.

Their leaves seemed to hold the morning light within them. Water ran somewhere farther in, but the familiar noises of the forest had changed. Brunti could hear birds behind them and the hum of insects among the ferns. Ahead, beneath the close branches, those sounds thinned into a patient quiet.

“You have seen it,” Sira said.

Brunti did not reply immediately.

She had imagined an old stone gateway or a carved marker: a place where a traveler might present a name, a letter, perhaps the reason for wanting passage. There was none. The wood simply became deeper and less willing to reveal a way through it.

A faint movement appeared between two trunks.

Brunti raised her head.

At first she saw only a darker patch among the shadows. Then two eyes caught the light, steady beneath a low branch.

A wolf stood there.

Its fur was so dark that the body seemed to continue into the shade around it. It made no sound. No leaves stirred under its paws. Only the eyes revealed where its head had turned, measuring the clearing and the two travelers at its edge.

Brunti's hand settled against the strap of her pack.

The wolf did not bare its teeth. It did not lower its head or prepare to leap. It watched.

Sira took a quiet step nearer to Brunti.

“Don't move toward it,” she whispered.

“I wasn't going to.”

“You were thinking about it.”

“I was thinking about whether it could hear us.”

The wolf's ears shifted once.

“That answers one question,” Sira said.

Brunti remembered the stories Mara had told about predators that survived because they let others come too close. Yet this creature held no posture of pursuit. Its stillness was stranger than aggression would have been.

She had heard the name Liani Shadowwolf from travelers on the forest roads. They spoke of a watcher who appeared only rarely, a keeper of knowledge who could not be made to offer counsel. Some considered an encounter with it a test. Others described nothing more than a dark wolf whose eyes seemed older than the forest itself.

Neither story told her what to do now.

Brunti waited.

A breeze passed through the canopy and moved the leaves above the animal. The wolf remained in its place.

“I have a question,” she said softly.

Sira turned toward her.

“For the wolf?”

“Perhaps.”

“What question?”

Brunti looked at the deepening path.

She wanted to ask whether memory could be preserved without becoming a thing locked away. Whether a lost name might be found in an old archive or heard in the voice of someone who remembered it. Whether an animal that lived beyond ordinary roads could know anything at all about the craft of the moving Skylands.

Each question sounded less reasonable than the last.

The wolf's gaze did not change.

“It doesn't owe me an answer,” Brunti said.

“No.”

Brunti stepped back from the pale boundary stones.

The Shadowwolf stood a moment longer. Then a shifting leaf crossed its eyes, and when the leaf settled, the dark place between the trunks was empty.

There had been no footsteps.

Sira let out the breath she had been holding.

“Were you disappointed?” she asked.

“A little.”

“Because it left?”

“Because I thought I might learn something.”

“You did.”

Brunti looked at her.

Sira bent to adjust the fastening on her traveling bag.

“You learned that a creature can watch you without inviting you farther in.”

Brunti studied the empty space beneath the branches.

That, at least, was true.

***

They returned to the stream but did not immediately find the public road.

The path widened through a grove of tall trees, then narrowed beside a bank of mossy stone. At its far end a pair of old trunks stood close together. Between them lay a strip of pale grass leading into deeper woodland.

The light in the gap was green.

Brunti was about to take a step toward it when Sira caught her sleeve.

“Wait.”

Something moved between the trunks.

A second wolf emerged from the shade, and this one did not resemble the first.

Its coat shone with soft emerald light, each movement sending small changes of color through the fur along its shoulders. The creature was tall and steady, its jade eyes passing over the woodland boundary before settling on the travelers.

It stepped across the opening.

Grass bent beneath its paws. The branches above swayed without touching the luminous coat.

Brunti stopped.

The animal did not growl, but it had placed its body precisely where the narrow path entered the hidden wood.

“Another watcher,” Brunti murmured.

“A Guardian,” Sira said.

“Are you certain?”

“It stands like one.”

The wolf looked at a single leaf drifting through the space before its muzzle. The leaf turned slowly in the green light and settled among the grass.

Then the Guardian raised its gaze again.

Brunti felt a strong urge to explain herself.

She could tell the animal about the memorial stones, the second note, and the old drawing she hoped to find. She could say that she had not come for treasure or power. She only wanted to know how to repair something without losing what had made it precious.

But there was no sign that the wolf understood her words, or that understanding would entitle her to pass.

Sira lowered her hands to her sides, palms open.

“We are travelers,” she said quietly. “We have followed a path that may not be ours.”

Brunti glanced at her.

Sira's voice had the careful shape she used when carrying a message from someone absent. She did not demand an answer.

The Guardian remained across the entrance.

Its luminous coat made the nearby leaves seem darker. A little wind moved through the clearing, bearing the scent of fern and wet wood. The wolf's gaze rested upon Brunti and then returned to the path behind her.

Brunti looked back.

The public road lay somewhere beyond the stream. The keeper had already told her where to find people who preserved old craft records. Her detour into this hidden part of the forest had brought her before a boundary she had no reason to cross.

She unhooked her hand from the strap of her pack.

“We can go,” she told Sira.

The Guardian did not follow when they turned away.

Only after the trees had hidden the green opening did Brunti realize how carefully she had been listening for a call behind them.

There was none.

***

They met the hunter at a shelter beside a fallen tree.

He had laid his bow across his knees and was examining a small tear in the leather of his quiver. Beside him sat a basket of gathered roots and a narrow flask of water. When he saw the two travelers coming along the mossy track, he moved the basket out of their way.

“From the inner paths?” he asked.

“Only their edge,” Sira replied.

He looked at Brunti's hammer, then at the direction from which they had come.

“Did you see anything?”

“A dark wolf,” Brunti said. “And another with a green coat that stood across the path.”

The man lowered his quiver.

“Two different animals, then.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

Brunti frowned. “Why good?”

“Because people often hear a name and decide every animal that bears the same color must be the same creature.”

He introduced himself as Halven. He hunted only along the outer paths, where the forest communities permitted it, and spent much of his time watching animals rather than taking them.

Sira offered a little food in exchange for directions. Halven accepted a small portion and indicated a flat stone near the shelter.

“The road is not far,” he said. “But I would avoid the lower track until the sun is high. A branch came down there after the rain.”

Brunti sat beside the fallen trunk.

“Do you know the Liani?”

“Some of their outer trails. Not their private ways.”

“Have they old records?”

“Perhaps. But if your work concerns the Skylands, why would you ask a wolf for them?”

Brunti thought about the question.

“Because old things are sometimes kept in places where other people no longer know to look.”

Halven nodded once.

“That can be true. It can also lead a traveler through a den while the owner is asleep.”

Sira smiled faintly.

Brunti did not.

The hunter took a narrow strip of wood from his pouch. He used it to hold open the torn seam of his quiver while he worked.

“There's another wolf in these woods,” he said. “A Predator. Green-furred, but not like the Guardian you describe. I saw it once among low leaves near a clearing. At first there were only two emerald eyes. Then a shoulder shifted, and I realized the animal's back had been lying against the green of the forest all along.”

He paused to pull the thread through the leather.

“It moved so low that the branches hardly stirred. One paw lifted at the edge of the open ground, then it stopped again. I couldn't see what it was watching.”

“Did it attack?” Brunti asked.

“No. I was not its concern.”

“Could it have been the Guardian?”

“Not the one I have seen. The Guardian stands in the open, if it wishes to be seen. This one was hunting.”

Halven tightened his knot.

“Do not make the mistake of calling every quiet animal wise. Some are quiet because they are hungry.”

Brunti looked down at the marks of wear on her own tool belt.

She remembered the Silvermane among the moonlit trees and the mother wolf standing before her cubs. Each had been dangerous. Neither had needed to become an enemy. She wondered whether the difference between danger and hostility was as difficult for a hunter to measure as it was for a warrior.

“Have you ever wanted to follow one?” she asked.

“The Predator?”

“Any of them.”

Halven considered.

“Of course. Once I spent half a day tracking a creature because its footprints were unlike anything I had seen. I forgot to notice where my own footprints were going.”

“What happened?”

“I found the animal.”

“And?”

“It was resting beneath a tree. I left it there.”

Brunti waited.

Halven smiled.

“By then I was lost.”

Sira laughed softly.

“Did someone find you?” she asked.

“Yes. A woman carrying herbs. She asked whether the roots I had gathered were worth the trouble.”

“Had you gathered any?”

“Not one.”

Brunti felt herself smiling.

The hunter tied the quiver closed and showed them the safer return path. It followed a raised bank around the fallen tree, then joined the outer road near a small market shelter.

Before they parted, he pointed toward the high branches.

“Watch the light when evening comes. Not everything that flies above these trees is gentle.”

***

They did not see the Thunderfly Matriarch until dusk.

By then the sun had sunk behind the western trees and the forest had begun to fill with long blue shadows. Brunti and Sira were climbing the raised bank Halven had described. The path was firm beneath their feet, and through the thinning branches they could see a pale line of open sky.

A low buzzing sound moved over the canopy.

Brunti stopped.

It was deeper than the call of an insect and steadier than the wind. Leaves trembled high above them. A few small birds rose from the branches and vanished toward the outer wood.

“Do you hear that?” Sira asked.

Brunti nodded.

The sound thickened into a rolling hum that seemed to press against the ground. She felt the vibration in her mineral seams, not as a familiar structural note but as a shifting pulse that followed something moving through the air.

A flash of sapphire crossed between the crowns.

Then the creature rose.

The Thunderfly Matriarch was immense. Sapphire and silver light slid across the plates of her armored body, and her vast wings beat the dim air with a force that stirred the leaves far below. Luminous chitin cast brief patterns over the treetops. Many-faceted eyes turned toward the ground, reflecting the fading sky in a hundred tiny surfaces.

Brunti had never seen anything quite like her.

The wings moved so rapidly that their edges became a continuous shimmer. Between their beats, the Matriarch's antennae quivered. A faint charge crackled along them, bright enough to show for a moment against the dark leaves, then vanished into the next sweep.

Sira caught Brunti by the arm.

“Come away from the open bank.”

Brunti did not move.

The Matriarch was holding above the trees, adjusting her position in small, deliberate movements. Each correction sent another tremor through the canopy. Brunti wanted to know how a body so large could remain suspended without falling. She imagined the pressure under the wings, the strength of the chitin, the pattern of the charge along the antennae.

“Brunti.”

“I see her.”

“Then see where we are.”

Brunti looked down.

The raised bank lay beneath a gap in the branches. If the Matriarch descended toward the clearing, the two travelers would be standing in the open beneath her. The charge on her antennae was not a sign Brunti understood, and the air around the creature carried a restless pressure she could not measure.

She stepped back into the shelter of the trees.

Sira followed, keeping close to the trunk of an old oak.

Above them the Matriarch turned. For a moment one wing caught the last sunlight, and silver light passed across its surface like water over polished metal.

Brunti watched until the creature moved beyond the canopy and the buzzing softened in the distance.

The leaves continued trembling for several breaths after she was gone.

“Magnificent,” Brunti whispered.

“Yes,” Sira said.

“I could have watched her for hours.”

“I know.”

“Perhaps she would have come lower.”

“That is what concerned me.”

Brunti looked toward the place where the Matriarch had vanished.

“She might have been harmless.”

“She might.”

“Or dangerous.”

“Yes.”

Brunti sighed. “You sound like Darrik.”

Sira turned toward her with an expression of great seriousness.

“Does that mean I am becoming wise?”

“It means you have learned to say a great many things without promising anything.”

“Then I am learning from you as well.”

Brunti tried not to laugh. She failed.

***

They reached the public road after nightfall.

It was a relief to find a path whose purpose was plain. The earth had been packed hard by carts and feet. Low stones marked the edges, and a small wayhouse stood beyond a bridge where lanterns hung from iron hooks.

Brunti's body had begun to feel the strain of the day's walking. The fine mineral seams around her knees held a dull ache, and she was grateful when Sira suggested they rest before continuing.

At the wayhouse they found a table near a low window.

The keeper served Sira warm food and brought Brunti a bowl of roots with ground minerals stirred through it. Brunti ate slowly, listening to the sounds of the room: a cart driver telling a story at the next table, someone sharpening a tool by the hearth, the faint rustle of papers as a traveler checked the contents of a satchel.

Sira unfolded one of her messages.

“Will we reach Aetherion by this road?” Brunti asked.

“Eventually. We still have to cross the trading paths.”

“And the records?”

“There are keepers of books along the way. The forest keeper gave us one name.”

Brunti took out the copied ledger page from her home.

The old mark of a joint between two stones remained as uncertain as ever. She placed it beside her notes on the northern Grimnir trunk and vault, the Verdani casket, the carved meadow chest, and the two wolves at the Liani boundary.

There were many drawings now. None had become the answer she sought.

Sira glanced at the page.

“You are making the same face you made at the green path,” she said.

“What face?”

“The one that says a door ought to open because you have found it.”

Brunti looked down.

She had indeed been wondering whether she should have asked the Guardian for passage a second time. Perhaps there was a phrase she might have spoken, a way of showing respect that would have changed its mind. Perhaps she should have stayed until the Shadowwolf returned.

The thought was pleasant because it promised another chance at certainty.

It was also useless.

“We were not invited,” she said.

“No.”

“And we had a road already.”

“Yes.”

Brunti folded the ledger page and put it away.

The table beside them grew louder for a moment as the cart driver reached the end of his story. A traveler in a weatherworn coat was arguing that a merchant from Celesterra had once sold him an object whose maker could not possibly have been mortal.

The other diners laughed.

Brunti lifted her head.

“Celesterra?” she asked.

The traveler turned, pleased to have another listener.

“That's what the fellow called it. A city beneath the earth, full of air and old secrets. He had a stall where the passage met the trading hall.”

Brunti looked at Sira.

Sira raised one eyebrow.

“You can hear the story,” she said. “Just remember where you heard it.”

Brunti opened a clean page in her notebook.

The road through the forest had led her back among people. Perhaps an archive could tell her more than a boundary that had never promised to speak.

Outside, the trees stood dark beneath the stars. Somewhere beyond them the Liani kept their hidden paths, and the Thunderfly Matriarch moved through a sky Brunti could no longer see.

She did not go back.

She listened to the traveler.
