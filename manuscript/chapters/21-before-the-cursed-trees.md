# Chapter Twenty-One — Before the Cursed Trees

The winter did not end at the last mountain.

For several days after leaving Everwinter, Brunti carried its cold in the fine seams of her hands. The mineral binding around her wrist had grown smooth again, but she could still remember the careful way the stag had lowered itself behind the stones, and the trembling of the blue arrow it carried.

Below Frostwind, the snow thinned into narrow stripes along the roads. Trees appeared singly at first, then in quiet clusters. Water ran again beneath the bridges. A roadside market was selling fresh greens brought up from the warmer lowland roads, although frost still lay in the shaded corners of its stalls.

Enna accompanied them as far as the southern junction where the winter patrols ended. There she returned the cord she had carried through the mountains to its proper place in her pack.

“I'd rather know you reached the crossing,” she said, “than hear someone tried to walk out by following a drawing of a straight line.”

Velen folded the map.

“The road isn't straight.”

“Then you've learned something.”

Brunti clasped the guide's forearm.

“You kept us from getting lost.”

“Not always.”

“You brought us back when we were.”

Enna seemed content with that distinction.

She turned northward the following morning. Brunti and Velen joined a southbound wagon that carried winter wool, carved tools and sacks of dried grain toward the Vale. From there they would need other roads, and perhaps another guide, before they could reach the western relief stations named in Sira's letter.

Brunti kept the letter near the top of her pack, separate from the papers she intended to carry home.

At each station Velen asked for recent reports from the roads bordering Keldor. Some messengers had heard of missing travelers. Others had not. One remembered a child walking beside a wagon but could not say whether the child was Verdani. Another described a walking stick taller than its owner and then admitted the report had come through two other people.

Brunti asked what each witness had actually seen.

The answers grew smaller.

Sometimes smaller was more useful.

***

The journey southward became a succession of ordinary things. Brunti took the last position when they walked beside the wagon, watching for a child or tired traveler who might fall behind. It was work she knew from the Skyland watch; it had no glory and very little room for error.

A broken harness needed mending before the cart could leave a village. At a riverside station the bridge keeper would not allow another wagon onto the timbers until his apprentice replaced a worn crossbeam. A child in a market square asked Brunti whether her stone skin could hold a flower without crushing it. She opened her palm. The flower remained intact.

Velen worked beside her whenever the roads allowed it, checking the dates written on Sira's message against the times a courier could reasonably have crossed the stations.

“The first sighting is older than the letter,” they said one evening.

“I know.”

“The later sighting near Keldor is older too.”

“I know.”

“It may already have become irrelevant.”

Brunti stirred the prepared mineral mixture in her water.

“That is why we're going to ask again.”

Velen marked another interval in their notebook.

“Darrik may be repairing the wall while we do this.”

“Yes.”

“Does that trouble you?”

Brunti turned her repaired wrist. Its seam gave a faint, healthy hum.

“I wanted to be there.”

“I know.”

“And it may be better that he doesn't need me to tell him what is safe.”

The archivist looked at her for a long moment.

“That is different from what you said when we left.”

“Yes,” Brunti replied.

She could have added many things. Instead she rinsed her cup and set it beside the warm stone.

The cart traveled through the Vale's broad roads for another stretch of days, carrying news and goods between places with no reason to share their troubles. The grass became greener. The air lost the sharp bite of the mountains.

And at last, near the western approach to Keldor, the distance between the trees began to close.

***

Sira was standing beside a wagon when Brunti saw her.

There was no song to announce her. She was arguing with a merchant over the number of blankets remaining in a bundle.

“You wrote twelve,” Sira said.

“I packed twelve.”

“Then one of them has learned to walk.”

The merchant opened the bundle and counted again. There were eleven.

Sira shook her head, but her expression softened.

“Another can be fetched tomorrow. I need the eleven you've actually brought.”

Brunti waited beside the road.

She had imagined finding Sira a hundred ways while traveling through Frostwind. In most of them Sira would turn at once, smiling, and all the strange unfinished hours between them would vanish.

Instead Sira finished counting, tied the bundle, thanked the merchant, and looked up.

For a moment she did not move.

Then she crossed the road and took Brunti's hands.

“You're cold,” Sira said.

“I was colder.”

“You're very difficult to keep warm.”

“I've been told.”

Sira's fingers closed around Brunti's mineral knuckles, and the water beneath her own skin caught the afternoon light in faint ripples. She held the hands a moment longer than a greeting required.

Velen approached carrying their papers.

Sira turned toward them.

“You've kept her from breaking anything important?”

“Only several opinions,” Velen said.

“That sounds exhausting.”

“Deeply.”

They embraced with less surprise than Brunti had expected. Somewhere between Aetherion and the mountain, the two had become comfortable enough to exchange a small joke without explaining it.

For a little while there were no missing names, no cursed roads, no urgent questions. There was only the three of them beside a wagon with eleven blankets, and a warm breeze moving through the grass.

Then Sira's smile faded.

“Come with me,” she said. “The witnesses are here.”

***

The relief station occupied the courtyard of an old roadside building where three routes met. One road ran east toward the settlements of Ethera's Vale. Another followed the safer southern trade line. The third approached Keldor's wooded boundary, though the station's map left much of that road unfinished.

Inside, tables stood beneath a patched canvas roof. Bundles of preserved food were stacked near a water jar, and names had been written on boards according to the shelters to which people had traveled.

Sira introduced Brunti to a keeper named Mera, a Verdani woman whose translucent hands carried faint green ripples under the skin. She had been coordinating messages between displaced families and the neighboring posts.

Mera spread three reports on the table.

“This is the first,” she said.

The page described a young Verdani traveling with a relief group after the raids. The child had been small enough to slip between the adults at busy crossings, and had carried a walking stick whose knots were set for a much taller hand. The last confirmed sighting placed the child near a change of wagons on the western road.

The next page had been written at another station. It described someone carrying a long stick near the northern turn of a timber track. The writer had not seen the person's face.

The third page was more recent. It reported a small figure near the outer trees of Keldor, walking away from the public road.

“The third witness is here?” Brunti asked.

“Not now,” Mera replied. “They passed through two days ago, going south. The person who took their statement is here.”

“Was the figure wearing the same clothes?”

“We don't know.”

“Did anyone see its horns?”

“No.”

“Did anyone speak to it?”

“No.”

Brunti let the silence hold.

Sira unfolded another sheet.

“There's a caravan carrying spare supplies to the northern road tomorrow. Their driver knows the outer paths. I want to ask what can still be checked without sending anyone into the forest alone.”

“Have you told the child's family about the report?” Velen asked.

“We told them a sighting had been reported. We did not tell them it was their child.”

The care in Sira's answer was familiar. She had not crossed half the world to become someone who turned every uncertainty into comfort.

“Do we know the child's name?” Brunti asked.

Mera nodded and touched the first page.

“We know it from the family. But the later witnesses never heard a name, and I won't give it to everyone who arrives with a story. The family will be asked before it's used beyond the search group.”

Brunti understood. A missing person was not a puzzle that belonged to whoever volunteered to solve it.

“Then give me the places and the dates,” she said.

Mera drew a simple route on a sheet of scrap parchment, marking only the paths that living witnesses had named.

Brunti looked at Sira.

“You were already doing this.”

“Yes.”

“Without me.”

“Yes,” Sira replied.

“And you asked because—”

“Because another pair of eyes can help. Another pair of strong hands, too. The people here have other families to look after.”

Brunti nodded.

“I can help with the outer roads.”

“Not the Blackwood.”

“No,” Brunti said. “Not the Blackwood.”

Velen wrote the limit beside the route.

***

They spent the next morning checking the station's reports before setting out.

The person who had taken the third witness's statement was an older road clerk. He remembered the traveler who described the small figure but had not seen the figure himself.

“Did the witness say the stick was knotted?” Velen asked.

“Long and crooked,” the clerk said. “That might mean knotted.”

“Did they call the figure Verdani?”

“I wrote that the witness thought so.”

“Was that because of its appearance?”

“Perhaps.”

The clerk looked uncomfortable.

Brunti waited.

Finally he set down his pen.

“They were passing at dusk. The light wasn't good. They said someone small went between the trees. There was a stick. Beyond that, I've told you all they told me.”

“Thank you,” Brunti said.

He looked surprised.

“Most people ask me to remember something else.”

“Then they need another witness.”

On the way out, Sira brushed her fingers over the rough wall beside the door.

“You're becoming very patient with people who cannot answer your question.”

“I'm not patient,” Brunti said. “I just stopped mistaking a longer question for a better answer.”

The relief station had one more useful piece of information. A forester who guided wagons along the outer Keldor tracks was due that afternoon. He could take them as far as the boundary and back.

He came with a pair of pack animals and an old wooden staff worn smooth at the place where his hand rested. His name was Varun. He knew which crossings remained used by traders and which had grown quiet after the forest's changes.

“I won't take you into the deep woods,” he said.

“Good,” Brunti replied.

Varun looked at her hammer, then at Velen's thick packet of notes.

“People usually argue.”

“We've done enough of that already,” Velen said.

Sira smiled.

They left the station before noon.

***

The road changed before the forest did.

Grass grew high along the margins, but the usual movement among it had become harder to find. In the Vale, insects had risen wherever Brunti walked, and small creatures left narrow paths between the roots. Here, beneath the last open stands of trees, she heard fewer wings.

A silver shape flashed between the trunks.

Sira stopped.

The animal appeared only a heartbeat later, a slender wolf with a pale coat that caught the scattered sunlight beneath the canopy. Her muscles moved smoothly under the silver fur as she crossed a patch of soft earth, placing each paw with care.

Her breathing was quick but even.

She paused beside a fallen leaf. It had caught briefly against her flank. The wolf waited just long enough for it to slip away.

Brunti held still.

The little creatures under the roots were silent. No rustling came from the brush beyond the wolf.

“She sees something,” Sira whispered.

“Something ahead of her,” Brunti said.

Varun had stopped behind a low stone, keeping the pack animals from moving forward.

“Silver Wolf,” he murmured. “Let her choose her road.”

The wolf lowered herself toward the ground. Muscles gathered along her shoulders and back. Her gaze remained fixed on a narrow opening among the trees.

Then she sprang.

In a single fluid movement she cleared a tangle of exposed roots and swept into the open beyond them. Grass rose behind her paws, silver flashing between dark trunks before she vanished into the undergrowth.

Brunti waited until the grass settled.

“Did she hunt?” Velen asked.

“Perhaps,” Varun answered. “I didn't see what she followed.”

Velen wrote down the direction of the leap, not a prey animal that might never have existed there.

Brunti looked at the quiet soil.

She remembered the red wolf Embersnout, moving low among snow-covered rocks. There had been no reason to make its choice into a message. Here the same was true.

The silver wolf's path told them where a wolf had gone.

It told them nothing of a child.

***

The outer road curved toward a cluster of ancient trees, then climbed to a low open rise. Varun chose a sheltered place to wait for evening before continuing. The pack animals needed rest, and the next safe marker lay beyond a stretch of ground that could be difficult to judge in changing light.

Sira helped tie the animals beneath a windbreak. Velen checked the road notes again. Brunti walked to the edge of the rise and looked over the border country.

The Vale's meadows spread pale and green behind them. Ahead, the trees grew closer together. A soft evening mist gathered among their lower branches.

The border did not appear as a line.

It was a change in the sound of the world.

At dusk the last birdsong faded. The canopy took on a silver light, and the scent of damp moss drifted up from the wooded slope.

A low growl came through the trees.

Varun raised one hand.

On a moss-covered hillock beyond the road, a powerful wolf stood above the shadowed woodland. Moonlight glistened along its glossy coat. The animal held a broad, commanding posture, almost perfectly still.

“Shena Alpha,” Varun whispered.

The wolf's paws had pressed deep impressions into the soft moss. One ear turned toward a faint rustling among the trunks, while its eyes continued searching the clearing below.

Another howl rose far off through the forest.

The Alpha lifted its head and listened.

Brunti stood beside Velen. Sira had come quietly from the animals and was watching from the shelter of a fallen tree.

The wolf did not answer the distant call.

For several moments it held its place, gaze moving slowly between dark trunks. A soft wind passed through scattered leaves on the hillock. The animal lowered its muzzle, and the quiet growl returned, barely louder than the stir of the branches.

Velen's pen made a small scratching sound.

The Alpha's ear turned.

All three travelers stopped moving.

Its gaze remained on the clearing. It neither charged nor withdrew.

Varun gestured for them to step back along the sheltered edge of the road.

They obeyed.

When the hillock vanished behind the trees, Sira released a breath.

“That was another wolf.”

“Yes,” Brunti said.

“Not the silver one.”

“No.”

“And neither of them was chasing the person we're looking for.”

“We have no reason to think so.”

Velen closed their notebook.

“Two wolves,” they said. “Two observations.”

Brunti glanced at them.

“That sounds familiar.”

“I've been listening.”

***

They returned to the small road camp as darkness settled, then stayed until the morning light made the crossing plain.

Varun pointed toward a narrower route running westward through an older stand of trees.

“That's the boundary track. It meets the approach to Darkwood Thicket. Some traders use it when the main road floods, but I won't send a cart down there.”

Sira examined the map.

“Is it still part of Keldor?”

“People argue about where Keldor begins,” Varun said. “The trees are not interested.”

They left the pack animals with a station hand and followed the foot track only as far as a known return marker.

Twisted branches reached across the narrow space ahead. The trees stood close together, their older trunks furrowed and bent, and only a little daylight filtered through the tangled canopy. Beneath them the forest floor lay under a thick carpet of leaves.

The air grew green and dim.

Somewhere deep among the trunks, an unfamiliar creature gave a low, wavering call.

It faded.

Something resembling a whisper remained, moving softly from branch to branch.

Velen raised their head.

“Did anyone speak?”

“No,” Varun said.

“Are you certain?”

“I'm certain none of us did.”

A breeze stirred the old leaves. Beneath the roots, pale flowers glimmered in the shade, their small heads delicate among the dark earth. The oldest trees were twisted into remarkable shapes, but their bark still held patterns that looked almost carefully carved.

Brunti touched the outer surface of one trunk.

It was cold and rough. The grooves bent in a pattern that suggested a line of figures walking under a canopy. She changed position. The figures vanished into ordinary folds.

The whisper came again.

It carried no words she could distinguish.

“People say the fae once lived here,” Varun said. “And that they were banished.”

“Why?” Velen asked.

“Every telling has a different reason.”

Sira studied a cluster of flowers at the base of the tree.

“Does anyone know if there were fae at all?”

Varun shook his head.

“Not from anything I've seen.”

The path continued between the trunks, darker beyond the first turning. It would have been easy to believe a small person might have followed it. Equally easy to believe a frightened witness had seen only shadows and made a story of what could not be found afterward.

Brunti examined the ground near the known marker. Leaves covered most of it. A small tread had left an impression in damp soil, but the edges were too worn to tell when or by whom.

“There's a print,” she said.

Varun crouched.

“An old one.”

“Child-sized?” Sira asked.

“Could be a short adult. Could be someone stepping on their heel. I wouldn't call it evidence of your child.”

Sira looked disappointed.

Brunti felt the pull to say something reassuring.

She did not.

Instead she took a small stick and marked the edge of the print so that Varun could direct another searcher to it without trampling the soil. Velen sketched the shape and measured its position from the trail marker.

The whispering continued deeper in the woods.

Brunti turned back toward the open path.

“We can report what we found.”

“And what we didn't,” Velen added.

***

The first sign of the afflicted wolf was not its eyes.

It was the leaves.

They began to turn slowly over the ground where the wind had fallen still. One dry leaf lifted from the soil, followed by another. They rose only a little way, circling close to the roots.

Varun stopped beside an ancient oak.

“Keep your distance.”

Between the trees a tall wolf emerged.

Its once splendid silver coat hung in ragged, matted strands. The powerful body beneath it trembled with movements that did not resemble ordinary shivering. A violent spasm passed along its back. One foreleg stiffened against the earth, and its head jerked sideways.

Pale teeth showed.

Then its eyes brightened.

An eerie spectral light spread across the scarred muzzle and along the torn silver fur. The glow deepened with each labored breath, casting a ghostly brightness upon the creature without lighting the trees around it.

Brunti's hand moved toward her hammer.

She stopped herself.

The wolf stood in the open for an instant. The trembling seemed to subside. Around its paws the dead leaves rose higher, turning slowly in the still air.

A strained snarl escaped its throat.

Sira moved nearer to Brunti.

“Can we help it?”

“I don't know.”

Another spasm shook the animal. Its luminous eyes turned toward the clearing where the travelers stood.

Varun drew them behind a fallen trunk.

“Cursed Silvermane,” he whispered. “Not every silver wolf is that one. Don't call to it.”

Brunti remembered the Silver Wolf's swift clean leap across the border grass the previous day. This creature shared a color with it, perhaps an older name in some accounts, but what she saw now was suffering neither wolf had chosen.

Its movements were painfully disordered. It seemed ready to spring one moment and barely able to stand the next.

Velen held their notebook against their cloak.

“Does the curse make it violent?” they whispered.

“Sometimes it lashes out,” Varun said. “But I don't know what it sees.”

Brunti looked for a safer path away from the animal.

To the left stood a broken stone wall, old enough that moss had grown across its lower blocks. Beyond it the track curved back toward a clearing. They could withdraw without entering the wolf's direct line.

She touched Sira's arm and pointed.

The Silvermane's head jerked again.

For an instant Brunti imagined approaching, taking hold of the struggling creature and carrying it to someone who could mend its disorder. She was strong enough to lift it, perhaps. She could not tell what held its limbs in those spasms or whether force would hurt it more.

There were things that no courageous grasp could make safe.

They withdrew behind the wall.

From the other side came a low, strangled growl and the scrape of leaves circling in still air. Then the sound stopped.

The travelers waited a long while before returning to the safer track.

Sira had not spoken since they left the wolf behind.

Brunti glanced at her.

“You wanted to help.”

“Yes.”

“I did too.”

“I know.”

Sira looked down at her hands. Light rippled beneath her skin, restless and thin.

“We used to think the Vale roads were the dangerous ones, because so many people were moving on them after the raids,” she said. “Here there's suffering in the things that have never left home.”

Brunti watched the trees.

“That doesn't mean we know how to touch it.”

“No,” Sira said. “It doesn't.”

***

At the roadside shelter, Varun reported the Silvermane sighting to a pair of forest wardens. They noted its position and asked whether it had crossed the public track. The answer was no.

One of them had heard a separate account from a traveler who had gone deeper along a Keldor path.

Sira drew Brunti and Velen aside before the evening meal.

“I have another report,” she said.

“About the child?” Brunti asked.

“Not directly.”

She unfolded a paper marked with the name of the witness who had spoken to her two days earlier.

“The woman came out of the eastern trees before dusk. She had been trying to find a shorter route between two forest stations. She said she saw something she knew from older stories as a Liani.”

Velen looked up.

“The Shadowwolf?”

“Perhaps. But she said it did not behave like the one those tales describe.”

Sira read from the report.

Two pale cyan eyes had appeared between the trees. Beneath them a dark wolf moved so silently that its shape was difficult to separate from the gathering shadows. Its fur seemed to absorb the remaining daylight.

A sudden chill had passed between the trunks.

Far away, a wolf had howled.

The sound faded into a murmur like a voice struggling to form words.

“The traveler stopped,” Sira said. “So did the wolf. She could see the eyes, but the body seemed to dissolve into the gloom.”

Brunti listened carefully.

“Did it speak to her?”

“No.”

“Did it offer counsel?”

“She said old tales promised wisdom from the Shadowwolf, though never without a price. This creature had none of the patience she expected. A whisper came from the darkness, too broken to understand.”

Sira turned the page.

“The eyes narrowed. The howl returned, close enough to make her turn, though the wolf before her had not opened its jaws.”

Velen reached for the paper but paused.

“May I copy it?”

“Yes. The witness agreed to her description being used for route warnings. She asked that we keep her name with it.”

Velen wrote it carefully at the top of a fresh sheet.

Brunti looked toward the dark windows. The afflicted Silvermane in the woods had been a real creature, suffering in plain view. The Cursed Liani existed for her only through a witness's account, and even that witness had not been certain what she had seen.

Two different beings.

Two kinds of evidence.

Neither pointed toward the child.

“Did the witness find the next station?” Brunti asked.

“Yes. She followed the river path back instead of the short route.”

“Then she knew a way out.”

“She did.”

Sira folded the letter.

“That's the part I want the wardens to remember.”

***

By the second evening they had assembled a short list of places that could still be checked.

The relief group had changed wagons near the western crossing. A traveler had seen a small figure at a turn in the road. A second witness had described a stick but not a face. The outer wardens knew of a sheltered route that remained open, though it passed through country where missing travelers sometimes appeared days after anyone expected them.

Varun drew three marks on the map.

“Here,” he said, “is where the northern foot track divides. Here is the old market road. Here is the last forester's house before the trail grows unreliable.”

Sira leaned over the sheet.

“Which of those can a search group reach without crossing the Darkwood interior?”

“The first two. The house, if the weather holds and someone knows the approach.”

“And after that?” Brunti asked.

“After that you ask the people who live there. The forest does not become easier because a map runs out.”

Velen wrote the destinations separately.

Brunti looked toward the northern road. At home, Darrik would be preparing a safe transfer. Her letter might be somewhere on the southern routes, moving at the pace of carts and couriers. She had chosen not to delay him.

This work was different.

She could not save the child by insisting the trail ought to lead where the sketch showed. She could not rescue a stranger by declaring that a wolf's howl must be a sign.

But she could join the people already keeping watch, carry supplies, listen to anyone who had truly seen something and help bring a lost person back if they were found.

“How many days have the wardens planned for the first search?” she asked.

“Two, before they report back,” Varun said. “Longer only if they find a reason.”

“Then we start with two.”

Sira looked at her.

“And if the trail gives us nothing?”

“We come back and tell the truth.”

For a moment Sira said nothing. Her fingers rested on the edge of the map, where an unfinished line ended at a blank patch of parchment.

“You crossed a long way to come here,” she said.

“I did.”

“You could have gone home.”

“I still can.”

Sira smiled a little at that answer, though her eyes remained troubled.

Beyond the shelter, the forest gave a distant, wavering call. A wind moved among the old leaves, bringing the scent of damp bark and the first hint of night mist.

Brunti folded the map along its original crease. Sira kept one hand over the open supplies list until Varun found the lantern. In its light they counted the food and clean bindings once more before the search.
