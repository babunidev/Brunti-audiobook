# Chapter Thirteen — After the Raid

The water stopped at the village gate.

It ran beneath the caravan road in a narrow clay-lined channel, clear and quick as it came down from the higher oasis. Then it struck a fallen beam and spread across the dust, soaking the ground outside a row of broken houses. Beyond the beam, the channel lay empty.

Brunti stepped down from the cart.

The village stood among trees that had been coaxed into shade over generations. Their roots followed channels cut through pale stone and reddish earth. Sand rose in dry folds beyond the garden walls, and farther off the wind moved along the edges of the dunes. Here, where the water should have divided among orchards and homes, the smell of wet dust mingled with old smoke.

Two Verdani were dragging splintered timber away from a passage between houses. A charred beam gave way in their hands with a sharp crack. They stopped until the loose fragments had settled, then began lifting the smaller pieces out of the path.

At the center of the settlement an ancient tree spread a wide crown of living leaves.

Every building around it had been damaged.

The tree had not.

Sira was already walking toward the nearest worker when the caravan guide caught her sleeve.

“Careful,” the guide said. “The western walls are still moving.”

Sira nodded and went on, slower.

A Verdani woman with soot-darkened hands raised her head as they approached. She wore a sash knotted tightly at the waist, and her horns held a few surviving flowers browned at the edges.

“We have brought water, food and tools,” the guide told her. “These three asked to come.”

“It's the eastern wall we lost first,” the woman said. “Ogrus broke it. Gobbls came through behind them. The lower houses never had a chance to close their doors.”

The woman looked at the cart and then at Sira.

“Sira?”

Sira stopped.

“Alen.”

The woman set down the piece of timber she was holding. For a moment she seemed to have forgotten how to move. Then she crossed the path and took Sira in her arms.

Brunti waited beside the damaged channel. Velen stood a little behind her, their document case closed and held carefully against their coat.

“I heard about the raid,” Sira said.

Alen nodded against her shoulder.

“Then you've heard some of it.”

She drew back.

“Do you know whether they found Tavi?”

Sira's expression changed.

“No.”

Alen shut her eyes.

“Of course not. I keep asking everyone before I remember the messengers have only just begun reaching the road.”

“Who is Tavi?” Brunti asked.

“My sister's son,” Alen said. “He went east with the first carts. He was meant to meet his uncle at the next Oasis Heart. We have sent word, but the route was broken.”

Brunti looked toward the empty watercourse.

“Where are your channel workers?”

Alen glanced at the branch of clay pipe protruding beneath the fallen beam.

“Two are injured. The others are trying to keep the lower gardens alive. Why?”

“Because that beam is holding the water out of the village.”

“Yes,” Alen said. “We know.”

Brunti looked up sharply. It was not a rebuke in the woman's voice, only exhaustion.

“We came to help,” Sira said.

“Then come and see what needs doing.”

***

The first person they found was resting at the edge of the oasis.

Beneath an old tree whose roots climbed over the low bank, a Verdani man leaned heavily against the trunk. His skin was pale jade, almost the color of the lichen around him. One hand lay on a twisted root; the other trembled as he tried to raise himself. His eyes were half-open, their green light faint.

Birds sang in the branches above him. Ferns grew close to the damp soil at his feet, and a thin breeze moved through them. The little grove smelled of wet earth and new leaves, startlingly green against the dry world beyond its walls.

“That's Renn,” Alen said. “He was guiding water from the reserve channel all night.”

Renn opened his eyes.

“I can still work.”

“No one asked you to,” Alen replied.

“I heard her talking about the main line.”

Brunti knelt near him.

“I'm a Grimnir craftswoman. I could look at the joint where the beam fell.”

“The joint isn't the trouble,” Renn murmured.

He tried to straighten and failed. A shallow breath escaped him, and his head settled against the bark.

Sira brought water and held the vessel where he could reach it. Renn drank slowly, taking small rests between movements.

“What happened?” she asked.

“The eastern feeder is cracked. The lower terrace is still drinking, but too much is escaping into the sand. When the upper wall fell, the main channel lost its slope.”

Brunti felt the shape of the problem before she saw it: a buried line continuing past a break, a damaged wall holding part of the grade, water choosing the easiest way down.

“Then we shouldn't pull the beam out until we've given the water somewhere else to go.”

Renn opened his eyes farther.

“Exactly.”

“I'd like to see the layout,” Brunti said.

“You can borrow my plan. It's in the basket beside the well.”

Sira looked at Alen.

“Renn needs a quieter place.”

“I know,” Alen said. “We have been trying to move him without taking another pair of hands from the line.”

Brunti rose.

“Then let's do that first.”

She and Sira made a support from a sturdy door plank. Renn protested until he discovered that lying upon it required less effort than standing. Velen carried the basket of rolled plans while Alen led them to a shaded shelter beside the elder tree.

The shade was full of salvaged things.

Clay bowls, instruments, bundles of cloth, a child's painted cup, a box of dried seeds. Someone had arranged them in careful rows on low stones. They were not prizes taken from the ruins. They were possessions waiting for their owners to return.

Renn settled on the plank. The light along his arms had steadied a little by the time Sira returned with a second vessel of water.

Brunti unrolled his plan.

It showed four feeder channels, a small settling pool and a set of stone supports beneath the main route. She could see where the fallen beam lay across the line.

“Who knows the flow schedule?” she asked.

“Ask Alen,” Renn said. “She knows which gardens can wait.”

Brunti looked at him.

He gave the smallest possible smile.

“I only know the stones.”

***

They did not begin by lifting the beam.

Alen gathered those who could work beside the surviving elder tree. Some came carrying tools. Others came because their homes stood below the broken channel and they needed to know when water would return. A few had no strength for more than standing in the shade, but they listened.

Brunti spread the plan over a flat stone.

“We could move the broken timber,” she said, “but if it was holding any of the old wall in place, the bank may collapse when the water returns. We need to support the channel before we shift it.”

A small Verdani with a scarf tied around one horn pointed to the lower feeder.

“My mother's garden is there.”

“Then we'll try to keep it wet,” Brunti answered.

“Try?”

“Yes.”

A mutter passed through the group.

Alen stepped forward.

“No one can promise all four lines will run today,” she said. “We have enough water for two safely, perhaps three if the repairs hold. Tell me which homes need it first.”

People began speaking at once.

Brunti listened to names and locations she did not know. The lower beds carried medicinal plants. The eastern terrace had food trees whose roots reached deeper. The shaded pool near the elder tree was being used by those recovering from injury.

Velen copied the claims onto a scrap of paper, then looked up.

“This is not a list of priorities,” they said. “It is a list of needs.”

“Exactly,” Alen replied.

A child tugged at her sleeve.

“What about the little orchard?”

Alen knelt to hear him.

“Which one?”

“The one with the crooked trunk. My brother planted it.”

“We'll see what can be done.”

The child nodded, not satisfied but willing to wait.

Brunti studied the plan again. Her first idea would have given the whole settlement more water at once, but she could not tell who would lose flow if the bank failed. She asked Alen where the temporary overflow should go.

Alen chose an empty strip of soil between two damaged walls.

“That ground can flood,” she said. “Nothing living depends on it now.”

They began there.

***

The beam was heavier than it looked.

Its outer surface had been charred, but the inner wood remained strong enough to pull against the stones supporting the broken wall. Brunti fitted ropes around it while two Verdani cleared loose fragments from the path. Velen held the survey pole steady and called measurements from Renn's plan.

Sira worked farther down the channel.

She guided a thin stream of water into a temporary trench, keeping it from running straight into the sand. The water moved around her wrists in narrow ribbons, darkening the soil beside each new cut.

Brunti raised the beam by a fraction.

Something cracked behind it.

“Stop,” Alen called.

Brunti froze.

A line had opened along the wall's lower edge. Dust sifted out of the stones. If they pulled farther, the remaining support would go with the timber.

“Lower it,” Brunti said.

They let the beam settle.

The waiting workers exchanged tired looks.

Velen came closer.

“The bearing is on the left side,” they said. “The old plan shows two supports, but only one remains beneath it.”

Brunti examined the broken masonry.

She could place a metal wedge where the lost support had been. It would hold quickly. She knew how to make it fit.

Then she looked at the soil, wet around the base and loose beneath the stone.

“No,” she said.

Velen glanced at her.

“What?”

“I was about to brace the load against ground that isn't stable.”

They looked at the mud.

“Then we need a wider base.”

“Or a different path for the weight.”

Alen approached with a broad slab salvaged from a roof. It was too worn to use as roofing again, but its lower face was sound.

“Would this do?”

Brunti tested its edge.

“Yes. If we set it beneath the dry side, then raise the beam onto it, the wall won't have to bear the load.”

Alen looked at the waiting workers.

“Can we do that?”

“We can try,” Brunti said.

The slab took four people to move. Brunti set it carefully against the bank while Velen measured its angle and Sira kept the water flowing through the temporary trench. They lifted the beam slowly, settled it onto the new support, and waited while the cracked wall adjusted.

Nothing moved.

Then a narrow stream began passing under the raised timber.

A sound rose from those watching—not a cheer, not yet, but a single shared breath of relief.

Brunti stepped back.

“Now we can clear the smaller pieces.”

They worked until the sun lowered behind the oasis trees.

Water reached the first two channels. The third still leaked, and the fourth remained dry. No one declared the village restored.

But at the far end of the medicinal beds, a child ran along the channel shouting that the leaves were wet again.

***

Sira found the room where her friend had lived just before dusk.

It stood near the elder tree, half open to the air where an outer wall had fallen. A shelf remained fixed above a low bed. Several small instruments lay upon it, wrapped in cloth to protect them from dust.

Alen stood by the doorway.

“She kept saying she'd mend the roof before the hot season,” she said.

Sira entered slowly.

Brunti remained outside with Velen. Neither spoke while the two Verdani moved among the surviving things.

After a time Sira came to the doorway holding a small carved flute.

“She changed the endings of every song,” she said.

Alen nodded.

“Even the children's ones. Especially those.”

“Did she sing after the raid?”

“She sang while we were carrying people to the elder tree. Then she stopped.”

Sira looked down at the flute.

“She was one of the names Iren told me.”

“Yes.”

The simple confirmation seemed to take all the sound from the room.

Alen reached toward the flute, then lowered her hand.

“We haven't decided what to do with her things. Some belong to her family, and some she made for children who never collected them.”

Sira turned the little instrument between her fingers.

“Then I should leave it here.”

“You can hold it a while.”

Sira hesitated, then returned it to the shelf.

She did not ask to take it.

When she came outside she looked at Brunti.

“I think I need to stay until the waterwork is safe,” she said.

Brunti nodded.

“We're staying.”

“You've already given us a day.”

“Then tomorrow will be another.”

Sira's face tightened.

“That isn't a small thing for you.”

“No.”

For a moment Brunti thought of the Skyland's convergence, the old memorial still resting in its isolated wall, and the drawing folded in her pack. She could not make time stop by refusing to count it.

But she had chosen to come here.

There was no use pretending she had been forced.

***

The letters began arriving on the second morning.

A caravan from the western stations had brought word from people who had heard of the raid and wanted to know which families were safe. In return, Alen sent lists of needed supplies, names of those who had traveled onward, and messages from survivors who could not yet make the journey themselves.

Velen offered to help organize the exchange.

They carried their notebooks to a shaded table beneath the elder tree and asked Alen which information could be copied for public use and which should remain private.

“Names of the missing can be shared with the road keepers,” Alen said. “But do not copy the things people say when they are too tired to decide who should hear them.”

Velen nodded.

Brunti spent the morning fitting the third feeder's cracked clay lining. By midday the water ran more evenly, and she returned to the shade to find Velen listening to a courier who had walked from the western trade roads.

The courier was Rokur, with a dark travel coat and a small satchel of sealed messages. She had seen a wounded warrior on another road weeks earlier.

“Not here,” she said when Brunti looked up. “Far beyond this village. A battlefield after the fighting had stopped.”

Smoke still clung to the stones, she told them. A Rokur warrior lay among the scorched ground, propped upon one arm. His volcanic-dark surface was charred, and smoke rose from his limbs as though the fire inside him had burned too fiercely. His weapon lay a few steps away, the edge holding a faint ember beneath the soot.

“He lifted his head when I came near,” the courier said. “The light in his eyes was low, but he was aware.”

“Did he speak?” Velen asked.

“Not much. He pressed one hand to his side and asked for water and shelter from the heat. I called for the others from our cart.”

“What became of him?” Brunti asked.

“We carried him to a healer's station. I don't know whether he traveled on after that.”

The courier opened her satchel and placed a separate letter upon the table.

“This account is from someone else,” she said. “A messenger who found an Aevis beneath a broad tree on a northern route.”

Velen looked at the seal.

“May we read it?”

“It was sent to the station keeper, not to me. You may copy the public description.”

The letter described a male Aevis sitting folded against a trunk in the shade. The air around him had grown unnaturally still. Faint light clung to his hands and wrists, then disappeared as he struggled to hold his form. Birds sang above him while he sat with one hand against the ground and the other pressed near his chest.

“He had been using too much of his own current,” the writer believed, though she admitted she had not seen the work that exhausted him.

Velen marked the difference between observation and explanation.

Brunti watched them.

The two reports had arrived on the same day. Both spoke of exhaustion. Neither belonged to the raid that had brought Brunti to this village.

She was relieved to hear Velen say so aloud.

***

In the afternoon an older Verdani traveler arrived carrying a bundle of herbs and a folded strip of cloth.

She had come through a woodland clearing several days before, far from Evergrove. There she had found a young Verdani woman lying on moss beneath the trees.

“The green had faded from her hair,” the traveler said. “Her skin looked pale, as though the water within her could no longer find its proper color. She lifted one hand when I spoke, but it fell back before she could answer.”

The woman paused.

“There were crushed herbs in the air and little flowers drooping beside her. I thought the whole clearing had gone quiet to listen.”

Sira leaned forward.

“Was the water clean?”

“I could not tell. The spring ran below the moss, and the woman had been alone for some time. I stayed until a healer from the next village came.”

“Did she recover?”

“Not while I was there. The healer sent a message later that she was resting.”

Alen accepted the herbs and placed them with the medicine supplies.

“That is one life,” she said. “We have enough trouble keeping the ones here clear in our minds without turning every distant wound into ours.”

The traveler nodded.

“I brought the story because the same road keeper asked whether your settlement was affected by sickness. I wanted you to know I had not seen such illness here.”

Velen wrote down the distinction.

Brunti returned to the canal before the conversation was finished. She needed to reset a clay lining where a stone had slipped overnight.

As she worked, she thought of the woman on the moss, the Aevis beneath the tree, and the Rokur warrior among burnt stones.

Each had needed help.

The similarity did not make their suffering the same event.

***

Another message came at sundown.

It was carried by a young trader who had crossed the southern farms after a different fire. He spoke of a Rokur woman named Flameveil Emberheart, whom he had found sitting among ruined houses with embers still burning beneath the fallen roofs.

“She wore a blue dress,” he said. “Torn and blackened at the hem. Her arms and shoulders were marked by fresh wounds. She kept one hand against her side.”

“Was she alone?” Sira asked.

“When I first saw her. Others came later from the road.”

The trader looked toward the surviving elder tree.

“Her eyes were on what remained of the home beside her. She didn't ask me to tell the story of the fire. She asked where the nearest place was that still had water.”

Brunti felt a small ache behind the mineral seams of her chest.

The trader described the smoke between broken walls, the bitter scent of charred harvests, and the low orange light where timber still smoldered. He had helped guide the woman away from the heat and could say no more about her fate.

Velen asked the place and date before writing.

Brunti watched the young trader fold his hands.

The details of Flameveil's blue dress remained in her mind. They would have been easy to forget if the story had been only a report of another burned village.

Later that evening, an Aevis messenger delivered a different account.

She had passed through a quiet woodland beneath an old canopy, where sunlight fell in one narrow beam among the roots. There an Aevis woman had been resting after a struggle, dust on her garments and marks along her shoulders. Light trembled at her fingers but would not hold.

“She tried to raise her hand,” the messenger said. “The threads gathered, then dissolved before they reached her palm. She watched the sunlight while I fetched help.”

“Was it the same Aevis in the letter?” Brunti asked.

“No. The first was a man, and the place was different. This woman had been on the southern woodland road.”

Brunti nodded.

She would remember that correction.

The messenger did not name a common cause. She knew only that the Aevis had been exhausted and that companions had taken her to shelter.

When the table was cleared, Velen set the five accounts in separate bundles.

“Different places,” they said. “Different witnesses. Different outcomes where they are known.”

Brunti glanced at the page containing her memorial sketch.

Once she might have placed them all beneath a single heading and tried to discover the flaw that connected them.

Now the separate bundles seemed more useful.

***

On the third evening, a Rokur visitor stood beside the village's broken eastern wall.

He had come with a caravan delivering new timber and clay. His garments carried a dusting of pale crystal that caught the fading sunlight. He was quiet during the unloading, but when the work was done he sat beneath the elder tree with the other travelers.

Someone asked whether he would sing.

He shook his head.

“There is a warrior's saga I remember,” he said, “but I met a man once who could not finish it.”

The listeners settled.

The Rokur told them of a soldier standing among ruins far to the north, where volcanic-dark material had gathered frost and ice crystal along old scars. The fire in his eyes burned low. He began a familiar warrior cadence, the kind that should have been answered by a Battledance.

No one answered.

The soldier's voice faltered. He lowered his gaze to the broken stones. One hand lifted as though marking the next movement of the dance, then remained suspended there while light traveled faintly across the crystal at his wrist.

“Did he die?” a child asked.

“No,” the Rokur replied. “He stood there when I left. I had offered to sit with him, and he said I might. We did not sing.”

The child considered.

“Then why tell it?”

The Rokur looked down at his hands.

“Because there are songs people cannot finish, and that does not mean they have forgotten the words.”

Sira sat very still.

Brunti thought of the little flute on her friend's shelf. She wondered how many endings a song could have before someone decided it was wrong.

Velen did not write immediately.

When the visitor had finished, they asked permission to record only the source of the account and the fact that it was incomplete.

The Rokur agreed.

***

There was another tale that night, told by a woman who had traveled through a forest called the Eternal Twilight Grove.

She spoke in a low voice, not to frighten the children but because the memory seemed to demand quiet.

“In that place the leaves shone without sunlight,” she said. “The sky stayed full of cold stars. I heard one spirit crying among the trees, then another, and another, until the voices overlapped so thickly that no single lament could be followed.”

A child moved closer to Alen.

The teller continued.

“Some of the spirits had no faces. One drifted through a trunk like mist, then gathered on its far side. The wind carried their cries close to the ground. For a moment they nearly held one note together.”

“Did they?” Velen asked.

“Not for long. They broke into separate voices again.”

“What happened after?”

“The leaves went on trembling. The grove remained where it was.”

Brunti looked toward the living elder tree above them.

The teller said there were traditions of calming the spirits through music or gentle magic. She had not tried it herself, and she could not say whether the grove's sorrow was caused by one ancient loss or many.

No one asked her to guess.

The children listened, then began speaking of the stars they had seen beyond the oasis walls. The tale slipped back into ordinary conversation.

Brunti was grateful for the change.

The grove belonged elsewhere. Its haunted twilight did not hang over their village, and its voices did not need to become an omen of what the survivors had endured.

***

The old Aevis woman arrived the following morning.

She came with a small supply cart and a companion who helped her down. Her form had grown quiet with age, its once-bright colors softened into pale silver and blue. She asked where she might sit out of the heat.

Alen led her into a shaded glade beside the surviving elder tree, where planted branches held a patch of cool grass. Through their leaves Brunti could see pieces of clear blue sky.

The woman settled beneath an old trunk.

A gentle tune came from her lips almost at once.

She hummed a few notes, paused to listen to the wind, then began again. Her hands rested in her lap, moving slightly as though keeping time for an instrument that was no longer there. When the breeze passed through the flowers, she turned her head and smiled.

Sira had been carrying water to the lower beds. She stopped at the edge of the glade.

The old woman looked up.

“Do you know this one?” she asked.

Sira listened to the melody.

“No.”

“Neither do I, entirely.”

Brunti sat on the grass a short way from them.

Velen remained near the tree, their notebook still closed.

The woman tried the next phrase. It rose lightly, followed the wind for a moment, then ended before reaching a resolution.

“I used to sing it with a friend,” she said. “It had a long ending, and I always hurried it.”

“Would you like us to listen?” Sira asked.

“Yes. But not to complete it.”

She hummed again.

A few notes found their way through the shade. The old woman's hands moved in her lap. The melody stopped, and the garden's ordinary sounds filled the pause: leaves, distant tools, water running along the repaired channel.

Sira began to sing a different little tune, one she had heard from a friend who changed the endings of songs.

The Aevis woman listened.

“That one is lovely,” she said when Sira finished.

“There were two endings,” Sira replied.

“Then sing the other.”

Sira did.

Brunti watched the old woman smile.

No one made the songs match.

***

By the fourth morning, the village had water in three channels.

The fourth remained dry.

Renn was able to sit beneath the elder tree and examine the repair plan, but Alen would not let him stand beside the damaged wall. The lower medicinal beds had recovered their flow, and the eastern food trees were receiving measured amounts. The little orchard with the crooked trunk still waited for water.

Brunti stood beside the unfinished fourth feeder.

The channel could be reopened by cutting through a damaged stretch of wall. It would take another day, perhaps two. The village had workers who understood its water system, but not all the tools they needed.

She looked toward Sira.

“You said we should stay until the work was safe.”

“Yes.”

“Three lines are safe. The fourth isn't.”

Sira was quiet for a moment.

“Alen has a plan for it. Renn has checked the new supports. The caravan is bringing another length of pipe tomorrow.”

“Then we could remain.”

“We could.”

Brunti looked toward the elder tree.

The village was not finished. Its houses were not all rebuilt. Its names were still being carried from one station to another. No departure would turn the work into something complete.

Sira came closer.

“I would like to stay longer,” she said. “Not because I think you must. Because there are people here I have not had time to speak with.”

Brunti nodded.

“And Velen?”

“Velen has already asked whether they may inspect the old canal records at the next Oasis Heart. The keeper said yes.”

Brunti smiled.

“Then we should divide the work.”

Sira looked at her.

Brunti continued.

“Velen and I can travel with the supply cart tomorrow, take the measurements and bring back the proper pipe if it is ready. You can stay with Alen and the others. We return here before the next caravan leaves.”

Sira considered.

“That would help.”

“Then let's do it.”

The plan took another hour to arrange. The guide agreed to carry Brunti and Velen to the next station if they helped unload supplies. Alen promised to keep the fourth feeder closed until the proper pieces returned. Renn marked the plan with the exact dimensions.

No one called it a grand solution.

It was a schedule.

***

That evening Sira walked with Alen to the eastern road.

A small group of survivors had gathered there beside a cart loaded for the neighboring oasis. They were not leaving because the village had been abandoned. Some needed treatment or relatives' help elsewhere. Others would return when the roofs were safe.

One woman carried a basket of young plants in wet cloth. A child sat on the cart's edge, holding a small wooden animal.

Alen spoke quietly with the travelers, checking names against the messages received that morning.

Then she turned to Sira.

“There is something else.”

Sira waited.

“An earlier group left before we could make the lists. They went by the southern caravan route, hoping to reach the safer settlements beyond the first Oasis Hearts. We have heard from a few of them.”

“Who traveled?”

Alen named two adults and a family whose home had burned. Then she mentioned a young traveler with a carved walking stick.

“The one who called it a sword?” Sira asked.

Alen almost smiled.

“Sometimes a sword. Sometimes a staff. The child said it made every road an adventure.”

Sira's face softened.

“I remember.”

“They were with the aunt's party when they left. We don't know whether they stayed together through the second crossing.”

“Has anyone reported them missing?”

“Not yet. Only that the caravan divided at a station where the main road was blocked.”

Sira nodded.

She asked for the names of the stations and who had last seen the group. Alen told her what was known and no more. Velen, called over from the supply cart, copied the route notes with their permission.

Brunti listened from a little distance.

There was a temptation to draw a line from the child's walking stick to every later danger the road might hold. She did not.

For now there had been a departure, a division of caravans and no confirmed loss.

Sira folded the list and put it with her messages.

“I'll ask at the stations,” she said.

“Thank you,” Alen replied.

The cart moved out along the road.

Dust rose behind it, pale against the darkening orchard walls. For a while the travelers could hear the wooden wheels striking uneven stones. Then even that sound faded into the evening.

***

At dawn Brunti and Velen joined the supply wagon.

Sira remained beneath the elder tree with Alen and the people still sorting messages. She had promised to meet them when the pipe was ready, and Brunti trusted her to keep that promise without needing to know every hour of her day.

Before leaving, Brunti walked to the repaired channel.

Water passed beneath the supported beam and into the first two feeders. The third ran steadily through its new lining. The fourth waited dry and open, marked with stones so no one would accidentally send water into the damaged ground.

Renn sat nearby, watching the flow.

“You left the old plan with Alen,” he said.

“It belongs here.”

“And the new measurements?”

“Copied for the next station. We'll bring them back with the pipe.”

Renn nodded.

Brunti looked toward the elder tree.

The shade beneath it was crowded with salvaged objects, food baskets, tools and people who were beginning to move with a little less urgency. Beyond the village walls the desert remained wide and dry. No gentle grove had appeared there by miracle. The water came because someone guided it, measured it, repaired its banks and cared for the roots it fed.

Sira stood among the workers, speaking with a child who had brought a piece of broken pottery to be mended.

Brunti raised her hand.

Sira answered.

Then Brunti climbed onto the wagon beside Velen.

The wheels moved over the road, past the empty fourth channel and the houses still open to the sky. Behind them the village continued its work.

It was not finished.

Neither were they.
