# Chapter Twenty-Seven — What the Throne Keeps

Beyond the arch, the procession disappeared.

Brunti had watched the first hooded figures pass into darkness with their extinguished lanterns held carefully before them. She crossed the threshold a few steps behind, expecting to see the same road and the same silent shoulders.

Instead, she stood alone.

The stone beneath her feet remained firm. It sloped gently into a hall so vast that the far end could not be distinguished from the darkness enclosing it. Faint ash-colored particles drifted through the motionless air. They glimmered and went dull, like embers that had forgotten their fire.

She turned.

Behind her, the great arch still opened toward the road. Its edges were visible, but there were no marchers within it. Neither the rustle of cloth nor the faint swing of an unlit lantern reached her.

Brunti stepped back toward the threshold.

The road was there.

It lay empty as far as she could see.

She remembered thousands of hooded figures moving in perfect unison. She remembered one with her own shoulders and her injured left hand, walking near the front.

There was no evidence of any of them now.

She held the arch with her sound hand and waited.

Nothing passed.

The silence seemed different from the Hollow Choir's. There, the missing sound had made room for voices that would not identify themselves. Here, even the place where a voice might have sounded felt occupied by something she could not see.

A faint pulse traveled through the floor.

Brunti felt it in the stone beneath her feet.

Then it went away.

She did not write it down.

She was tired of having to decide which parts of her own experience could survive the page.

***

The hall rose around her as her eyes adjusted.

Enormous arches curved overhead, fashioned from blackened rootwood or a material that resembled it so closely she could not distinguish the two. The arches vanished upward into a depth without a visible ceiling. Ancient pillars lined the central aisle. Each appeared to have been drawn out of living roots and twisted into a shape no builder would choose for an ordinary load-bearing column.

Between them moved faint clouds of ash.

No breeze disturbed the air. The particles drifted nevertheless, crossing the path in slow eddies before vanishing into shadow.

Brunti walked along the aisle.

Her footsteps returned faintly from the floor. The small sound pleased her for an instant, then frightened her because it seemed to come from a little behind her rather than beneath her body.

The nearest pillar was broader than the trees of her home Skyland. Narrow ridges ran vertically through its dark surface, meeting in curved seams that reminded her of bones set inside a joint.

She touched it.

The material gave a low response through her fingers.

Not exactly stone.

Not exactly wood.

It was hard, cool and scored with fine channels whose edges caught beneath her glove. She moved her hand slowly along one.

The channel branched.

Another joined it from the dark.

She had spent much of her journey reading marks left by tools. A worker's hesitation, a change in weight, the direction of a cut could reveal how a thing had once been made.

There were no toolmarks she could identify here.

The branching channels could have been grown, carved or worn away.

She could not tell.

She stepped back.

At the end of the central aisle, a dark shape stood above the floor.

A throne.

It was so large and so intricately joined to the architecture around it that Brunti needed several moments to understand where its base began. Intertwined roots rose from the floor and curved into a seat of petrified wood. The surface was cracked with age, crossed by dim crimson veins that pulsed slowly beneath the bark-like stone.

Something sat upon it.

Brunti stopped walking.

A single robed figure rested within the towering back of the throne, motionless beneath layers of dust and tangled roots. Its clothing hung in ragged folds, the color of dried blood and parchment left too long in the dark.

The robe covered most of its body.

Its hands were barely visible.

She could not tell where the figure ended and the throne began.

***

There were others in the hall.

Brunti had been looking so steadily toward the seat that she had not noticed the shapes gathered around it.

Stone figures knelt in immense circles.

The nearest ring began beside the central aisle and continued into shadow. Beyond it another ring spread around the throne, then another, until the floor seemed occupied by countless bodies lowered in the same act.

Some resembled humans.

Others had elongated limbs or skulls crowned with antlers. Several bore features that did not belong to any people Brunti knew. A broad kneeling figure near the aisle had a heavy mineral back and arms clasped before its chest.

She looked at it for a long time.

There were similarities to a Grimnir, but not enough to name it one.

The statues' faces were not identical.

One expression suggested reverence.

Another seemed fixed in terror.

A third held such deep sorrow that Brunti turned away, unable to decide whether the lines had been cut into the stone or whether the stone had once been capable of changing its own face.

She approached the nearest figure.

Its hands rested upon its knees. Fine dust lay in the grooves between its fingers. Nothing about the pose suggested recent movement.

Still, she remembered the stone figures half-submerged in the Weeping Mire.

There she had been tempted to decide they had once been alive because their shapes seemed too purposeful to be ordinary rock.

This hall gave her no additional proof.

She knelt beside the figure.

The material of its arm bore the pale line of a fracture filled with darker mineral. The repair, if it was a repair, had held under pressure for a long time.

Her craft instincts stirred.

She examined the join.

It was a small, lovely piece of work, the sort of patient correction no one would notice once the stone had settled back into its place.

She wanted to believe someone had cared enough to mend the figure.

Perhaps they had.

Perhaps the dark material had grown within the crack.

There was no way to know.

Brunti withdrew her hand without touching the damaged seam.

Above the kneeling circles, the throne's crimson veins brightened and dimmed.

Their rhythm was slower than her breath.

***

A deep vibration reached her from somewhere behind the seat.

Brunti lifted her head.

At first she thought the throne's back extended upward beyond the darkness. Then a different shape resolved behind it: an immense root rising toward the unseen ceiling.

It was larger than any castle tower she had known. Its surface crossed between the textures of bark and flesh, covered in channels that shone sickly green beneath a dark, strained skin.

A pulse moved through it.

The entire root seemed to tighten.

Then relax.

A faint tremor ran through the floor.

Brunti felt it in her left wrist.

She put her sound hand against the nearest pillar.

Another pulse came.

The channel beneath her palm answered at nearly the same instant.

She turned toward the other side of the aisle.

Fine lines in the adjacent pillars seemed to darken and brighten in sequence.

For one terrible moment the hall no longer looked built.

The pillars resembled bones.

The branching channels resembled veins.

The arches overhead curved like the inner frame of something that had grown so large that its own chambers had become a world.

Brunti pressed her back against the pillar.

The sensation arrived with the force of a realization rather than a thought: she was standing inside something living.

She could not have said what evidence established it.

She felt a pulse. She saw channels carrying faint light. She saw immense roots arranged like organs.

Her mind joined them.

She had joined signs before and been wrong.

The realization remained.

So did her uncertainty.

The great root contracted again.

This time she felt the vibration travel through her chest, beneath the mineral plates that protected the finer crystal at her center.

It was not like the familiar hum of a resting stone.

Resting stones soothed a Grimnir body by supporting its own rhythm. This pulse ignored her rhythm entirely. It passed through her as though she were another small piece of the hall's material.

She drew away from the pillar.

The vibration continued through her hand after contact ended.

A fine crack sounded within her wrist.

She clutched it against her chest.

***

The robed figure on the throne had not moved.

Brunti watched it from the aisle.

There had been a time when she would have approached such a figure with a question already prepared, as though the presence of something ancient proved it had answers for her.

She had imagined the Blackwood Abyss as a place where forgotten memory might be separated from false resemblance. She had hoped that someone or something deep inside it could tell her which rhythm belonged to which old stone.

Here, before the solitary keeper, she could not even confirm that a question would be heard.

The figure might be a guardian.

It might be a prisoner.

It might have been placed there long ago and left to decay.

The roots beneath its robe held it as completely as the throne held its seat.

Brunti remembered Darrik's hands at the memorial wall.

He had refused to let the damaged casing remain near an inhabited resting chamber, even though it meant moving the old stones away from the place where people had listened to them for generations.

At the time she had mistaken his caution for indifference.

Now she stood before a figure joined to a throne by roots that might have grown for centuries.

If this was a keeper, what did it keep?

If it was bound, who had decided that its work should never end?

She had no right to make either answer from a robe and a silence.

Another pulse passed through the hall.

The kneeling figures remained still.

She said, in a voice that sounded strangely small beneath the arches, “Can you hear me?”

Nothing answered.

The figure did not move.

Brunti waited until the green light faded.

“I came looking for a way to remember two people without making them one.”

The words traveled a short distance and seemed to be swallowed by the dark.

She thought of the separate rhythms in the old memorial casing, and the two places where Velen's drawing suggested stones might lie. Whoever had left those echoes belonged to a local history, worn and repaired by people with ordinary hands. She still had no names to place beside the rhythms.

What would this being know of them?

Even if it could hear, why should it possess the missing part of a craft record from a moving Skyland?

She had followed a resemblance into a forest and then into the place beneath it.

There was no chain of evidence connecting them.

She lowered her head.

“I shouldn't have come here for that,” she said.

The hall remained silent.

***

The great root contracted.

This pulse was stronger.

Brunti felt a hard pressure strike her through the floor. Her knees bent involuntarily. The mineral plates of her legs seemed to hold their weight a fraction too slowly, as though the signal that coordinated their movement had been delayed.

She reached for the pillar.

Her hand found only air.

The pillar was farther away than she remembered.

Brunti stumbled and caught herself against a shallow rise of stone at the aisle's edge.

For a moment the nearest kneeling figure appeared to turn its head toward her.

She looked again.

It had not moved.

Dust remained undisturbed across its shoulders.

A second tremor passed through the hall.

Something within Brunti's crystal wrist grated against itself. She pressed the injured joint into the shelter of her body and tried to steady the other hand.

The stone beneath her seemed to rise.

Not enough to lift her.

Enough to make distance and weight feel uncertain.

She looked toward the exit.

The great arch through which she had entered was no longer visible. The aisle stretched behind her through successive layers of shadow, each one framed by the same towering pillars.

She could try to follow it.

She should try.

Her knees would not hold their motion.

Brunti lowered herself to the stone and waited for the vibration to pass.

The pulse returned.

There was a faint sound then, somewhere close to her ear.

A voice.

She could not make out the words.

Perhaps the robed figure had spoken.

Perhaps the sound came from the many faces in the hall, or from the empty procession beyond, or from a memory the abyss had shaken loose.

She turned her head.

The figure remained motionless.

The voice came again.

This time it seemed to say the name of a person who had been dead long before Brunti was born.

She knew the name.

She had read it on the memorial's older inscription.

She almost answered.

Then the sound changed, stretching into a tone with no distinct syllables.

She realized she had supplied the name herself.

The realization gave her no power to stop hearing it.

***

Brunti tried to rise.

A sharp fracture ran along her left wrist. It was not loud, but she felt it travel through the crystal join with such precision that the rest of the world seemed to fall silent around it.

She sank back to the floor.

Her hammer pressed against her hip.

She reached for it out of habit.

A tool could give her leverage.

A tool could test the stone.

A tool could help her make the next movement safely.

Her fingers closed around the worn wooden handle.

The weight was familiar.

She rested it across her knees.

For a moment the hall steadied.

She remembered making a hinge at the western shelter, fitting the pin until the gate carried its own weight without dragging against the frame. She remembered the girl who had come back from the forest holding her walking stick close, and the small boy who had hummed beside the lantern.

Nothing in those memories promised a way out.

They reminded her that she had once known what a necessary task looked like.

She set the hammer down.

She could not repair this hall.

She did not know whether it needed repair.

She could barely hold her own body together.

The green pulse brightened behind the throne.

Brunti looked up.

The robed figure's face was almost entirely lost beneath the shadow of its hood. Dust and roots crossed the lower part. There were dark hollows where eyes might have been.

For an instant one hollow seemed to catch the light.

Then the other.

She could not tell whether the eyes had opened or whether the green radiance had reached them for the first time.

Her own vision narrowed.

The enormous root rose behind the throne, trembling with its slow internal motion.

The crimson veins beneath the seat answered.

The hall's pillars seemed to flex.

Bone and wood, stone and something more intimate than either.

Brunti understood that if she survived, she might spend the rest of her life trying to describe this place.

She also understood that description would not make her certain of what it was.

She wanted to leave.

Not after one more question.

Not after finding the figure that had resembled her among the marchers.

Now.

She pushed against the floor.

Her injured wrist gave way.

The hammer slid from her knees.

The last thing she clearly saw was the robed figure beneath the immense root, still seated while the hall's light grew too bright to distinguish its face.

Then the floor moved beneath her.

Or her body moved against it.

She could not tell.

***

Darkness loosened around a small, steady sound.

It was not the pulse of the immense root.

It was water moving over stone.

Brunti could hear individual drops and the gentle rush between them. The sound had no hidden interval waiting to become a name. It varied as water does, falling around small obstacles and gathering again.

Warmth reached her.

She tried to open her eyes.

Light pressed against them. She closed them once more and listened.

Somewhere nearby a bowl touched a wooden surface.

A person moved.

Brunti smelled crushed herbs, damp earth and something clean enough to remind her of morning rain.

When she opened her eyes again, sunlight lay across a shuttered window.

The room was small.

Its walls were made of wood and pale stone. Shelves held tied herbs, medicinal plants and glass vials that glowed softly. Protective wards shimmered at the corners of the window, faint enough to resemble reflections in water.

A cup stood beside her.

Her body rested on a bed shaped to support its mineral weight, with broad cushions placed beneath the joints that had carried too much strain. Her left wrist was bound in a careful brace that did not press against the fracture.

Brunti stared at the ceiling.

It was visible.

Four beams crossed it.

She counted them twice.

The number did not change.

A chair moved beside the bed.

“Steady,” someone said.

Brunti turned her head.

A Verdani healer sat close enough to reach her without leaning across the bed. Curved antlers rose above her brow, woven through with living vines and small blossoms that opened and closed almost imperceptibly with her breath. Her features held something of a deer, refined by the clear poise of a person accustomed to watching small changes in other bodies.

Water-like magic moved around her hands in slow, suspended ribbons.

Her expression softened when Brunti met her gaze.

“You have been through more than your body can safely carry,” she said.

Brunti looked toward the window.

Outside, mist hung beneath a pale sky.

“Where?”

“A lazaret on Keldor's edge.”

The answer did not seem to fit inside the room.

“Outside?”

“Yes.”

Brunti lifted her left hand.

Pain ran through the crystal.

The healer steadied the brace without holding her wrist down.

“Not yet,” she said. “Let the repair settle.”

“How did I get here?”

The healer's hands became still.

“Two outer-route wardens found you at first light beyond the last maintained path. They had gone to replace a broken warning cord and discovered you beside an old boundary stone, where a disused haul track disappears into the trees. Neither saw you arrive.”

“Who brought me?”

“They carried you on a litter to the ridge post. A supply wagon brought you here before the next night. Your wrist was fractured, and the mineral seams along your side had grown dangerously cold. The post's boundary marker is numbered. Beyond it, the wardens could make no trustworthy map.”

Brunti stared.

She had expected a name, a trail, a sequence of steps.

The healer gave her none.

“Was there a door?”

“I didn't see a door.”

“A tree?”

“I didn't see that either.”

Brunti lowered her hand.

The sun moved slowly across the coverlet.

***

The healer placed a bowl of shimmering liquid on the table.

“I know a little of Grimnir care,” she said. “Not enough to pretend your body works like mine. We have sent for supplies suitable for your mineral balance, and the brace was set with advice from a traveler who knew stonework better than our attendants.”

Brunti looked at the binding again.

It held her wrist in a position that allowed the uninjured portions of crystal to remain quiet.

“Who set it?”

“I did, with help. You were not conscious enough to consent to every small adjustment, so we did only what was necessary to prevent further damage. You can tell me what feels wrong.”

Brunti flexed one sound finger.

The water-like magic around the healer's hands moved in response, then settled.

“I don't remember being brought here,” Brunti said.

“That is common among those recovered from Keldor.”

“From the abyss?”

The healer did not answer immediately.

“Some of the people we treat use that word. Others remember only a forest. Some arrive with injuries that tell a clearer story than their speech.”

“I saw a hall.”

“You spoke about a hall while you were unconscious.”

“Roots.”

“Yes.”

“An enormous throne.”

“You mentioned something seated in the dark.”

Brunti tried to lift herself.

The room tilted.

The healer moved a cushion behind her shoulders and waited for her to settle before withdrawing her hand.

“Those are things you said,” she explained. “I cannot confirm that I have seen them.”

Brunti closed her eyes.

The hall returned at once: the countless kneeling shapes, crimson veins beneath an ancient seat, a great green-lit root moving like a part of some buried body.

The memory was so vivid she could almost feel the tremor.

Then it shifted.

The hooded figure wore a familiar cloak.

Her own hammer lay across its knees.

Brunti opened her eyes.

The bed was still beneath her.

The healer watched without asking what she had seen.

“Severe trauma,” she said after a while. “Exhaustion. Delirium. Some injuries we can measure, and others whose shape changes when a person tries to speak of them.”

Brunti looked at her wrist.

The fracture was real.

The bowl was real.

She could see sunlight through the shutters.

“I don't know what happened,” she said.

“That is an answer we can work with.”

***

During the next hours Brunti slept in short intervals.

At first she woke whenever water moved through the channels of the building, convinced for a moment that the enormous root had begun to pulse beneath the floor.

Then she recognized the smaller sounds.

A bucket was set down in the next room.

Someone opened a door.

A cloth was folded.

The heater in the wall settled with a dry click.

Ordinary things changed without demanding an explanation from her.

She began to watch them.

The healer returned with a cup of glowing water prepared for her condition. The liquid contained dissolved minerals and a faint, earthy sweetness. Brunti drank it slowly, allowing the warmth to move through the fine channels of her body.

A little strength returned.

Not enough to stand.

Enough to move her sound hand across the coverlet without feeling as though it belonged to someone else.

The healer checked the brace.

“How long was I gone?” Brunti asked.

“From your last known place?”

“From the shelter.”

“The wardens found you at dawn three days before you woke. They could not tell when you left the shelter, or how you came to be beside that marker. We can ask those who last saw you when you're ready.”

“What day is it?”

The healer told her.

Brunti tried to count the nights she remembered.

The number would not hold.

She had slept beneath roots.

She had crossed pools.

She had watched the procession.

She had stood before a throne.

Between those memories were spaces she could neither shorten nor fill.

“I thought the journey would lead to an answer,” she said.

The healer adjusted a blanket beneath her shoulder.

“About the forest?”

“About something at home.”

“Did it?”

Brunti looked at the light along the shutter.

“No.”

The healer nodded.

She did not tell Brunti the journey had been worthwhile merely because she had survived it.

***

A bird called outside.

One short note, then a pause.

Brunti turned toward the window.

The sound came again from a branch beyond the shutters, ordinary and sharp. She could hear the wingbeats when the bird moved to a higher perch.

It had no message.

It was simply there.

The healer opened the shutters a little farther.

Mist lay beyond the trees, softened by daylight. A path ran past the lazaret toward a fence and the first cultivated beds beyond it. Someone had tied strips of pale cloth to a row of stakes to mark where the ground was still too wet to walk.

Brunti watched the strips move.

A child passed along the path carrying a bundle of herbs. The child stopped to listen to the bird and laughed when it answered.

Brunti had seen too many things in Blackwood that seemed to demand she identify their meaning.

This sound needed nothing from her.

She let it remain a birdcall.

Then a low tremor seemed to pass beneath the floor.

It was so faint that she could not decide whether the sensation belonged to the building, her injured body, or a remembered pulse rising through the bed.

She looked at the healer.

The Verdani woman was arranging the tied herbs.

She did not look down.

“Did you feel that?”

“What?”

Brunti waited.

The sensation had passed.

“Nothing I can locate.”

The healer left the herbs on the shelf.

“If you feel it again, tell me. I'll check the supports.”

Brunti nodded.

The world outside the window went on moving.

***

A few days later Brunti could sit upright without help.

The healer had given her a resting surface fitted to the broad mineral plates of her back, and warm, mineral-rich water had eased the strain along the damaged seams. Her wrist remained bound. An experienced Grimnir craft worker would need to inspect it before she trusted it with heavy work again.

Brunti accepted that.

The healer did not praise her for accepting it.

She brought a small tray with nourishment and asked whether the texture was comfortable for Brunti's body.

Brunti tasted it.

“Too coarse,” she said.

The healer looked disappointed for half a breath.

“Then I'll have it ground finer.”

“You asked.”

“I did.”

They both considered that answer.

Brunti found she was almost smiling.

Outside, the station's attendants worked through the day. People arrived from Keldor's border with bruises, frostlike burns, exhaustion and stories that did not always agree with their injuries. The healer made no attempt to organize every account into one explanation of the forest.

She listened.

She asked what hurt.

She offered what care the station could actually provide.

Brunti had watched Sira work in much the same way.

The recognition struck her more deeply than any memory the abyss had offered.

***

On the fourth morning after Brunti first woke, Sira came to the lazaret. The wardens' ordinary incident report had traveled from the ridge post to Elda's shelter. It named no certain survivor, but described a Grimnir with a travel hammer and a damaged left wrist. Sira had taken the next supply wagon after recognizing the account.

She did not arrive in a flash of green light.

A wagon drew up outside. Voices spoke beside the gate, someone counted bundles, and footsteps crossed the little courtyard. Brunti heard Sira asking directions to the recovery room before the door opened.

Her deep-blue robe was dusted from the road. A narrow strap crossed one shoulder, and she carried a bundle of fresh dressings tied with plain cord.

For a moment she stood at the threshold.

Brunti had imagined this meeting during the long nights of sleep and waking. In some versions Sira was furious. In others she was relieved enough to forgive everything.

The real Sira looked tired.

Her gaze went immediately to the brace on Brunti's wrist.

“Can you move?” she asked.

“A little.”

“Can you walk?”

“Not far.”

“Good.”

Brunti blinked.

“Good?”

“It means the healer hasn't let you pretend you're ready to cross another forest.”

Sira set down the dressings.

She crossed the room and stopped beside the bed without touching Brunti.

“I was afraid,” she said.

The words were simple.

Brunti looked away.

“I know.”

“No. You know what I said at the shelter. You don't know what it was like to hear that someone had been found in the deep woods and not know whether it was you.”

Brunti held still.

Sira's face was very quiet.

“I was angry too,” she added. “I am still angry.”

“I know.”

“You keep saying that.”

“It's the only answer I have.”

Sira drew up a chair.

She sat.

They listened for a while to the little stream moving beyond the lazaret wall.

Brunti wanted to describe the throne, the pulsing root and the robed figure whose eyes might have opened.

She wanted Sira to understand why the place had held her.

But another sentence came first.

“The girl was safe,” Brunti said. “The search was over. I left anyway.”

“Yes.”

“I told myself there might be something there for the memorial.”

“And did you find it?”

“No.”

Sira looked at the brace.

“Then say that when you tell the others.”

“I will.”

For the first time, Brunti heard the promise as a task she could actually perform.

***

Velen arrived after Sira.

Their route had taken longer, not because they had been searching the forest but because they were carrying records between staffed stations. They came into the lazaret with a travel case, a clean notebook and several pages of correspondence protected from the weather.

They stopped when they saw Brunti.

The relief on their face was followed almost immediately by the careful reserve of a person trying not to write an ending before hearing the facts.

“We heard you had been found,” Velen said.

“Apparently.”

They sat opposite Sira.

Brunti watched them open the notebook.

“I'm not here to decide whether what you remember is true,” they said. “I'm here to help you keep the parts you can describe separate from the parts you can't.”

Brunti looked at the page.

A blank column ran beside the ruled lines.

Velen had left it on purpose.

“I saw a wooden door high in a cliff,” she began.

They wrote.

“I don't remember crossing it.”

They left space.

“Afterward there were roots. A very large tree. Faces singing in hanging wood. Black water that didn't show what stood above it.”

Velen wrote each statement separately.

Brunti told them about the procession and the figure that resembled her.

She described the enormous hall and the kneeling stone figures.

When she came to the throne, her voice slowed.

“There was someone seated there.”

“A person?” Velen asked.

“I couldn't tell.”

“Alive?”

“I couldn't tell.”

“Did they speak?”

“I heard something. I can't say where it came from.”

Velen wrote *sound perceived; source uncertain*.

Brunti watched the pencil move.

The words were few.

They were not sufficient to carry the scale of what she remembered.

They were sufficient to keep her from inventing the rest.

“I thought the place was alive,” she said.

Velen paused.

“Because of the root?”

“Because the whole hall seemed to move with it. I felt a pulse through the floor. The pillars looked like bones.”

Velen kept the pencil poised.

“Shall I write that you believed it was alive?”

“Yes.”

“And that you couldn't establish it?”

Brunti closed her eyes for a moment.

“Yes.”

They wrote both lines.

***

There were places she left blank.

The interval between the cliff door and the roots.

The interval beyond the Blackwood Guardian.

The path from the throne to the forest's edge.

Velen did not fill any of them with arrows.

They did not draw a route that made the unremembered places touch.

When the page was nearly full, Brunti took the pencil in her sound hand.

At the bottom she wrote:

*No object recovered.*

*No method learned.*

*No verified connection to the memorial.*

She looked at the last sentence.

It hurt more than the ones above it.

Sira watched her from the chair by the window.

“Does that mean you're going back?” she asked.

Brunti did not answer at once.

She could remember the way the Blackwood trees had hung upside down. She could remember the faces and the silent marchers, the immense root and the robed figure beneath it.

Some part of her still wanted to return and look more carefully.

She did not know whether she could find the place again.

She did not know whether the memories she already possessed would survive another visit.

The healer had warned her that her body needed rest and that damaged crystal could not be hurried into soundness by wishing it so.

Sira had work among the living.

Velen had records that belonged to people who had trusted them.

Darrik had a wall at home that needed a safe transfer when the next convergence came.

Brunti laid the pencil beside the notebook.

“No,” she said. “I'm going home.”

Sira did not smile.

She reached across the space between their chairs and placed one hand beside Brunti's sound hand, close enough to offer contact without asking for it.

After a moment Brunti placed her palm against Sira's.

The healer returned to check the brace and found them sitting there.

No one asked Brunti to stand.

Beyond the shutters, someone was repairing a fence. A hammer struck wood with a small, ordinary rhythm.

Brunti listened.

The blows were uneven.

One was too light.

The next landed squarely.

Someone adjusted the nail and tried again.

This time the wood held.
