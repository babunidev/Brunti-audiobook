# Chapter Twelve — Where Water Is Guarded

The first person to tell Brunti that Velen had been granted leave was a woman selling onions.

She stood beside a cart in Aetherion's morning market, polishing a stone weight with her sleeve. Brunti was trying to find the proper price for a bag of mineral grain when the woman glanced toward the corner of the square.

“Your scholar's coming,” she said.

Brunti turned.

Velen emerged from the crowd with a leather document case tucked beneath one arm and a traveling coat fastened at the throat. Their pale hair drifted in the little currents passing between the stalls. They were walking quickly enough that people stepped aside before they reached them.

“The archive approved my request,” they said.

Brunti smiled.

“Good.”

“For a survey of copied construction records along the southern trade routes, followed by the northern collections if the evidence warrants it. I am to return the source notes and account for anything borrowed.”

“Very good.”

“And there are conditions.”

“There are always conditions.”

“Only when other people are responsible for what you carry.”

Velen held out a sheet bearing the seal of the Archive of Echoes. It authorized them to examine regional copies and take measurements, not to remove original material without its keeper's permission. A second page contained names of workshops and record-holders who might know where older drawings had traveled.

Brunti looked at the signatures.

“Do you need me to sign it?”

“No.”

“Then I approve.”

“You do not have that authority.”

“I was speaking of your joining us.”

Velen's mouth moved almost into a smile.

Sira arrived from a nearby stall with a packet of folded cloth and a small sealed letter. She listened to the news, then gave Velen a brief embrace before they could object.

“Welcome to the road,” she said.

“I have traveled before.”

“With companions who will sometimes stop for reasons unrelated to cataloguing?”

Velen considered Brunti, who was already putting away the grain.

“That will require practice.”

They left the market after midday, following a road that curved away from the central districts toward southeastern Ethera's Vale. Ahead lay old waterways, cultivated meadows and the lands where the green country began to give way to Evergrove's dry approaches.

Brunti carried the measured drawing in her pack. The original remained safely in the archive, attached to its old volume. She checked the copy each evening, not because she expected its missing lines to appear but because she wanted to be certain that the paper stayed dry.

Velen noticed.

“Fold it at the marked edge,” they told her on the second evening. “Not across the measurements.”

“I know.”

“You have folded it across the measurements.”

“Only once.”

“Once is how a crease begins.”

Brunti stared at them.

Sira walked between the two, listening without intervening.

Later, when Velen had gone to ask a caravan keeper about the road ahead, Sira said, “I think you have met someone who loves paper as much as you love stone.”

“Paper is much more easily damaged.”

“Exactly.”

Brunti looked at the folded drawing and adjusted its wrapping.

***

The land changed slowly over the following days.

The Vale's broad fields narrowed into green courses along streams. Beyond them rose stretches of pale earth and low, dry ridges. Roadside wells were marked with care, and the travelers began to see water carried in covered vessels rather than drawn freely from every brook. Verdani gardeners tended channels that turned away from the road toward groves of fruit trees and beds of flowering plants.

At one rest stop a currentwright was repairing a sluice gate. She stood knee-deep in the shallow channel, guiding a thin ribbon of water around the damaged edge while a companion fitted a new piece of wood.

Brunti watched for a while.

“The channel could be made wider,” she said.

The currentwright looked up.

“It could.”

“Then it would carry more.”

“Into which garden?”

Brunti glanced along the narrow watercourse.

Two groves depended on it. One had new growth; the other held older trees whose roots reached farther into the soil.

The currentwright pointed to a low ridge cut into the bank.

“That ridge keeps the water from taking the shortest course. If we remove it, the first garden floods and the second dries. Width isn't our problem.”

Brunti nodded.

She thought of the memorial wall and its old support, then deliberately left the comparison unspoken.

Sira knelt beside the water and asked the currentwright for news of the oasis routes. The woman knew several of the places Sira named. Most were well. One caravan had been delayed by damaged road markers. Another had turned back after finding a canal too low for safe passage.

“Where are you headed?” the currentwright asked.

“Toward Nereval's sanctuary first,” Sira said. “Then perhaps the Evergrove road.”

The woman's face grew thoughtful.

“There have been messages traveling west,” she said. “Not all of them clear. Speak to the riverkeeper near the border. He often knows who has passed.”

Sira thanked her.

Brunti could see that she wanted to ask another question, but the currentwright had already turned to her work. Water slid through the newly fitted gate and settled into its proper channels.

The travelers continued.

***

That evening Sira told them about a cave in Evergrove.

They had reached a sheltered stopping place where an old tree leaned over a stone wall. The meadow lay behind them; ahead, the air smelled drier, with dust rising from the road whenever a cart passed. Velen was arranging their field notes into separate bundles.

Brunti had been considering the darkening sky when Sira said, “There is a shrine I would like to visit again someday.”

“Where?” Brunti asked.

“Among the oasis groves of Evergrove. Not far from a route I used to carry messages along.”

“A shrine to Nereval?”

“No. To Umbrael.”

Brunti looked up.

Umbrael belonged to the Terran Assembly of her own people, Goddess of Darkness and of the truths hidden beneath the world's visible surfaces. Brunti knew her crescent mark from the Skylands. She had not expected to find it in the heartland of the Verdani.

“Why there?”

“Because a person who needed the quiet built a place for it,” Sira said. “Or so the keeper told me.”

She described a modest cave beneath old trees cultivated around an oasis. No grand door marked its entrance. Small candles stood at different heights beside a smooth stone altar. Melted wax lay at its base, and a deep-cut crescent rested in the dark surface. The candlelight touched the edges of the carving without reaching its deepest grooves.

“It smelled of cool earth and candle smoke,” she said. “There was a low hum in the stone. Outside, the leaves moved in the daylight, but their sound reached the altar only faintly.”

Velen lifted their eyes from the notes.

“Was it a public temple?”

“No. A little place. The stone before the altar was smooth enough for anyone to sit.”

“Did you learn who founded it?”

“I asked the keeper. She said her grandmother had tended it before her. She knew stories about its beginning, but not a date.”

Velen reached for a pencil.

Sira gave them a look.

“You are welcome to write down that I heard a story. Not that the story has become a founding record.”

“I was going to note the location.”

“You may note the oasis route. The cave is known to local people, but it is not a place to send every curious traveler who asks for secrets.”

Velen set down the pencil.

“Very well.”

Brunti looked toward the dark fields.

“What did you pray for?”

Sira took time to answer.

“Nothing I could ask the shrine to give me.”

“Then why go?”

“Someone I knew had died. I was carrying words for her family. There were people waiting for me, and I was afraid of speaking them badly. The cave was quiet enough to remember what I had been asked to say.”

Brunti listened.

She had once thought darkness merely meant a thing concealed from sight, perhaps for a reason. Sira's account made it something else: a little room where words could wait until their bearer was ready.

“Did it help?” Brunti asked.

“Yes.”

“Did the keeper speak?”

“Only when I was leaving. She told me the candles were not there to defeat the dark.”

Velen glanced up.

“Then what were they for?”

Sira smiled faintly.

“To give someone a small place to see.”

They rested beside the old wall until night settled over the road. Brunti felt no need to draw the cave. She had not seen it. Sira had, and that difference seemed important.

***

They reached Nereval's sanctuary after another day's travel.

Its approach lay near the southeastern border of Ethera's Vale, where clear water crossed ancient stones before flowing toward the dry lands beyond. The surrounding meadows held tall grasses and low pools. Trees gathered in a sheltered fold of land, their roots gripping banks that had been shaped by many seasons of water.

The sanctuary was not marked by towers.

Its doorway stood among moss-covered stones at the edge of a quiet clearing. Weather had softened the carved face, and roots traveled through the earth beside it. A Verdani guardian waited before the entrance, wearing armor that seemed grown from leaves, bark and green water rather than assembled by a smith.

The guardian's eyes followed the travelers as they approached.

Sira bowed her head.

“We come to honor Nereval,” she said.

“And to look upon the sanctuary?” the guardian asked.

“If we are permitted.”

The guardian lifted one hand toward the weathered stone door.

“This is a place of water, adaptation and healing. Those who enter must show the rune of the river, a sign of respect for the one whose care is kept here.”

Brunti looked at the door.

No visible lock lay within the stone. The pale seams of the seal were quiet, yet she could feel a faint coolness moving through the surrounding rock.

She did not touch it.

“What form does the rune take?” Velen asked.

The guardian regarded them.

“Have you never seen a river change its course?”

“I have seen many,” Velen said. “But the written forms differ by place and age.”

“Then the written form alone will not answer your question.”

Velen looked as though they wished to reply. Sira placed a hand lightly upon their arm.

Brunti studied the stones around the doorway.

A narrow watercourse passed through the grass nearby. It should have joined a shallow pool at the base of the clearing, but the last section lay almost dry. Fine silt had gathered beneath a fallen branch, and water trickled away through a low crack in the bank.

A small plant grew beside the broken channel. Its leaves had curled inward, and the soil around its roots was pale with dryness despite the sound of water so close.

Brunti walked toward it.

“May I look at this?” she asked the guardian.

The guardian inclined their head.

“Look.”

She knelt and examined the damaged bank.

The fallen branch was not large, but it had caught reeds and loose clay until they pressed against the channel's edge. Water had found an easier route beneath the stones, leaving the plant dry. The bank could be opened by force, but that might wash away the roots holding the soil together.

Sira joined her.

“Can it be helped?” she asked.

“Probably. Not by tearing the whole bank apart.”

Brunti found a flat stone and set it beside the channel to support the loose earth. She waited while Sira loosened the reeds from the branch, freeing the trapped water a little at a time. Velen placed their document case safely beneath a tree and came to help hold the upper bank steady with a narrow board borrowed from their pack.

The work was muddy and slow.

A small stream of water returned to the proper channel, then vanished beneath the silt again. Brunti adjusted the stone. Sira lifted the branch slightly, feeling the water collect against its underside. Together they cleared enough space for the current to move without carrying the bank away.

The water reached the little plant.

It soaked the dry soil gradually, darkening it around the roots. Sira touched the leaves with wet fingers, guiding a soft flow along the curled edges. The plant did not spring upright at once. Its stems remained tired and bent, but the roots were wet again.

Brunti sat back.

“That should hold until the bank settles,” she said.

Velen examined the small stone support.

“The upper side may shift after a heavy rain.”

“Yes. It will need to be checked.”

The guardian stood where they had been throughout the work.

“Why did you mend it?” they asked.

Brunti looked up.

“Because the plant was drying.”

“Was that the price you expected to pay for entry?”

Brunti glanced at Sira.

“No,” she said. “We were asking about the rune before I saw it.”

The guardian's expression did not change, but their hand lowered.

“Then you have shown an understanding of the river that no carved sign could have proved alone.”

They turned toward the doorway.

Brunti remained beside the little plant.

She had come to a sealed temple once before, at Hearthguard Sanctuary, and learned to open its barrier through a moving pattern in stone. This door was different. Its keeper had not asked her to solve a mechanism, and the water she had helped restore belonged to the land whether the door opened or not.

She rose slowly.

The guardian stepped aside.

***

Somewhere beneath the tangled roots, water began to trickle.

No visible stream flowed into the stone doorway, yet the sound grew as the guardian moved away from it. It passed through the clearing like a distant melody, weaving between the leaves and the quiet wind.

Blue light appeared beneath the moss.

One by one, forgotten runes awakened across the weathered stone. Their glow revealed an inscription to Nereval, God of Water and Healing, whose name Brunti knew but whose older signs she could not read. At the center of the door a single symbol remained dark.

Velen took a breath as though to speak, then stopped.

“The mark of restoration,” Sira whispered.

Brunti studied it.

The other runes held their light, but the central sign seemed to wait. Its grooves were shaped in a flowing curve, like water meeting an obstacle and finding a way around it.

The guardian watched without offering instruction.

A cool breath passed through the clearing, carrying the scent of rain upon fertile earth. Beyond the sealed doorway something shimmered faintly, as though sunlight were moving beneath deep water.

Then the sound beneath the roots fell silent.

Brunti looked at the little plant they had helped.

It was still bent. The restored water had reached its roots, but the upper leaves remained curled.

She might have tried to make the rune shine by placing her hand upon it. She might have asked Velen whether the glowing lines could be copied into their notes and compared to other seals. Instead she walked back to the channel.

The flat supporting stone had settled slightly into the mud, leaving a thin gap beneath one edge. Water was passing through it. If the stone slipped farther, the new channel might fail before morning.

Brunti crouched.

“The bank needs another support,” she said.

Sira came beside her.

“Now?”

“Yes.”

Velen looked from the dark symbol to the small stream.

“Perhaps the seal is waiting for you.”

“Perhaps,” Brunti said. “The bank certainly is.”

She took a second stone from the dry ground and fitted it gently against the first. Velen steadied the upper edge while Sira guided the water around the roots, keeping the soil from washing away. They worked until the channel held its shape without their hands.

A thin stream ran steadily toward the shallow pool.

The little plant's leaves glistened with moisture. One of them eased outward, no more than a small movement, but enough to show that the stem could answer the water.

Behind them a low note passed through the stone doorway.

They turned.

The central symbol had begun to glow.

Its blue light spread through the old grooves and met the surrounding runes. The seal loosened with a sound like water slipping free of a narrow place. The door did not swing open at once; instead a soft radiance drew back from its edges, revealing a passage beyond.

The guardian inclined their head.

“You may enter.”

Brunti looked at her muddy hands.

“Will the channel remain safe while we are gone?”

“I will watch it,” the guardian said.

Only then did Brunti step toward the doorway.

***

The sanctuary held the quiet of deep water.

Cool air moved through the passage. The walls carried worn inscriptions and small carved basins where water rested without spilling. Beyond them lay a chamber of pale stone, its floor crossed by narrow channels that met beneath a low, open space.

There was no throne.

A simple pool occupied the center, bordered by smooth stones whose surfaces caught the soft blue light. The water lay still enough to reflect the ceiling, where old lines formed patterns Brunti did not recognize. At the far wall a carved sign of Nereval curved through the rock, not raised above the room so much as becoming part of it.

The guardian remained at the entrance.

“Those who come here may rest and remember,” they said. “They may ask for guidance in healing, but the sanctuary does not promise to restore everything that has been lost.”

Sira bowed her head.

Velen stood beside the pool, looking at the old inscriptions without opening their notebook.

Brunti knelt.

She placed one hand against the smooth stone at the pool's edge and felt the coolness move through her mineral seams. The water carried a faint vibration, but it was not the double note she knew from her Skyland.

It had its own rhythm.

She closed her eyes.

For a while she imagined the old memorial wall. Two stones beneath one cover, separate echoes entering the same passage, the brace that had carried their sound into the resting chamber. She had wanted the wall to remain where it stood because that was how she had always heard the dead.

But the people who lived beside it needed rest.

The memory of Hearthguard Sanctuary returned to her: the sealed hearth, the stone bench, the jarred supplies waiting for people who had not yet arrived. That place had been protected so it could remain useful. Its barrier had not made it less welcoming.

She opened her eyes.

Velen was standing beside Sira, speaking quietly.

“Do you think the seal responded to the repaired channel?” they asked.

Sira looked toward the entrance.

“The guardian asked for the river's rune. Perhaps the water showed it.”

“That is one interpretation.”

“It is.”

Velen glanced at Brunti.

“Would you record the sequence?”

Brunti considered.

“Yes,” she said. “But not as a method to open every sanctuary.”

Velen's expression softened.

“Agreed.”

She took out her notebook and wrote down what they had observed: the guardian's request, the blocked watercourse, the plant with its dry roots, the restored channel, the runes that glowed after the guardian stepped aside, and the mark of restoration that remained dark until the bank held.

She did not write that the door would always open for such work.

She did not know.

The sanctuary remained quiet around them.

Sira sat beside the pool and sang a few low notes. The water answered only by carrying the sound into the stone.

They rested until the light at the entrance changed.

***

The guardian was waiting when they came out.

The little plant stood beside the channel with its leaves still damp. Water ran steadily past the supporting stones. The guardian knelt to examine the bank, then pressed one hand gently into the soil.

“It will need care after the next rain,” Brunti said.

“I know.”

“Will someone be here?”

“I am here.”

Brunti nodded.

Velen asked permission to copy the runes they had seen within the passage. The guardian considered the request and allowed them to record the visible inscriptions, but not to take impressions from the stone.

Velen accepted the condition without argument.

It was a small victory for everyone who had spent the previous day listening to them.

They left the clearing with the evening light falling through the trees. The road beyond ran toward a broad river, where the green country began to break into shallower channels and drier fields.

Sira walked ahead.

Brunti could tell she was thinking about the messages traveling west from Evergrove. She had asked at every waystation since Aetherion and received nothing certain. Now they were close enough that a wrong rumor might lead them toward people who were not expecting guests, or away from someone who needed help.

“Do you know whom we're looking for?” Brunti asked.

Sira slowed.

“Someone who may have passed through the river stations.”

“That is not a name.”

“I have names. I don't have a place yet.”

Velen adjusted their document case.

“Then the next keeper may know which route they took.”

“Perhaps,” Sira said.

She did not sound comforted.

Brunti remembered how often Sira had waited while she drew stones, questioned witnesses and searched for the perfect mark. The time had belonged to them both. Yet Sira had carried obligations in her bag that Brunti could not read and sometimes forgot to consider.

“Would you rather go ahead?” Brunti asked.

Sira looked back.

“Without you?”

“If that is what you need.”

For a moment Sira seemed startled by the offer.

“No,” she said. “I would rather you came with me. But I would like us to move when the road allows it.”

Brunti nodded.

They walked on.

***

The riverkeeper was laughing when they found him.

His voice rolled over the water before the travelers saw the old tree where he rested. He sat with his back against the trunk, one knee raised and his head tipped back, his laughter coming in great warm bursts that seemed to startle the birds among the reeds.

A basket of food rested beside him.

He stopped laughing long enough to wipe one hand across his face.

“Ah,” he said. “Three travelers. I was just telling the river how long a proper meal ought to last.”

Velen looked toward the water.

“Did it answer?”

“Of course. It kept running. I take that as agreement.”

Sira smiled and bowed her head.

“Good evening, keeper.”

“Evening? It was morning a moment ago. Someone has been walking too slowly.”

Brunti liked him at once.

He was an elderly Verdani, his horn branches softened by years of growth and his watery skin carrying the gentle shimmer of the river. The bark behind him had been polished by many seasons of his resting there. He shifted to make room along the trunk.

“Sit,” he said. “Unless you have brought a tale so urgent that it cannot wait for a bite.”

“We have been traveling from Aetherion,” Sira said.

“Then you have passed at least seven places where people claim to cook the best supper in the Vale.”

“Eight,” Brunti answered.

The keeper grinned.

“Ah. The city has improved.”

They sat beside him.

His name was Iren, though he said most people called him Riverkeeper because it saved them the trouble of remembering where he lived. He offered food from his basket: spiced roots, soft fruit and a small cake of grains. Brunti found a portion prepared with crushed mineral salts and accepted it gratefully.

“Do you always carry this much?” she asked.

“For myself? No. I am old enough to know that my appetite has not learned moderation, but I am also old enough to expect company.”

Sira laughed.

Iren began a story about a boatman who had tried to bargain with a fish.

“The boatman said he would let it go if it told him where the river was deepest. The fish looked at him for a long time, then said nothing at all, which was a wiser answer than most boatmen give.”

Velen looked skeptical.

“Fish do not speak.”

“Not usually,” Iren said. “That is what made this one remarkable.”

Brunti smiled.

The keeper continued, embroidering the tale until the boatman had lost his hat, his supper and finally his patience. Iren laughed so hard at the ending that he had to stop for a moment, leaning against the tree while the river filled the silence.

Then he grew quiet.

“There was a woman who told that story better than I ever could,” he said.

He looked across the water.

“Her name was Luma. She always gave the fish a sharper tongue.”

Sira did not interrupt.

Iren rested one hand against the bark. For a while the only sound was the river moving among the stones.

“She's gone now,” he said. “But I keep forgetting which parts of the story are hers and which I have added.”

Brunti considered the difference.

“You remember her laugh?”

“Oh yes.”

“Then perhaps that is enough for tonight.”

Iren looked at her.

“Perhaps.”

The corner of his mouth lifted again.

“Though I suspect she would say I have still told it too slowly.”

They laughed together, and the river went on.

***

Sira waited until the meal was finished before asking about the western messages.

Iren's expression changed.

“You are carrying words for the oasis communities,” he said.

“Yes. And I have heard that something happened along one of the southern chains.”

He nodded.

“A messenger came through this morning. She was bound for the trade stations and carried news from a settlement beyond the first desert crossings.”

“Which settlement?”

Iren named a small oasis village.

Sira went very still.

Brunti watched her.

“I know people there,” Sira said.

“I thought you might,” Iren replied. “The messenger asked for anyone traveling toward Evergrove who knew the old garden routes.”

“What did she say?”

“That the village was attacked. The water channels were damaged. Several homes burned. Some people reached the neighboring oasis, but not all their messages have caught up with them.”

Sira looked down at her hands.

“Who is missing?”

“I do not have a complete list.”

“Did she name the dead?”

“A few names. I will tell you what she said, but I won't pretend that a traveler's report is the same as standing among the people who are waiting there.”

Sira nodded.

Iren spoke slowly.

The names came without ceremony. Some belonged to gardeners, others to families who had worked beside the water channels for generations. One woman had been taken to a nearby settlement for care. A child had gone with an aunt; the messenger did not know where they had reached. The route between two oasis Hearts was damaged enough that the ordinary water carts could not travel it safely.

Sira listened without moving.

When Iren finished, she asked him to repeat two names.

He did.

She closed her eyes.

Brunti felt the old urge rise in her: to ask for a map, count the distance and decide what tools would be needed. Her hammer could lift burned beams. Her knowledge of supports might help rebuild a channel. There were things she could do.

But Sira was still sitting beside the riverkeeper, holding names she had known before Brunti had ever met her.

Brunti waited.

Velen had taken out a small notebook but kept it closed.

After a while Sira said, “I carried a message from that village last season. They asked me to tell their relatives that the first blossoms had returned after a dry winter. I was supposed to go back when the summer waters were high.”

Her voice grew quieter.

“I kept thinking I had time.”

Iren nodded.

“Most of us do.”

Brunti looked at her.

“Do you want to go there?”

“Yes.”

“Then we will.”

Sira turned.

“You have your own road.”

“I know.”

“And the drawing.”

“I have the drawing.”

“The convergence—”

“Darrik is keeping the wall safe. He will not abandon the people resting beside it.”

Sira looked away.

Brunti felt the weight of the words. The deadline was still real. If she stayed away too long, the memorial might be moved before she returned. She could not promise otherwise.

But she could choose what to do with the road directly before her.

“We can travel by the safer oasis route,” she said. “I will not pretend I know the place. We will need someone who does.”

Velen spoke then.

“The archive authorization includes examination of regional workshop copies. There may be records along those roads. But that is not why Sira needs to go.”

Sira looked at them.

“No.”

“I can help document what people ask to preserve, if they want that done.”

Iren inclined his head.

“That is often useful,” he said. “Just make certain they have water before you ask for their histories.”

Velen closed the notebook.

“Agreed.”

***

They stayed beside the river that night.

Iren gave them directions to a caravan station where guides knew the mapped oasis chain into Evergrove. He warned that some of the smaller channels could be low or damaged, and that a direct line across the dry land would not be safe without local knowledge.

Brunti copied the route.

Sira sat a little apart, looking toward the darkness beyond the river. Velen was checking the straps of their document case, making sure the pages inside would remain dry if they crossed a shallow ford.

Brunti walked to Sira and sat beside her.

“You knew one of the people he named,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Were you close?”

Sira looked toward the water.

“We grew up listening to the same songs at the oasis gatherings. She was older than me. She used to change the ending of every song she taught, then insist that anyone who remembered the old ending was singing it wrong.”

Brunti smiled faintly.

“What happened when someone argued?”

“She laughed. Then she sang both endings.”

They sat quietly.

The river moved below them, carrying scraps of moonlight between the reeds.

“I should have gone back sooner,” Sira said.

Brunti considered several answers and rejected them.

“You did not know,” she said at last.

“No. But I knew I had promised.”

Brunti looked at her own hands.

She had left the Skyland because she had promised Darrik she would seek evidence before the convergence. Sira had joined her and spent days following stones and strange paths while her own messages waited.

“I didn't ask enough about your road,” Brunti said.

Sira shook her head.

“I didn't tell you enough. I was afraid that if I asked you to turn toward Evergrove, you would think I was pulling you away from the only thing you cared about.”

Brunti looked up sharply.

“That is not true.”

Sira met her gaze.

“I know that now.”

The words were simple. They left no need for a speech.

From the old tree came another burst of laughter. Iren had apparently remembered a second ending for the fish story and was telling it to Velen, who sounded increasingly uncertain about whether they were being teased.

Sira listened.

Then, very softly, she began to sing the first line of the song her friend had once taught her.

Brunti did not know the words. She heard the melody rise, turn and rest.

Sira sang the line again with a different ending.

For a moment the two versions seemed to hang together above the moving water.

Brunti waited until the song was finished.

***

At dawn they crossed the river with a small caravan bound for the first Evergrove waystation.

The guide was Verdani, her garments wrapped against the dry wind. She inspected their water vessels, asked how much food they carried, and insisted that no one leave the mapped route without speaking to her first.

Brunti liked her immediately.

Beyond the river the meadows thinned. Pale earth showed between the grasses, and the light grew stronger over low ridges of sand and stone. Farther away, wind-cut hills rose against a sky that promised heat.

Sira walked at the front beside the guide.

Velen followed, carrying their authorized archive case and a growing collection of questions they had learned not to ask before breakfast.

Brunti came behind them with the tools.

She looked once toward the river, where Iren's old tree stood small in the distance.

The keeper had stayed to watch the passing boats and hear the news of travelers. He had offered them food and names and the directions needed to reach people who were waiting.

Nothing in his kindness had required a test.

Brunti adjusted her pack and followed the caravan eastward.

Somewhere beyond the dry hills, water still moved through cultivated roots. Somewhere among those roots, a village was trying to mend what violence had broken.

They went toward it, carrying more than a drawing.
