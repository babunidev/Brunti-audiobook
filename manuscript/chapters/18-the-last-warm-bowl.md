# Chapter Eighteen — The Last Warm Bowl

The fire below the mountains vanished before they reached it.

One moment its orange point hovered in the falling dusk. The next, a shoulder of dark stone hid it from view, leaving Brunti with the long white slope and the sound of Hesk's boots breaking through crusted snow.

“The post is still there,” he said, without turning.

“I know.”

“You looked disappointed.”

“I was beginning to like seeing it.”

Velen walked between them, carrying the papers beneath their cloak. The journey from Ordo's shop had been quiet. Brunti's repaired wrist was sound enough to bear her pack again, though a faint roughness remained when she turned it. Every so often she tested it against her other palm. The mineral binding held.

Far below, the broad approaches to Everwinter Valley lay beneath a sheet of old snow. A chain of low ridges separated the road from the trading post. Smoke from its hearth ought to have been visible beyond them, but the wind was driving close to the ground and the last daylight was failing.

Hesk stopped by a wooden marker half enclosed in ice.

“We won't reach it before dark,” he said. “There's a ledge shelter farther down.”

“We have the lantern,” Velen said.

“And there's a trail.”

“I know.”

Hesk looked at Brunti.

“Do you always travel with someone who agrees so enthusiastically?”

“They're an archivist. They have a special affection for evidence.”

“We have evidence of a shelter,” Velen replied.

“Then you can sleep in it.”

Brunti would have laughed, had the sound not come from above them.

A small, sharp crack.

It crossed the air so swiftly that she could not place it at first. Hesk was already looking toward the frozen cliff on their right. A jagged line opened across its upper face. Pebbles broke free and bounced over the ledges below, each strike sending another handful of stone downward.

“Back,” Hesk said.

The first large rock fell.

It struck the outer edge of the trail with a sound like a door being torn from a great hinge. The impact sent snow into the air. Before the powder cleared, more stone broke loose behind it.

Hesk seized Velen's sleeve and pulled them toward a recess under the inner wall. Brunti followed, turning once to see the path ahead disappear beneath an avalanche of boulders.

The mountainside gave way all at once.

Stone struck stone. Snow rose in a pale cloud. A violent grinding traveled through the slope, so deep that Brunti felt it in the mineral layers of her legs. A block the size of a small house broke against the cliff and vanished into the falling dust. The narrow trail simply ceased to exist.

Velen pressed both hands over their ears.

Brunti crouched beside them and braced one arm against the sheltering wall. Hesk held his staff across the recess, not because it could stop the mountain but because it gave him somewhere to keep his shaking hands.

The noise rolled on. At last the largest impacts became fewer. Loose fragments still pattered down the slope.

As the dust thinned, a mound of shattered stone emerged where the road had been. It rose nearly to the ledge above. An overturned waymarker protruded from its base, its painted point buried in the rubble.

Something shifted deep within the pile.

A lower block settled with a heavy groan.

Above the broken route, another crack began to spread.

Hesk did not move.

“Don't go near it,” he said.

“I can see the upper seam,” Brunti replied.

“Then you can see why we aren't crossing.”

The new fracture widened by a finger's breadth. A stone no larger than an apple fell and struck the rubble below. None of them spoke until the echoes stopped.

Velen uncovered their ears.

“Was anyone on that part of the trail?”

“Not that I saw,” Hesk said.

Brunti looked into the drifting dust. She could make out the place where two sets of older footprints had ended at the fallen waymarker. Beyond it, nothing remained to read. The prints might have belonged to travelers who passed hours earlier. It was equally possible that someone had been beyond the bend when the slope broke.

“We should call,” she said.

Hesk nodded once.

They called into the settling cloud. Their voices reached the stone and returned strangely flat. No answer came.

Brunti could have climbed the nearer rubble. She saw handholds, a narrow line between the blocks, an exposed strut of darker rock that might support her weight. The upper crack ran directly above them.

“We can't see the far side,” she said.

“No,” Hesk replied. “But a messenger may be able to reach it by the lower track.”

“Is there one?”

“There was. Before last winter's melt.”

He searched his route book, then pointed beneath a ridge toward a stand of dark firs.

“A drovers' way. Longer and lower. We go carefully.”

Brunti looked once more at the fallen marker. Its painted point, meant to direct travelers, now indicated the heart of the pile.

She turned away.

***

The lower path was not a path for most of the first hour.

They descended through snow deep enough to hide the uneven stones underneath it. Hesk tested the ground with his staff, found a firmer shelf, and brought them along its edge. Brunti followed, giving Velen her hand over the places where the ice had collected in hollows.

The air grew colder as they lost height. Behind them the broken cliff disappeared among the ridges. There was no way to tell whether it had stopped moving or merely grown too distant to hear.

At the edge of the firs they found a roofless stone pen used in warmer seasons. Old hay lay beneath the snow, mixed with broken cord and the rounded marks of animal hooves. Hesk crouched beside a narrow drainage channel.

“This was the drovers' way,” he said. “It still runs toward the southern post. The trees should shield us from the worst of the wind.”

Velen sank onto the wall.

“Is it certain?”

“Nothing about a trail is certain,” Hesk answered. “I can tell you which one I would take.”

Brunti opened their food parcel. The bread was hard. The little pouch of prepared minerals from Ordo remained dry, and she separated a portion for the water she would need later. Velen ate a piece of bread without looking at it.

“We should leave a sign at the upper junction,” they said.

“For the next traveler?” Brunti asked.

“For the next guide. And the road office. If Hesk has a copy of the former trail, we can record where it broke.”

Hesk took a narrow slat from the old pen and cut a warning into its surface. Brunti added a clear arrow indicating the lower turning. With a length of cord they fastened it where the open route would be visible from the approach.

It was an imperfect sign. Wind might tear it loose; snow might bury it. They fixed a second marker farther down.

At a bend the trees opened to the valley. Brunti saw the orange light again, this time much closer. It shone from a low cluster of roofs beyond a frozen stream. Smoke moved in a thin line from the tallest chimney before the wind scattered it.

“That's the place,” Hesk said.

Velen's shoulders settled a little.

The light looked small enough to be covered by a hand.

***

The trading post stood at Everwinter's southern edge, where the last sparse trees gave way to the permafrost valley. Its walls were made of dark stone and closely joined timber, with shutters strengthened against the wind. A covered rack held fur-lined cloaks, spare harness and bundles of dried roots. Outside, an empty cart had been set on blocks while its owner repaired a wheel.

The door opened before Hesk could knock.

A young Rokur woman stood in the entrance. Her hair rose in vivid red tongues of contained flame, and emberlight moved beneath the dark contours of her face. Warmth flowed past her into the snow.

“Hesk,” she said. “You're late.”

“The mountain was early.”

She looked at the dust along their cloaks and the scored edge of Brunti's pack.

“Rockfall?”

“The southern upper ledge. The whole turn is gone.”

Her expression changed. The light beneath her skin drew inward, becoming steadier.

“Come inside. Tell me after you've warmed.”

The comforting smell of stew met them at the threshold, mingled with wood smoke and a faint sweetness of dried herbs.

A great pot hung over a hearth where wood crackled above glowing coals beneath iron bars. The room held shelves of rations, old lanterns, knitted mittens and bundles of preserved food. Fur-lined cloaks lined the far wall. Several travelers were seated near the fire, their boots steaming in the warmth. On a long counter lay a ledger, a folded road map and a single clean bowl.

The keeper lifted the pot's heavy lid. Steam briefly concealed her glowing eyes. She gave the contents a gentle stir.

“Ah, nearly ready,” she murmured.

“I'm Aksa,” she said when it cleared. “Don't put your wet gloves on the dry papers.”

Velen had been reaching for the counter. They withdrew their hand.

“I wasn't going to.”

“Your fingers were.”

Aksa set a clean wooden bowl beside the hearth, where firelight danced over its polished rim. Then she wiped a place clean and motioned for them to sit.

The stew contained roots, pale beans, winter herbs and pieces of something Brunti could not identify by scent. She had known Grimnir cooks who could make stone minerals taste faintly of pears, but no one at home had cooked with the restrained heat that a Rokur carried so naturally into a room. Aksa tended the stove carefully, neither feeding it too much nor allowing its warmth to drop.

“Fresh vegetables have been scarce,” Aksa said. “The last southern cart brought grain, salt and preserved fruit. We make a good pot from what remains.”

“Does everyone receive stew?” Brunti asked.

“Everyone who needs a bowl receives a bowl while I have one to give. After that, they receive whatever I can make.”

Aksa ladled portions for the two travelers. She set an additional small dish of ground mineral salts by Brunti's place after Hesk mentioned her wrist.

“For the water, if you use them,” she said.

Brunti examined the mineral mix. There were no unfamiliar glittering ingredients, only finely prepared material suited to replenishment.

“Thank you.”

“You can thank me by telling me what happened to the road.”

Brunti gave the sequence precisely: the first small crack, the movement across the upper cliff, the boulders, the buried marker, the new fracture and the absence of an answer when they called. Velen laid their sketch beside the ledger. Hesk traced the lower way they had used.

Aksa listened without interrupting.

“The upper turn was already weak,” she said at last. “Three weeks ago a wagon driver reported water running beneath the crust. Someone marked it for repair. No crew had time to come.”

“The warning wasn't at our lower station,” Hesk said.

“It should have been.”

Aksa made a short note and copied the location into a separate register.

“The valley office will need this. Someone must check the far side before another traveler approaches it from the north.”

Brunti leaned over the drawing.

“I could climb around it tomorrow from below, if I had two supports and—”

“No,” Hesk said.

“You don't know what I was going to say.”

“A longer version of climbing around it.”

Aksa turned the ledger so that Brunti could see a road already marked in faded red ink.

“There is a western shelf that joins the old track beyond the fall. The patrol sometimes takes it. No one travels that route after a fresh storm, and never without someone who knows the descent. There's also the eastern shortcut.”

“Why not use the shortcut?” Velen asked.

Aksa set down her pen.

“Because the Frostfang Alpha hunts there.”

Velen looked at the map, where the eastern line passed among several pale, open basins.

“A wolf?”

“An enormous one.”

She opened a page tucked into the ledger's cover. The account was written in a hurried hand and signed by a courier who had carried provisions from the valley.

“The courier saw two blue eyes before he could make out anything else. Then the creature rose out of a snowdrift, snow sliding from its white shoulders. Its ears turned toward the far slopes, and its breath showed as a pale cloud. The courier had mistaken its body for an accumulation of snow until it moved.”

Aksa spoke without trying to make the event more frightening than the writer had found it.

“It turned toward the open valley and gave a deep, commanding howl. The sound reached the far ridge and came back thin as an echo. Then it bared its long teeth. The courier was already withdrawing. He told me its paws sank deeper into the snow, planted as though it meant to hold that ground, while its blue eyes remained perfectly still.”

“Was he attacked?” Brunti asked.

“No. He backed away before finding out whether the animal would follow.”

Velen studied the writing.

“Are there other accounts?”

“Several. The name Winterworg appears in some. I won't tell you they're different creatures merely because the names differ. The trackmarks and places are close enough that the guides treat it as the same danger.”

Brunti nodded. She remembered the fiery Embersnout, its body held low in the snow, and the silence after it chose another direction. A wolf did not need to be hunted to make a traveler change course.

“The eastern road can wait,” she said.

Aksa's flame brightened a fraction.

“Good. Now eat before it cools.”

***

By morning the trading post had become a place of small, urgent tasks.

Someone was repairing the wheel outside. A pack animal had rubbed a patch of skin raw beneath a badly fitted harness; Hesk loosened the strap and padded it with folded wool. Two travelers waited for word about the rockslide. The post's messenger had gone to find the nearest road patrol.

Brunti slept on a prepared resting stone near the hearth, its steady vibration carrying into her mineral layers through the night. When she rose, the roughness near her wrist had eased. She helped Aksa move a crate of dried roots onto a higher shelf while Velen copied the route warning in letters large enough to read from the wall.

The keeper's kitchen was far less generous in daylight than it had seemed in the evening.

The stew pot remained warm, but its contents had grown shallow. Aksa had a row of jars she turned away from the window to keep out the light. The fresh vegetables were counted. She kept the remaining grain under lock against dampness rather than theft.

Brunti saw her pause above the pot.

“Will another cart come soon?”

“Soon is a word with no number in it,” Aksa said.

“Velen doesn't like those either.”

“I rather like them,” Velen said from the notice board. “I just dislike when someone calls them dates.”

Aksa smiled.

“The next caravan was due yesterday. It may be on the other side of the rockfall, or it may have taken a safer road. The store will last if no one becomes greedy and the weather allows some people to continue.”

A great knock shook the door.

Aksa crossed the room, and cold air swept over the threshold as she opened it.

A broad blue figure filled the entrance. The visitor bent beneath the lintel, his enormous ears glittering with frost. He wore a patched cloak around his shoulders and carried an empty basket so small in his hand that it resembled a toy.

“Aksa!” he cried. “There's a smell in your house that will make me die of hunger.”

“Fergus,” she said. “You said that yesterday.”

“And I survived. That's how good your cooking is.”

He entered with difficulty, careful not to strike the doorframe with his shoulders. His ice-blue skin sparkled beneath the hearthlight. A small button nose lifted toward the stew, and a deep rumble came from his belly.

The sound interrupted his own laughter.

He laughed again.

Brunti recognized the ice troll from the travelers' accounts. Frostbite Fergus, who was said to be as hungry as he was friendly, and friendly almost all the time.

He placed his basket on the counter.

“Two turnips,” he announced.

Aksa peered inside.

“One turnip and a stone.”

“The stone looked promising.”

“It's a stone.”

“I was hungry when I found it.”

Velen took a sudden interest in their papers. Their shoulders were shaking.

Aksa lifted the stone out of the basket and placed it beside the window.

“Go sit outside for a moment, Fergus. The room is crowded.”

“Will there be soup?”

“When there's soup.”

“I like that answer.”

“Then remember it.”

He walked to an old pine log beside the sheltered wall of the post and lowered himself onto it with a grunt. The log creaked under his weight. Fergus pressed both broad hands to his round belly. Another deep rumble answered him. For an instant he listened with a look of enormous seriousness, then threw back his shoulders and laughed hard enough to shake the frost from his cloak. He stopped as a breeze reached him, little button nose twitching and oversized ears turning in opposite directions. Whatever scent had reached him brought his broad toothy grin back at once. With a contented sigh he shifted sideways to leave a generous space beside him and patted the empty seat.

Brunti stepped out carrying her small mineral-water cup and a piece of bread for Velen.

Fergus lifted his nose to the breeze.

“Is that bread?”

“Yes.”

“Is it yours?”

“No. It's Velen's.”

“Are you Velen?”

“Do I look like an Aevis?”

“I'm trying to improve my chances.”

She sat beside him. The log was wide enough for her heavy mineral body, though it leaned slightly when she settled. Fergus adjusted his cloak so it did not cover the dry patch where she placed her cup.

“You're a Grimnir,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Do you eat stones?”

“Prepared minerals, among other things.”

He considered this.

“I have a stone you might like.”

“Is it a turnip?”

He stared at her, delighted.

“No! Aksa said it was a stone!”

Brunti began to laugh. Fergus joined her, so loudly that a little snow fell from the post's roof.

For a while they spoke about nothing important. Fergus asked where she had traveled. She told him that her home moved above the world; he asked whether it ever dropped its dinner into the clouds. She described the Skyland's resting chambers and the craft that kept them warm. He said he preferred logs because they could be found almost anywhere, though he admitted there were fewer good ones in Everwinter than he would like.

His ears turned toward the far road.

“Cart,” he said.

Brunti listened. She heard a distant creak and a faint metallic rattle.

Fergus raised his nose again.

“Not much food on it.”

“How can you tell?”

“Smells like rope.”

A moment later a small sled appeared between the buildings, pulled by two tired animals. Its driver had wrapped a cloth around one hand. Behind the sled walked a young traveler with frost on his eyelashes and a bundle under his coat.

They came straight to Aksa's door.

***

The driver was carrying a message from the far side of the rockslide.

No one had been struck on the northern approach, as far as the patrol could confirm. The line of travelers had stopped after hearing the first fall; two carts had turned back. The upper passage remained unstable. A road crew was marking a longer route, but the work might take days.

Brunti felt her shoulders loosen at the news. For the first time since the rockslide she could hear a traveler stamping snow from his boots without mistaking it for another fall.

The younger traveler was less fortunate.

He had been walking behind the sled when one of its runners caught beneath the ice. He had helped raise it, lost his gloves, and continued through the wind with a bundle of letters held against his chest. His fingers were stiff and pale. Aksa brought him toward the hearth while the driver went to tend the animals.

The little bowl beside the counter was the last full portion of stew.

Brunti saw Aksa look into the pot. There was broth left, thin enough to reveal the bottom, and not much else.

Fergus appeared in the doorway, his large ears lowered to fit under the lintel.

“Is someone cold?”

“Very,” Aksa said.

He moved aside to let a helper pass with blankets.

Aksa placed the bowl into the young courier's hands. She did not ask him what he could pay. She did not tell him it was the last.

He drank carefully while she warmed his hands from a safe distance, keeping her contained fire from becoming more heat than his skin could bear. Velen unfolded a dry cloth and arranged it beside the hearth so the letters would not be scorched.

Brunti watched the steam rise.

She had been thinking about that pot all morning. The measured stores. The absent supply caravan. The way Fergus asked for food without pretending he did not want it, and the way Aksa always answered with what she could actually give.

“Have you any roots left that can be used tonight?” she asked.

Aksa nodded toward the high shelf.

“Some. Why?”

“We can make another meal.”

“From one turnip and a bag of dry grain?”

“And the mineral salts. Not all of them are for eating, but I can separate those that are. There are old roots in the crate we moved. They need cutting.”

Aksa considered.

“I'm not putting stone dust in a stew for everyone.”

“Neither would I. The mineral mixture is prepared for Grimnir. Mine can be separate.”

“Good.”

Velen looked up.

“I can fetch clean water.”

“Can you carry a bucket without reading the label on its handle?” Aksa asked.

“I can try.”

Fergus held out his enormous hands.

“I can bring firewood.”

“There is plenty of firewood,” Aksa said.

“I can bring more.”

“Bring the small split pieces, not a tree.”

Fergus glanced toward the snow.

“There was a tree I was considering.”

“Leave it standing.”

By afternoon the kitchen had filled with work. Brunti cut the older roots into small even pieces, saving the good portions and separating the damaged ones. Aksa adjusted the hearth's heat in careful stages. Velen wrote the revised ration count for the ledger and fetched water from the sheltered cistern. Hesk returned with the road patrol's messenger, and the driver helped carry crates away from the damp wall.

Fergus brought exactly six logs. He counted them twice.

When the new pot began to simmer, the room smelled not quite like the meal of the previous night. The flavor was plainer. There was less oil and fewer herbs. It made no pretense of abundance.

The courier had fallen asleep near the hearth.

Aksa glanced at the bowl in his empty hands and then at the new pot.

“We won't feed a whole caravan on this,” she said.

“No,” Brunti replied.

“But we can feed the people here tonight.”

The keeper nodded.

Fergus's belly rumbled from the doorway.

“And one very large person,” he added.

“Half a bowl first,” Aksa told him. “Then we'll see.”

He drew himself up.

“Half a bowl is a very generous beginning.”

The young courier woke in time to see Fergus accepting his share with solemn ceremony. The troll raised the bowl in both hands and smelled it as though he were judging a rare perfume.

“It's warm,” he announced.

“That's often the idea,” Aksa said.

He drank. His ears lifted.

“It's good.”

It was the same word Brunti had heard Sira use for a serviceable bridge, a finished song, and a shelter in which no one was required to prove they deserved a place. She had once wanted the word to mean that everything had been put right.

Here it meant that the pot was not empty yet.

***

The snow stopped before dawn.

The valley outside the post lay under a thin blue light, every exposed rock edged with silver. Hesk had received a message from the road patrol: the western shelf would be safe enough for a guided crossing later in the day, provided no new weather came.

Brunti and Velen had another task before they could leave.

Aksa had been keeping a small collection of travelers' descriptions of the northern sites. Some were copies of old reports; some were route notes; a few contained sketches of markings on ruins. She showed the packet to Velen beside the hearth.

“These are not the old records themselves,” she said. “Most of those are kept farther in. But they can tell you where the guides have actually found a passable approach.”

Velen handled the pages carefully.

“Are there names attached to them?”

“Where I have them. Where I don't, I have left a space.”

Brunti looked at the empty spaces. They no longer annoyed her as much as they once had.

Aksa pointed to a rough drawing of an icebound arch.

“Travelers call it the Everwinter Guardian. Beyond it is a cave, or so the accounts say. The path is sometimes open and sometimes not. I won't promise that reaching the arch gives you the cave, much less the old Frostweaver knowledge you're seeking.”

“That's exactly the sort of warning we need,” Velen said.

“Then copy it before you leave.”

While Velen worked, Hesk readied their packs and checked the weather. The young courier was recovering enough to help sort letters, though Aksa ordered him to remain at the post until his fingers moved freely. Fergus was sitting on his log again, looking across the bright slopes.

Brunti joined him.

“Leaving?” he asked.

“Later.”

“Everyone does that.”

“Not everyone.”

“Most who come here.”

He patted the log, making space as before.

Brunti sat.

“Do you ever travel into the valley?” she asked.

“When the snow lets me. Sometimes I help pull sleds. Sometimes I go to see what the wind has brought. Mostly I get hungry and return.”

“Has the wind ever brought you something worth keeping?”

Fergus thought for a long while.

“A glove,” he said.

“One glove?”

“It was left behind on a fence. A small one. I put it inside the post for its owner.”

“Did the owner return?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“They needed the glove.”

He shrugged, as if that were sufficient.

Brunti looked over the valley. Beyond the nearer ridges stood the frozen country they had come to see, with ruins and records older than the roads they now depended upon.

Fergus stirred beside her.

“Do you know why Aksa cooks so much?”

“Because people arrive hungry.”

“That's one reason.”

“What's the other?”

“She likes people to have somewhere to sit.”

He spread his great hands over his knees.

The morning wind touched his ears. His nose rose toward the kitchen smoke.

“You can have a bowl even when you're not very interesting,” he said.

Brunti laughed.

Fergus looked pleased.

Then his expression changed. He turned his head toward the eastern rocks, where an old icebound path led across a low shoulder of the valley.

“Bear,” he said.

***

They saw the Frostclaw Behemoth from the upper end of the path.

It rose beyond a cluster of ice-coated boulders, immense and white, the color of the snow around it. For a moment the movement might have been a drift collapsing. Then the shape lifted higher, bringing great paws into the pale air.

Long claws stood clear against the sky.

The bear roared.

A thick cloud of breath poured from its jaws. The sound crossed the valley and returned from the frozen heights in a delayed, ragged echo.

Velen froze beside Brunti.

Fergus, who had accompanied them to the turning, raised one broad hand.

“Stay,” he said quietly.

The bear's orange eyes searched the open ground. It turned on its hind feet, a great weight shifting through fur and muscle. One paw lowered slowly. Somewhere beyond the rocks, old ice creaked.

Brunti felt the pull of her hammer. It was a familiar calculation: distance, weight, angle, the narrow line of retreat behind Velen. The creature could cross the open ground far more quickly than they could carry their supplies back up the slope.

Hesk had shown them another route that morning, a longer curve under the western bank. It was still passable.

Brunti lowered her hand.

She touched Velen's elbow and indicated the rocks behind them. Fergus stepped sideways to clear the narrow turning. Together they withdrew without running, keeping the bear in sight until the ridge hid it.

Only then did anyone speak.

“Was that the Frostfang?” Velen asked, voice thin.

“No,” Brunti said. “It was not.”

“A different reason to avoid the eastern shortcut,” Fergus observed.

Velen gave a startled laugh.

They reached the sheltered western path as the light strengthened. Across the valley the bear's roar came again, more distant now. It was neither a warning meant for them nor a challenge they had to answer. The animal was living its own vast, difficult life among the ice.

Back at the post, Aksa listened to their account without surprise.

“Frostclaw,” she said. “It's been seen near the frozen shelves lately. Those claws can cut through more than ice.”

“We didn't approach,” Brunti replied.

“Good. If it has chosen that place, the road can wait.”

She marked the sighting on the board beside the mountain warnings. Then she looked at Brunti's pack.

“Your guide is ready for the western route. Have you eaten?”

Velen opened their mouth to answer, but Aksa had already set down a small portion of the new stew.

It was not much. The bowl held more broth than vegetables, and the grain had softened overnight. Brunti had her prepared mineral-water drink beside it. Velen divided the bread they had kept for the road.

They ate standing near the warm counter while Hesk checked the straps once more.

Outside, Fergus laughed at something the courier had said. The sound rolled across the frozen yard, a deep and cheerful thunder unlike the breaking rock of the previous evening.

Aksa returned the copied packet to Velen.

“Guardian first,” she said. “Cave after, if the passage is sound. You cannot reason with a collapsed mountain or a hungry bear.”

Brunti looked toward the valley.

“No,” she said. “And I intend to return for another bowl.”

The keeper smiled slightly.

“Then mind what the guide tells you.”

By midday the western route was marked and the patrol had signaled its opening. Brunti took up her pack. Velen secured the copies under their cloak. Hesk went ahead to meet the guide who would take them farther into the valley.

At the last turning, Brunti looked back.

Aksa stood in the doorway, a bright figure of contained fire against the dark stone. Fergus had returned to his log and was holding a small bowl in both enormous hands. A thin line of steam rose from it into the cold.

Beyond them, the northern snow lay silent over things that had been preserved for centuries and things that were already beginning to break.

The warm bowl vanished when the path curved.

The road continued.
