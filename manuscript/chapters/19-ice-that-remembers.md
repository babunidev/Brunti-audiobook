# Chapter Nineteen — Ice That Remembers

Snow covered the tracks behind them before the trading post had disappeared from sight.

The western trail climbed a low ridge above Everwinter Valley, then turned inward between banks of blue-shadowed ice. Brunti looked back once. The roof where Aksa kept her stove was already hidden, and only a thin column of smoke marked the place where they had eaten.

Their new guide was a weathered Aevis named Enna, whose hair was tied beneath a dark hood and whose eyes rarely stopped moving. Hesk had brought them to her at the patrol marker, exchanged a few route notes, and gone back toward the southern road. He had promised to tell Aksa when the next passage was safe.

Enna carried a length of blue cord across her shoulder. At each turning she examined the snow before putting a boot into it.

“You follow my steps,” she told Velen. “The dark places are not always holes, but the pale ones sometimes are.”

“That seems a poor system,” Velen said.

“It's an excellent system if you follow the part about my steps.”

Brunti walked last. Her heavier mineral body could test a questionable ledge without breaking as easily as some bodies, but she could still bring a thin crust down beneath the others. She paid attention to the distance between their feet.

The valley was not silent.

Ice moved somewhere under its own weight with a long, deep complaint. Snow whispered across the ridges. From far away came the uneven tapping of a loose wooden marker against a post. Whenever the wind dropped, a new sound emerged, small enough to have been overlooked in the mountains.

“Do people live up here all year?” Brunti asked.

“Some,” Enna said. “Other people have tried.”

The guide stopped and looked toward a hollow where the wind had swept the ground bare.

Something glowed in the snow.

It was the corner of a chest.

***

The chest rested in a shallow depression among wind-rounded stones, half covered by a dusting of new snow. Its timber had darkened almost to blue-black with age and weather. Delicate flowers and winding leaves were carved across the lid, their petals filled with fine frost. A faint fragrance of blossoms rose with the warmth despite the bitter air. Iron bands secured the corners, and long icicles hung beneath them, hard and transparent in the pale moonlight. At the center, an iron lock remained firmly fastened, a tiny icicle hanging from its lower edge.

Brunti crouched.

A faint warmth reached her hands.

It rose from the wood as surely as heat rose from Aksa's stove, yet the icicles remained intact. Not a drop fell. The thin crust of ice along the hinges held its shape.

“Someone has put a fire inside a box,” Velen said.

“Someone would have had to keep feeding it,” Brunti replied.

“A spell, then.”

“You're allowed to say you don't know.”

“I have said it rather often recently.”

Enna remained standing on the firmer stones.

“Collectors come looking for this one,” she said. “Many think the chest contains something worth more than all the supplies they carried here. They find the warmth first. Then they decide it must be meant for them.”

“Has anyone opened it?” Brunti asked.

“Not that I've seen. It moves less than the stories about it.”

The runes beneath the frost glimmered blue. A gust swept powder across the lid until the markings vanished. Then their light shone through the new snow, one unfamiliar sign after another, illuminating the frost from below. The glow strengthened slowly as Brunti watched, then softened again. She drew a finger near the wooden surface without touching it. The warmth remained, mild and oddly steady, while the frost at the carved edges showed no sign of melting.

She thought of the pair of memorial stones at home. A familiar resonance had persuaded her to brace them too quickly. The material had answered her action, but it had answered in a way she had not understood.

“Warmth is happening,” she said.

“Yes,” Velen replied.

“And the ice isn't melting.”

“Yes.”

“That's all we've seen.”

Enna gave a small approving sound.

Velen opened their book. Brunti watched them write the two statements on separate lines. Beneath them they left a space for a possible explanation.

The chest remained closed. The flowers continued to glow faintly beneath their frozen outlines.

As the travelers turned away, Velen looked back.

“Do you think something inside wants to be found?”

Brunti adjusted her pack.

“I don't know how we would ask.”

They went on, leaving the little pocket of warmth under the open sky.

***

The entrance to Everwinter Cave lay above an old ice shelf, reached by a route so narrow that Enna had them unfasten their packs and carry them low beside their bodies. The cliff above curved out to shelter a crescent of still air. Beyond it stood a pale mouth of ice and stone.

A sudden puff of frost burst from the icy entrance and scattered small crystals across the snow. Something stirred within the blue shadows.

Velen halted.

One enormous ear moved toward the mountain wind while the other turned into the cavern.

A small ice troll stepped into view, its shaggy body nearly the same glacial blue as the wall behind it. Its ears extended well beyond the width of its head. Thick hair hung over a gnarled face. Its nose wrinkled beneath a heavy brow, and its clear watchful eyes studied the visitors with an attention that made Brunti stop moving.

“So this is the Guardian,” she said.

Enna lowered her voice.

“That's the name the guides use.”

The troll's head tilted toward her. It drew a breath, then exhaled a little squall of frost. Crystals swirled around its feet and rattled against the entrance before settling. For a brief moment it looked surprised by the storm it had made, then returned its watchful attention to the passage.

Velen tucked their hands under their cloak.

“Does it speak?”

“Not to me.”

“Does it ask for an offering?”

“Not that I've seen.”

The Guardian took one short step and stood squarely across the opening.

Its ears moved independently, following the wind outside and something quieter within the cavern. Brunti could hear faint drips somewhere deep inside, although the cold was sharp enough to stiffen the joints of her fingers. Perhaps the sound came from ice touching stone. Perhaps it came from something else.

Enna indicated a flat ledge well away from the entrance.

“We can wait there. If it doesn't move, we turn back.”

Brunti looked at the troll. She could lift it with both hands if she wished. The thought came easily, the way a skilled worker might think about the size and weight of a tool. It told her nothing about whether the creature meant harm or why it stood there.

“Then we wait,” she said.

They settled on the ledge without unpacking. Enna ate a little dried fruit and watched the upper slope. Velen copied the outlines of the entrance. Brunti kept an eye on the troll, which kept an eye on all three of them.

For a long while nothing changed.

Then a faint grinding came from inside the cavern. It was not loud, but the Guardian's nearer ear turned sharply toward it. A dusting of ice slid from the ceiling just beyond the opening.

The troll looked back over one shoulder. Its breath gathered into another pale cloud.

It stepped to the side of the entrance, leaving a narrow clear way between its body and the ice wall.

Velen looked at Enna.

“Is that permission?”

“I don't know,” the guide said.

“Would you go in?”

“I would look at the ceiling first.”

Brunti stood and examined the passage. The ice above it had no fresh cracks she could see. The shallow drift beneath the outer lip had settled after the small fall. Beyond the first turn, the ground looked reasonably level.

“We need a way out that we can still find,” she said.

Enna tied one end of the blue cord around a stout stone protrusion at the edge of the ledge.

“I'll stay near the entrance,” she said. “Take only the length you need. If the floor becomes uncertain, come back.”

The Guardian watched while they passed. Brunti did not touch it. Velen kept their papers close. No word or ritual was spoken, and no sign explained whether the creature had chosen to allow them through or simply wished to listen to the sound behind it.

The cave received them without an answer.

***

A faintly luminous line ran across the frozen wall.

At first Brunti thought it was a crack catching the lantern light. Then Velen lifted their hand and revealed several more lines beneath the frost, arranged with the deliberate spacing of writing. Each stroke shone softly blue through the white coating.

The passage widened.

Stalactites hung above them in great translucent points. Natural ice sculptures rose from the floor, folded and ridged as though a current had frozen in the middle of its own motion. Their lantern drew reflections from surfaces that were clear in one place and clouded in the next.

Velen approached the writing.

“Do you recognize it?” Brunti asked.

“No.”

“Any part?”

“A repetition. Here and farther down. But I can't say whether the second mark is the same sign or a damaged version of another.”

Brunti saw the place Velen meant. One narrow stroke divided into two, then vanished beneath an opaque patch. Beyond it, a similar stroke emerged from clear ice. Between them lay a gap where no lantern could carry light far enough to reveal the whole line.

“Could you copy what is visible?”

“Of course.”

“Then copy that.”

Velen drew a small grid across their page and began. They were patient with the visible marks, turning the lantern to judge whether a faint curve belonged to the script or to the frost over it. Brunti stood nearby, feeling how differently the ice carried sound. A step made in the outer passage returned in a short sharp echo. Here it spread and softened through the walls.

Farther in, the rock held another kind of record.

A great shape curved through the clear ice, suspended at an angle far above the floor. Brunti raised the lantern. Bones lay within the frozen mass, their smallest ridges preserved alongside larger structures. An arch of old vertebrae bent toward the wall; below it a row of thin elements resembled the ribs of a creature she had never seen alive.

“An animal,” she said.

“Or what remains of one,” Velen answered.

On the opposite side, a second set of bones crossed the first at another angle. It lay deeper in the wall and was partly hidden by a milky layer. There was no obvious reason to believe the two creatures had come here together, or at the same time.

Yet in the lantern light they seemed close enough to be companions.

Velen drew back.

“How many years?”

Brunti shook her head.

“Ask the stone how long it has been cold.”

“I wasn't asking you to give me a number.”

“You sounded as though you wanted one.”

Velen smiled only faintly.

They walked between two columns of clear ice. Above them another fossil emerged from a thick frozen seam, more delicate than the first. The end of a narrow bone was visible with extraordinary sharpness. Its continuation was lost in a dark pocket of rock.

The cave had held these forms with a precision no ordinary paper archive could match. It had retained edges, teeth, joints and small marks that might have worn away anywhere else.

It had not kept their movements, their voices, the ground on which they once lived, or the cause of their ending.

Brunti could imagine the missing creature in a dozen ways. Each would take shape around the bones and fit them well enough to be tempting. None would be the creature itself.

“Something survived,” she said.

Velen lifted their lantern.

“What?”

“The shape of part of it.”

The archivist looked from the fossil to the writing farther along the wall.

“A copyist would call this an extraordinary preservation.”

“And you?”

“I would call it that too.” Velen paused. “But the rest is still missing.”

They came to a narrowing where the cord had nearly reached its end. Beyond, the ice darkened, and the luminous inscriptions continued beneath a cloudy layer. Brunti could see one additional preserved shape turning away into the mass. Its proportions might have belonged to a wing, or a fin, or a broken limb. There was not enough exposed to decide.

Velen stood still a long time.

“I could draw the missing line,” they said at last.

Brunti looked at the page.

“You could.”

“It would be a reasonable line. The marks on both sides appear to continue in the same direction.”

“Would you call it a copy?”

“No.”

They did not draw it.

Instead Velen shaded the area where the ice hid the writing, and wrote beside it: *not visible*.

Brunti reached toward another pale inscription but stopped with her fingers short of the wall. In Aetherion, a partial craft diagram had taught her that the casing around two memorial stones might have been repaired more than once. Here, another kind of material held traces of a past she could not read.

One did not explain the other merely because both were old.

The cord tightened slightly in her hand.

“Enna is giving us the end,” Brunti said.

They turned back.

The Guardian was still at the entrance, its broad ears moving beneath the curtain of ice. It watched them pass without stepping nearer. When Brunti reached the outer ledge, the creature lowered its head a fraction and released a thin cloud of frost across the snow.

Enna was checking the sky.

“The weather has changed,” she said.

Above the valley, the first distant peaks had vanished.

***

The storm arrived while they crossed a low saddle south of the cave.

At first it was a loose veil, cold powder drifting over the old tracks. Then the wind gathered it into a continuous sheet. The far ridge disappeared. The near rocks followed. Within minutes the world had narrowed to Enna's cloak, the blue cord between the three travelers, and the dark patches where Brunti's heavy feet found exposed stone.

“Stay together,” the guide called.

Velen answered, though their words were pulled away.

Brunti kept her hand on the cord. The air brought snow into the little seams around her eyes. Her mineral skin did not burn as flesh might, but the cold made the fine plates at her joints less willing to move.

Ahead, someone shouted.

It was neither Enna nor Velen.

The three stopped. A second call came through the blizzard, so broken by wind that only its urgency remained.

Enna raised one hand.

“People.”

Brunti heard another sound beneath the storm: boots scraping against a crust that no longer showed a trail.

They followed the voices only as far as Enna could confirm the ground. At a shallow bend, a group of travelers appeared out of the white, huddled so close together that their cloaks seemed to form one dark shape. The foremost dragged a heavy hem through the snow, leaving a narrow furrow that the wind quickly filled. Two others stood behind, their hands around the arms of a fourth.

One pointed toward the hills, then dropped the arm when the snow revealed nothing.

“We were following the markers,” the traveler said through chattering teeth.

“Which markers?” Enna asked.

“The black stones. There should be another by the southern turn.”

“There is,” Enna said, “but you've passed it.”

The group looked at one another.

Velen took out the dry cloth they had wrapped around their records and offered it to the person with the barest hands. Brunti stepped closer to the traveler whose cloak dragged on the ground.

“Can you walk?”

“Yes.”

“Are you certain?”

The traveler tried to move. One foot slipped at once.

“We have to get back,” another said. “The wind—”

“I know,” Brunti replied. “Where is the last place you knew?”

The question seemed to puzzle them. A woman with a torn hood looked down at the snow beneath her feet.

“A low cairn,” she said. “And a narrow cut in the rock. We were following someone else's prints.”

Enna recognized the cut.

“That was the old trail. It goes toward a slope that has been closed.”

“Can we reach shelter?”

“Yes,” the guide said. “Not by following those tracks.”

Brunti took one end of the blue cord and fastened it around a stone where the path widened. Enna checked the nearest rocks and found a firmer line to a small hollow beneath the ridge. They moved the exhausted travelers there one at a time, bringing them behind the rock rather than urging them to hurry into the white.

One person began to cry. Another was too cold to speak.

Velen knelt beside them and produced the last of the dry bread from the trading post. Brunti opened her mineral-water flask, then remembered it was prepared for her own body. She set it aside and gave the travelers the clean water Enna carried instead.

The woman with the torn hood watched her.

“Will you leave us?” she asked.

“No.”

The answer came before Brunti could decide how much longer the storm might last.

She stood at the edge of the hollow while Enna made a short line of visible knots along the cord. They would follow it back to the last safe stone when the wind eased. If it did not ease, the hollow was sheltered enough for a careful fire and a longer wait.

From inside the group, a thin voice said, “There was someone calling us.”

Velen looked up.

“Where?”

“Farther along the ridge. A woman's voice. She said there was a warm place.”

Enna stopped knotting the cord.

“Did you see her?”

“No. Only heard her.”

“Was that before you lost the markers?”

“I don't know.”

The traveler lowered their head.

Brunti remembered the trading post's warm bowls, set down without asking for a story in return. These people needed shelter that could be reached, not an answer about every voice on the mountain.

“Rest,” she said. “We can speak of it when you're warm.”

***

The blizzard thinned after dark.

Enna led the group out of the hollow slowly, keeping the cord taut between the safe stones. A low patrol shelter stood beyond the next ridge, its roof built into the lee of a rock face. A lantern burned behind a small shutter, proof of someone tending it. They reached the door with frozen hems, sore hands and more exhaustion than anyone admitted.

The shelter keeper brought blankets and a hot kettle. Velen helped the lost travelers describe the last marker they had recognized. Enna traced their route on a board so that the patrol could close the misleading turning when daylight returned.

Only after the group was fed did one of the travelers speak again of the woman's voice.

The woman with the torn hood had heard another account in the valley. A figure called Frostlace appeared between the frozen ridges, silver-white hair flowing over alabaster-pale shoulders, crystals bright along its strands. Her deep blue eyes seemed almost tender. With slow, graceful steps she moved backward in a silent dance, extending a slender hand. A whisper of warmth drifted across the snow, as if from a distant hearth, although there was no fire anywhere among the ridges. Where Frostlace passed, the snow hardened and frost crept over bare stones. At the mouth of a narrow, dark passage, she paused, smiled faintly, and curled her fingers in invitation. Her fingertips glittered with frost even as the promise of warmth returned on the wind.

“People followed her?” Velen asked.

“So they say,” the woman replied. “Farther and farther into the ice.”

“Did you see her?”

“No. I thought I heard someone promising a fire. It might have been the wind. Or someone else shouting from farther away.”

Another traveler nodded.

“I heard nothing. I was watching the person ahead.”

Velen wrote the accounts separately. The sound that had brought the group off the route could not be reconstructed with confidence, and no one proposed following it back into the darkness.

Enna added the description to her caution list.

“Frostlace is a dangerous name here,” she said. “That does not mean every sound in a storm belongs to her.”

“Nor every sound like a hearth is a hearth,” Brunti replied.

The woman with the torn hood looked toward the real fire.

“No,” she said. “This one has a roof.”

A little later, when the keeper brought another kettle to the table, Velen pushed their copied cave drawing toward Brunti.

At the top of the page, the visible runes had been reproduced carefully. Beneath, Velen had written the unresolved gap as *not visible*. In the margin another hand—Velen's own, but hurried—had begun a possible continuation in faint pencil.

“Did you mean to put that there?” Brunti asked.

Velen looked down.

“Yes.”

“Will you keep it?”

“I'll keep it as a conjecture. On a separate page.”

They erased the faint line from the source copy. The marks that remained were fewer. They were also exactly the marks the cave had allowed them to see.

***

The weather held clear the following morning, though low fog gathered across the northern floor of the valley.

Enna took them past a narrow line of black rock where a traveler had once set a row of small stones to show a winter crossing. Beside the stones lay fresh snow, undisturbed except for the tracks of a small animal. Brunti could see the mountains beyond the mist only when the sun moved high enough to touch their upper ridges.

“Do you know the cries they call Misthowl?” Enna asked.

Velen had been studying the valley maps.

“The spectral wolf?”

“That's what the older guides call her.”

Enna described a white shape seen moving through fog in the northern reaches, its flanks so translucent that the mist passed through them. Its eyes shone faintly above the snow. When it raised its muzzle, a mournful howl carried far across the valley and returned from hidden heights.

“Some people turn back when they hear it,” she said. “They think it means a storm or an avalanche is near.”

“Is it a warning?” Brunti asked.

“Sometimes bad weather follows. Sometimes it doesn't, or the person telling the story doesn't remember the days when nothing happened.”

Velen looked up, pleased to hear a guide speak this way.

“Then you don't know whether she causes the storms or senses them.”

“No,” Enna said. “And not knowing is better than climbing a bad slope to see if I can prove the story.”

A pale disturbance moved in the fog below. For a moment Brunti thought of a wolf's shoulder dissolving into cloud. It resolved into a low wind dragging loose snow from a ridge.

Velen did not write Misthowl's name beside it.

They followed the firmer trail north.

Near midday the valley narrowed between ice-coated cliffs. The snow there was fine and dry, lying in delicate whorls around the stone. Enna slowed as they approached a place where the path split. The higher trail led toward a long white shoulder. The lower one descended into a fog-filled basin.

“Where does the upper way go?” Brunti asked.

“Toward one of the older frozen reaches. Some call it the Queen's country.”

“The Icebound Nymph Queen?” Velen asked.

Enna nodded.

Brunti had seen the name in Aksa's packets. It belonged among dangerous reports, not among the routes a traveler could treat as open to anyone who wished to ask a question.

A pair of blue lights appeared ahead.

At first they hovered alone. Then the snow around them began to turn, lifting from the ground into a narrow column. Crystals circled the lights without falling. The shape drifted sideways across the upper path, almost silent.

“The Warden,” Enna whispered.

Brunti watched the vortex gather into a clearer form. Frost extended through the air along its movement, and the path beneath it whitened. Behind it lay the high frozen ground that the valley's stories named as the Icebound Nymph Queen's realm.

The blue eyes remained steady.

Velen took one step forward before stopping.

“I've never seen anything like it.”

“Neither have I,” Brunti said.

The Warden drifted closer to the center of the path. Its outer crystals continued circling while the middle held still, a barrier made from motion. The blue light sharpened. A fresh layer of frost spread over the ground ahead.

Brunti could imagine striking the column, scattering it long enough for the three of them to pass. She could also imagine the scattered crystals gathering behind them, closing the route they needed to return.

There was no reason to cross.

Velen had come for old knowledge, not a contest with a creature guarding someone else's ground. The cave had shown enough to make another question necessary.

Brunti put her hand lightly on Velen's sleeve.

“We can take the lower way.”

“But this may be the older route.”

“It is not ours to force.”

The guide was already moving toward the lower trail. Brunti waited until Velen had turned, then followed them. Behind her, the Warden remained across the snow, its blue eyes fixed on the place they had occupied.

When they reached the bend, the vortex vanished behind the cliff.

The air was still cold. The world had not opened a secret passage because they had chosen restraint. It had simply allowed them to leave.

***

At their next shelter Enna found an older traveler repairing a pair of snowshoes. He knew of the scholars who had searched Everwinter's ruined places and could direct Velen toward the safer approaches to their collections.

“One of them is called Jorvald,” he said. “Keeps books and speaks cautiously, if he speaks at all.”

Velen went very still.

“You've met him?”

“Not lately. A messenger from the northern stations brought a note with his name. There's a route he sometimes uses when the upper snow is firm.”

The traveler gave them the station, not a promise of finding the scholar. Enna copied it onto their map beside a pair of caution marks. The direct upper road was under fresh snow and had been reported unsafe. There were other ways to make inquiries without crossing the Warden's ground.

That night Brunti set the cave drawing beside the letters from home.

The memorial wall's problem remained a problem of joined materials, two quiet lives pressed beneath one casing, and a choice about how to keep the resting rooms safe. Aetherion's old sketch had given her a practical lead. The mountain had not improved it by showing her ancient bones or luminous marks.

Velen sat across the table.

“I thought the cave would make things clearer,” they said.

“It did.”

“Did it?”

“We know what is there. We know what it doesn't show us.”

“That is a disappointing kind of clarity.”

Brunti considered the complaint. It was one she might have made herself when she first left the Skyland.

The ice had kept bones from crumbling and lettering from vanishing. Yet without the lives that made them, neither could tell the whole story. An archive could protect what remained. It could not make the missing part appear through patience or desire.

“Would Jorvald know more?” Brunti asked.

“He may know a language we don't, or where a better copy exists.”

“And if he doesn't?”

“We'll have another name beside the answer we could not find.”

Brunti looked at the page with its shaded gap. It reminded her of the space Sira had left in the displaced families' records, unfilled until a witness could speak for it.

Outside the shelter, snow began to fall again, softly enough that no one rose to check the path.

Velen reached for a fresh sheet.

This time, before writing anything, they left a broad blank space at the top.

Brunti watched the pen hover there and did not ask them to fill it.
