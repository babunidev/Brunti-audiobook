# Chapter Twenty-Six — The Voices Beneath

Brunti took another step.

The stone received the weight of her body. She felt it through the mineral seams of her feet, through the careful arrangement of crystal and metal that held her upright.

There was no sound.

She tried again.

The small click of a loose fitting at her belt vanished before it could become an echo. She touched the hammer against the rock. The wood of its handle gave a faint pulse to her hand, but the blow made no noise.

The silence was not emptiness.

A sound existed somewhere ahead.

It came so gently that Brunti did not recognize it as singing until a second note joined the first.

She raised her head.

The trees hung upside down.

Their great trunks disappeared into a dark height without a visible ceiling. Roots, or what she had taken for roots from the farther passage, stretched upward into nothing. Their branches reached toward the ground in long crooked lengths, moving slowly though the air around her was still.

At first the voices seemed far away.

One line rose, low and sorrowful. Others entered around it, some reaching the same tone and others falling beneath. The music passed between the suspended trunks with a strange clarity. Brunti could hear individual notes even though her own footsteps remained swallowed.

She wondered whether she was hearing one singer, many singers, or something without a throat at all.

The cavern did not answer.

The song continued.

***

The nearest hanging tree ended several lengths above the ground.

Its branches reached toward a floor of black stone crossed by shallow seams. Between the branch tips hung pale fibers, so fine that they seemed to float without settling.

Brunti held out her hand.

A strand touched her fingers and dissolved into dampness.

She looked toward the song.

It had drawn nearer without growing louder. The separate voices remained impossible to count. A low phrase seemed to pass along the trunks while a high line lingered above it, repeating the end of something whose beginning had been lost.

Then Brunti heard a name.

At least, she thought she did.

The sound came between two longer notes, almost hidden beneath the music. It might have been a word from a language she did not know. It might have been no word at all.

She waited for its return.

The next phrase bent away.

Another broken syllable appeared, close enough to resemble a name remembered from childhood.

Brunti took out her notebook.

She had written names in it before. Beside the names of travelers she had noted where they spoke, who had repeated their accounts, and which details they claimed to have seen themselves. Velen had taught her to separate a person's own words from the cleaner sentences another listener might supply afterward.

She wrote:

*Voices heard. Number uncertain.*

She tried to describe the word she had heard.

Her pencil remained still.

It would have been easy to write it as a familiar name and then forget that she had chosen the spelling herself.

She left the space blank.

The music shifted.

A high, thin voice held a note that Brunti felt deep in the crystal running beneath her sternum. Another answered it after an interval so familiar that her hand tightened upon the book.

Two sounds.

A pause.

The old memorial had done that when she first came to work beneath its casing.

The two rhythms had seemed to come from different places beneath one old casing. Velen's incomplete drawing suggested how two records might have been housed together, but neither of them had learned whose stones they were. One person's memory could not be made into a continuation of another's.

Brunti had once mistaken their overlapping resonance for a problem a better brace could solve.

Here, among the inverted trees, a similar pause appeared within the song.

Her whole body seemed ready to complete the pattern.

The thought arrived almost as a relief: the same interval, carried across so much of Morkahja, as if the thing she sought had followed her into the deepest forest.

She counted.

The phrase came again, but the pause had lengthened.

She counted a third time.

Only one voice remained, the others passing beneath it too softly to distinguish.

Brunti lowered the notebook.

This music was not her memorial.

The resemblance had been real to her ears. That did not make it evidence.

She knew the distinction.

Knowing it did not quiet the part of her that wanted the song to repeat.

***

A face appeared in the bark above her.

She had mistaken it for a knot until the next phrase of music made its mouth move.

Brunti stepped backward.

The face remained fixed in the dark trunk, the grain of the wood drawn into a brow, cheeks and lips. Its eyes seemed open but did not blink. Its expression held grief with an intensity that no living face could have sustained without changing.

The lips parted.

A line of melody rose.

Farther along the hanging wood, another face emerged from shadow. Then another.

The branches swayed. As they moved, their shadows slipped across features that had not been visible a moment earlier: a mouth drawn in terror, a forehead creased with longing, eyes set deep within a smooth expanse of bark.

There were too many to count.

Their mouths moved together and apart with the choir. Some seemed to sing without opening their lips. Others stretched into shapes too broad to belong to familiar bodies.

Brunti could not tell whether the faces had been carved.

She could not tell whether they were living in the wood.

She remembered the accounts of the Hollow Choir that had reached the libraries outside Keldor. The records disagreed about what sang. Some named trapped spirits. Others spoke of an abyss that imitated memory as water imitates the sky.

Neither account could be tested from where she stood.

A face turned its gaze toward her.

It did not move in any way she could measure. Its eyes had been directed elsewhere, and now they seemed to hold hers.

Brunti shifted to the left.

The gaze followed.

She looked toward another face. That one watched her too.

So did the next.

She tried to find an expression that changed with the song, some ordinary sign of attention passing from one thought to another.

The faces remained sorrowful.

Their mouths moved.

Their eyes followed.

A line rose through the cavern, carried by a voice that almost sounded like a child trying to finish a tune.

The memory came unbidden: a small Verdani boy beneath the lantern at Elda's shelter, teaching a few notes to a girl whose walking stick was too large for her hands.

He had known only part of the song.

She had told him it was enough.

Brunti heard those same notes, or something her mind fitted to them, within the distant voices.

She had been present at the shelter. She could have described the children's bench, the lamp, the sound of their different endings.

This cavern could not be evidence that their voices had traveled here.

She turned away from the face.

The song continued behind her.

***

She found a shallow notch between two rises of stone where the suspended branches could not reach.

There she stopped to inspect her left wrist.

The old fracture had widened along a crystal join. Its edges held, but a fine tremor crossed the mineral beneath whenever she lifted her pack. Damp had entered seams that normally rested in warmth and open air. Her food stores were nearly gone.

A part of her wanted to remain in the notch until the singing stopped.

The voices showed no sign of ending.

She took what mineral mixture remained and stirred it into a little water at the bottom of her flask. Its taste was rough and faintly sweet, an ordinary prepared nourishment that seemed absurdly out of place beneath the hanging forest.

It did not restore her strength all at once.

She allowed herself to rest.

The stone held her weight. From the part of her body pressed against it, a muted vibration traveled through her joints, just enough to remind her that she remained solid even when the silence refused to carry her steps.

The choir rose and fell.

Brunti closed her eyes.

Behind the notes she thought she heard Darrik asking a question in his dry, patient voice.

*Which wall?*

It was a question he had asked years ago while teaching her to distinguish sound stone from stone suited to a particular burden.

She remembered her own impatience.

*For a wall.*

*Which wall?*

The song carried the intervals.

Brunti opened her eyes.

She could not say whether she had remembered his actual voice or assigned it to a sound that already resembled speech.

The lesson itself was real. It had shaped her hands and the way she measured the places where people slept.

No new lesson came from the choir.

When she rose, she left the face-filled trunks where they hung and searched for a path between the stone ridges.

The nearest mouth opened as she passed.

A clear note reached her, held so long that she could no longer decide where it had begun.

She did not turn back.

***

The music thinned gradually.

First the higher voices faded. Then the low line lost its harmony and became a distant sound carried somewhere above the passage. Brunti could still hear it when she stopped, but she could not locate it by turning her head.

The stone descended.

A cold draft moved through the gap ahead, and with it came the scent of wet earth.

At the bottom of the slope, something reflected the faint light.

Black water lay between low stones.

The first pool was scarcely wider than Brunti's shoulders. Its surface showed a dark sky scattered with dim pale points, though there was no sky above her. The cavern roof continued over the marsh, lost in roots and shadow.

Brunti stood at the edge.

A second pool stretched beyond the first.

Then another.

Water and mud filled the valley beneath the abyss, interrupted by narrow ridges of stone and half-sunken shapes that rose from the surface like people standing very still.

The air smelled of decay and damp ground. Somewhere water dripped, though its sound appeared to come from beneath the pools rather than the ceiling.

She studied her reflection.

A Grimnir face looked back at her.

Dark mineral planes, pale seams, the shine of crystal near her cheek. Her pack strap crossed one shoulder; her hammer hung at the other side.

For a moment the image matched her movements.

Then it smiled.

Brunti had not.

The reflected mouth opened just enough to change its expression. The eyes remained fixed upon hers while the water around them scarcely stirred.

She stepped back.

The smile did not follow.

The face in the pool continued to watch the place where she had stood.

***

Brunti tested the nearest patch of ground with her hammer's handle.

It was firm enough to carry her weight, though mud had gathered along its edges. Beyond it, a strip of stone rose from the water and curved toward another bank.

She could cross by keeping to the raised pieces.

She crouched to look again at the first pool.

Her reflection was still there, but the face had changed.

One eye appeared missing. The socket was hollow, filled only with dark water beneath a ridge of stone.

Brunti touched her own cheek.

Both eyes were present.

The reflection's mouth moved.

No sound followed.

She stood.

Farther along, another pool caught her image with its head turned away. A thin line of damage ran down the reflected wrist, deeper than the fracture she carried. The mineral seam had opened so far that the whole hand seemed ready to fall apart.

Brunti held up her hand.

The actual join remained narrow and intact enough to move.

In the water, the damaged fingers curled with painful slowness.

She lowered her arm.

The pool continued to show the injury.

The reflection did not answer when she spoke.

She had not expected it to.

The urge to ask was there all the same.

If the water could show a different state of her body, perhaps it could show a different state of the memorial at home: the brace never fitted, the damaged wall repaired correctly, the two dead Grimnir never crowded beneath a single casing.

The thought came with startling tenderness.

She knew how much she had wanted such a picture.

She looked again.

The pool showed her face with both eyes, but its expression was unfamiliar. The reflected lips had drawn tight with a bitterness she could feel no precise equivalent of in her own features.

It was not a view of home.

It was another image of herself.

No more.

***

A soft sob rose beneath her feet.

Brunti stopped on the narrow stone.

The sound seemed so near that she bent to look along its edge. Black water lay on both sides, still except for a slow ripple moving toward the bank.

The sob came again.

It had the small, exhausted shape of someone trying not to be heard.

Brunti knelt.

“Is somebody there?”

Her voice traveled only a short distance over the water.

Nothing answered.

She reached toward the surface, then stopped before her fingers touched it.

The last people she had helped in Keldor had been able to speak to her. The injured woman had asked to see the door. The lost girl had asked whether someone would take away her stick. The boy had wanted to know whether he would be made to sing.

Help had begun with answers that belonged to them.

Here she had only a sound.

The water gave no location for its source.

She listened from the firm stone for a long time.

The sobbing seemed to move when she did.

She shifted toward the bank; it moved beneath her.

She backed away; it remained directly below.

At last Brunti stood. She could not find anyone to lift from the water, and she would not enter a pool with no visible bottom because a sound had asked nothing of her.

She continued along the stones.

The crying followed.

***

The half-submerged figures emerged gradually.

One stood waist-deep in black water, its head turned toward the empty passage. Its features had eroded until Brunti could barely make out where eyes might once have been. Faint grooves crossed the chest and arms.

Beyond it stood another.

Then several more.

Some leaned. Some appeared to kneel beneath the water. The nearest figures bore shapes that could have been robes or merely folds in damaged stone.

Brunti approached one from the safe bank.

The upper portion was smooth with wear. Its hands, if they had been hands, rested against a rounded shape at its center. Beneath the water the body widened into shadow.

She wondered whether it had been carved.

The faint grooves suggested deliberate work. But she had seen roots assume the forms of ribs and mouths in Keldor's upper forest, and the abyss below it had offered no reason to trust resemblance.

The stone might have been shaped.

It might have been changed.

If it had once belonged to a living person, there was no reliable name left in the material she could inspect.

She wanted to touch it.

A Grimnir stone worker could sometimes read a tool's angle in a shallow cut. She might learn whether the grooves had been made with a chisel or drawn slowly through a softer substance.

The water between her and the figure was black and deep.

She remained on the bank.

A sob rose beneath her feet.

She looked down.

Her reflection stood directly below, though there was no pool beneath the solid stone.

The image had both hands lifted, as if trying to press against a surface above its head. Its mouth was open.

Brunti stepped away.

The stone remained firm.

The image vanished.

She took out the notebook, but her wrist trembled too sharply to hold the pencil steady.

She rested the book against her knee.

*Stone figures. Origin unknown.*

*Reflections move independently.*

She paused.

*Cry heard beneath solid ground. Source not found.*

The words looked inadequate.

They were also the limits of what she could honestly say.

***

A little farther through the mire, she came to a broad pool whose surface lay perfectly still.

She meant to pass without looking.

Then she saw the memorial wall.

It stood in the water's reflection where Brunti's own body should have been. The two stones were held within a shared casing. She could see the very point where she had tried to fit the brace, the narrow corner that had first made the old join unsafe.

The image was exact enough to stop her.

She had seen that corner a hundred times.

The bracket she had made ran beneath it.

In the pool, the bracket was straight.

No pressure crossed the seam.

The wall seemed secure.

Brunti moved nearer.

The reflection changed.

Now the casing had been opened. Two separate memorial seats lay beneath it, clearly labeled. The old toolmarks were visible in clean lamplight, and no one had to guess whether the records belonged together.

Then the image changed again.

The room was empty.

The stones were gone.

Brunti felt the old fear rise: that moving the memorial would mean abandoning the people remembered there, that a safe transfer would become a kind of erasure.

She crouched at the bank.

The image brightened.

She reached toward it without touching.

For a heartbeat she thought a hand had risen from within the room. Darrik's hand, perhaps, setting a tool against the old casing.

But when the water stirred, the hand became the outline of Brunti's own hammer against her pack.

The wall vanished.

Her face returned to the pool, eyes dark, mouth drawn into an expression of grief she had not chosen.

The water could offer images her mind recognized.

It could not tell her which were true.

The knowledge came with no relief.

She had crossed so far for certainty that even a beautiful lie had begun to feel like a gift.

***

The way out of the mire was not marked.

A shallow rise of cracked stone crossed the water, but several sections had sunk beneath the black surface. Brunti could see other banks beyond it. Their positions shifted with each pool's reflection, making the distance between them difficult to judge.

She tested the stones one at a time.

At the first gap she lowered the hammer handle into the water. It touched something solid just below the surface. She stepped across.

At the second, the handle found no bottom.

She chose another route.

The effort of moving her mineral body from one secure patch to another began to wear at the fracture in her wrist. Once she slipped against wet stone and felt a sharp vibration travel through the crystal join.

She held still until the tremor eased.

No one was coming to steady her.

Sira would not appear with a song that made the water safe. Velen would not materialize with a copied map that made the reflections agree.

Brunti had left them behind.

That fact remained true whether or not the mire chose to show her their faces.

She used the broadest stones and kept her movements small.

When the far bank finally rose, she climbed onto it and rested against a dry ledge. The smell of decay lingered. The air above the water was colder than before.

She looked back.

The stone figures stood motionless in the marsh.

The nearest pool showed a pale sky that had never existed over this place.

Beneath the stone where she sat, somebody seemed to weep.

Brunti closed her notebook.

She could not give the sound a name.

She left it unnamed.

***

The road began where the mud ended.

It was made of cracked black stone, laid in broad slabs whose edges remained remarkably true despite the corruption surrounding them. The surface was dry. Its joints formed a clear line forward into darkness.

Brunti placed her foot upon the nearest slab.

For the first time in what seemed like days, the ground answered her weight with an ordinary, predictable solidity.

She stood still and felt the firmness travel through her body.

A road.

Not a root that had become a bridge.

Not a pool that showed another face.

A road cut and laid by hands, or at least by something capable of making an even edge.

Brunti looked along it.

Hooded figures were moving in the distance.

At first she saw only a small company, perhaps a dozen travelers crossing beneath the dark. Then she noticed others beyond them, and more beyond those, until the line seemed to fill the road as far as she could see.

They carried lanterns.

Every lantern was extinguished.

No light shone from the metal frames; still the figures held them with the care of people bringing small flames through wind.

Their robes concealed their faces.

They walked in complete silence.

Brunti heard the stone beneath her own feet when she crossed the first slab. The sound was faint, but it existed.

The marchers made none.

***

She stood beside the road and watched the figures pass.

One after another moved by at an unchanging pace. Their steps rose and fell together, as if all were obeying a measure too slow or too deep for Brunti to hear.

Some were tall and narrow beneath their robes.

Others carried broader shoulders. One had a gait that suggested a heavy leg. Another held its lantern close to the chest, though there was no flame to shield.

None turned toward her.

Brunti tried to count the procession.

She reached fifty, then lost the number when the line divided around a broken section of road and rejoined beyond it without slowing.

She began again.

The figures passed too steadily for counting to matter.

There were hundreds within sight.

There might have been thousands beyond.

No beginning came into view behind them, and no end appeared ahead.

Brunti thought of the last funeral procession she had seen at home.

The Skyland had moved slowly that morning, its inhabitants gathering in the central passage while a memorial stone was carried to its resting place. A young Grimnir had walked beside the bearers, one hand pressed against a smooth mineral surface that still carried an echo of the person being remembered.

There had been pauses.

People had stepped out of the way for one another.

Someone had stumbled, and another had offered an arm.

The procession in Blackwood contained none of those small human arrangements.

Everyone moved together.

No one seemed to need anyone else.

Brunti raised her hand to the nearest figure.

“Can you hear me?”

The hood did not turn.

She stepped closer, keeping out of the path.

“I don't know where this road leads.”

The figure passed.

A second did the same.

A third carried an empty lantern whose frame had been worked into a shape like falling leaves.

It went on without a sound.

Brunti stood in the dark beside the black stone, surrounded by travelers who would not acknowledge her presence.

She wondered what it would mean to be part of a procession without ever knowing whose departure it marked.

***

The road curved toward a colossal shape in the distance.

At first Brunti thought it was another cliff, a dark wall rising beyond the marchers. Then a thin line of structure appeared against the gloom, high and angular, too large to identify as a building.

All the lanterns pointed toward it.

The figures continued in perfect unison.

Brunti followed along the edge of the road.

She kept enough distance to watch without joining the line. Whenever a stone slab had cracked, the marchers stepped over the gap with the same measured motion.

Their coordination did not falter.

She thought she recognized a familiar movement.

One of the figures ahead held its left shoulder slightly lower than the right, the way Brunti did when the strap of her pack pulled against the old fracture beneath her wrist.

She stopped.

The figure moved on.

Another, farther ahead, carried its lantern with the same measured care she used when holding an object whose weight might shift suddenly in her hand.

The gestures were not rare.

Travelers everywhere learned to protect a sore arm or steady an uncertain burden.

Yet the thought had entered her mind before she could test it.

She searched the line again.

A broad figure near the front had the height and build of a Grimnir.

Its shoulders were squared beneath the robe. Its gait was firm, each step carried from the heel through the weight of the foot in a manner that Brunti recognized more intimately than any face.

Her own movement.

She watched it lift a hand.

Beneath the sleeve, a faint line of pale mineral appeared at the wrist.

Brunti's breath stopped for a moment.

The figure did not look back.

It walked on.

***

She tried to reach the front.

At first the distance seemed manageable. The broad-shouldered figure moved perhaps twenty paces ahead, separated from her by a line of hooded marchers carrying unlit lanterns.

Brunti lengthened her stride.

The marchers did not hurry.

She passed one, then another, keeping to the open edge of the road. Her footsteps sounded against the cracked stone, the only sound among the moving thousands.

The figure ahead did not draw nearer.

She tried again.

The road seemed to stretch.

Between one glance and the next, another rank of marchers stood between Brunti and the familiar shoulders. It was impossible to say where they had come from. The line remained orderly, and none of its walkers appeared to have changed pace.

She continued until the fracture in her wrist sent a sharp tremor through her arm and the mineral joints of her knees began to resist.

Then she stopped.

The broad figure was still near the front.

It moved its head in a small, habitual gesture that belonged to Brunti. She had done it since childhood when she wished to look upward without shifting the weight of something held in both hands.

There could be other Grimnir who moved that way.

There could be memories shaped into likenesses by this place.

It might be an image she had made from a robe and a shared rhythm.

She knew all of that.

Still, she wanted to see its face.

Not to ask for prophecy.

Not, she told herself, to learn her future.

Only to determine whether it was truly hers.

The distinction grew thinner each time she repeated it.

***

Brunti stepped into the procession.

A hooded figure approached, lantern hanging from one long arm. She stood directly in its path, making herself as visible and solid as she could.

It did not stop.

At the last moment, the marcher's course shifted a little. The figure passed so close that the edge of its robe touched Brunti's metal wrist guard.

She felt a cool brush of cloth.

Or the movement of air between two bodies.

The lantern moved past her shoulder without striking it.

A second figure came.

It altered its path by the same small distance and continued.

Brunti reached toward the sleeve of the third.

Her fingers did not close.

The figure moved beyond her hand as though her intention had no more weight than a falling leaf.

She did not seize its hood.

She already understood that reaching for a concealed face did not give her the right to reveal it. More importantly, nothing in the marchers' movements suggested that touching one could produce a reliable answer.

She stepped back to the road's edge.

The line flowed together again.

There was no disturbance where she had stood.

Brunti looked down at her own hands.

They were solid.

The hammer was still at her belt.

The old fracture remained.

She had not become one of the figures.

The image of herself ahead had not slowed.

She wondered whether the procession had passed around her, through the space she occupied, or along a road that obeyed some other measure of distance.

She had felt cloth.

She had not held anyone.

That was all she could tell.

***

A long memory came to her as the line passed.

She was young again, standing in a workshop above the clouds. Darrik had placed two stones beside one another and asked her to listen.

She had closed her eyes and waited for the faint difference between them.

One stone carried a short, bright note.

The other answered with a slower resonance, softer at its beginning.

She had announced that they belonged together because the two notes formed a pleasant interval.

Darrik had asked her what they were meant to support.

She had not known.

He had told her that beauty could reveal a possibility, not a right to join two things forever.

Brunti could remember his hands on the stone, the light across the workshop, the slight roughness of the second piece.

Now the procession made the same remembered interval with its footsteps.

At least, Brunti heard it.

There was no sound from the marchers at all.

She looked toward the broad figure near the front.

It lifted its left hand.

She felt her own left wrist respond with a sharp tremor.

For a heartbeat the likeness seemed perfect.

She wanted to follow it farther.

She wanted to know what happened when it reached the immense structure ahead.

The wanting was so powerful that she almost forgot the road itself had offered no proof of anything beyond resemblance.

She took out the notebook.

The last page was crowded with short observations and spaces where she had refused to guess.

She wrote:

*Saw a figure resembling me among the marchers.*

She paused.

The pencil trembled.

*It was ahead of me.*

The sentence seemed too simple for what she had seen.

She added:

*Could not reach or identify it.*

She closed the notebook.

The likeness was still walking.

***

The procession passed beneath an arch formed by two immense slabs of black stone.

Beyond it, the road descended toward the colossal structure. Brunti followed beside the marchers, no longer trying to outrun them. The arch held a chill that passed into the seams of her mineral body as she walked beneath it.

She looked back once.

The road behind remained full.

The silent figures continued forward from a darkness with no visible source. Their extinguished lanterns swayed together, their hoods concealing faces that might or might not have existed beneath the cloth.

The figure like Brunti moved nearer to the structure ahead.

Not because she had caught up.

Because the road itself had carried all of them onward.

The shape grew larger.

It was no simple cliff. Its dark surfaces rose in lines that resembled immense arches, with a shadowed central opening through which the marchers passed without hesitation.

Brunti could not see beyond the threshold.

A faint movement seemed to pass through the stone, too deep to be a sound and too slow to call a tremor.

She placed a hand against the nearest edge of the road.

The pulse moved beneath the slabs.

Once.

A pause.

Then again.

She knew how easily she could make those two movements belong to the memorial at home. Its old casing still concealed who had left those rhythms. Nothing beneath this road could name them for her.

Brunti withdrew her hand.

The pulse continued.

The broad-shouldered marcher passed beneath the dark arch and disappeared.

She did not see its face.

Behind her the procession moved forward.

Before her lay the opening in the great structure, and with it the last of the certainty she had believed she could carry into this place.

Brunti stepped toward the arch.

The dead lanterns vanished one by one into the dark.

She followed, though the figure she had wanted to question had given her nothing but its back.
