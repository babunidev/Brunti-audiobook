# Chapter Five — The Lives of the Vale

“Does your road always begin with a dance?” Brunti asked.

“Only when there is someone willing to dance,” Sira said.

They had been walking since the early light passed over the Ethereal Glades. The path widened and narrowed as it followed the lower hills, bordered by flowers whose colors changed from white to blue where the shade deepened. Brunti had already caught herself setting too quick a pace twice. The third time Sira simply stopped to adjust the strap of her bag, and Brunti came back to discover that she had been walking alone for several minutes.

“I wasn't trying to leave you behind.”

“I know. You were going somewhere.”

“Aetherion.”

“Yes. It has been there a very long time. It may survive another hour without us.”

Brunti regarded her.

Sira's horns were threaded with fresh blossoms, and she carried her messages in a weatherproof fold of cloth against her side. When she spoke, the waterlike shimmer of her skin changed with the light. She had told Brunti that she was carrying words from communities without regular scribes, and that some of the messages were not written down because their senders had wished them spoken in person.

“Wouldn't a written copy be safer?” Brunti had asked.

“For the words, perhaps.”

“For what else are you carrying them?”

“The silence before they were said. Sometimes that matters.”

Brunti had given the matter considerable thought and remained unconvinced.

By late morning they reached a clearing where a narrow field track joined the main road. Someone had hung a strip of dull cloth between two posts at knee height. It looked too low to stop a cart and too insubstantial to turn an animal.

A woman sat beside one of the posts, sharpening a short knife.

“Don't take the hollow path,” she said without greeting.

Brunti stopped. “Why?”

“Something is using it.”

“What kind of something?”

The woman raised her eyes. Her hair was bound tightly against her neck, and an old scratch ran through the skin above one eyebrow. She wore a tracker’s leather cuffs, though the rest of her clothing was patched in a dozen different colors.

“The sort that will take your argument personally.”

Sira smiled. “We were told the road was clear.”

“It is, if you stay on it. The hollow is not the road.”

Brunti looked toward the lower track. Crushed stalks lay across it, mixed with dark patches of churned earth. A few deep prints had filled with yesterday's rain.

The woman followed her gaze.

“Call me Mara,” she said. “I mark the animals' passages for the caravans. A driver moved my warning yesterday because he thought the best route ought to be wide enough for him.”

“Did he get through?”

“He came back with half a wheel.”

Brunti knelt beside a print.

The imprint was broad, much larger than any ordinary farm animal’s. Soil had been thrown forward in a heavy crescent.

“You should make the marker higher.”

“I did. Something knocked it down.”

“Then stronger.”

Mara gave her a look.

Sira said, “Perhaps the marker is not the problem.”

Brunti opened her mouth, then closed it.

From somewhere in the wooded hollow came a sudden crack of breaking brush.

Mara was on her feet before the sound finished.

“Up,” she said.

***

A massive boar burst between the trees, dark hide bristling with mud and old scars. Two sharp horns curved from its head above a jaw lined with long, stained fangs. It drove through the undergrowth with such force that branches snapped against its shoulders. Every stride sent a dull impact through the soil.

Brunti felt the first vibration through the stone of her feet.

She took one step in front of Sira and drew her hammer.

“Not that way,” Mara called.

The boar lowered its head. Its eyes fixed on the opening between the two posts.

Brunti could have struck it as it passed. Her weapon had been made for forceful work, and its edge was heavy enough to break stone. She judged the distance and the angle with practiced speed. The creature was coming too quickly to stop at a spoken warning.

“Brunti,” Sira said, close behind her. “Look where it's going.”

A narrow strip of crushed grass ran from the hollow across the road toward a dark passage between two boulders. The boar's tracks had cut it deeper over several days. They passed directly between Brunti and the open field beyond.

They stood on its route.

Brunti shifted her feet, caught Sira's arm and moved them both toward the taller bank. Mara sprang clear at the other side.

The boar thundered past. Its hot breath carried the smell of crushed roots and wet hide. One horn struck the nearest post, splintering the wood, but the animal did not turn toward the three travelers. It tore across the road and vanished between the boulders, leaving the cloth warning tangled in the grass.

For a while nothing moved.

Brunti held her hammer ready.

From the hollow behind them came a quieter rustle. Smaller prints crossed the damaged path, then disappeared beneath the trees. Mara crouched to examine them.

“You didn't have to hurt it,” she said.

“I could have stopped it.”

“Perhaps.”

Brunti looked toward the boulders.

The Bloodfang was gone. The broken post lay at her feet. Whatever it had been protecting, it had passed without lingering to fight.

Sira retrieved the fallen cloth.

“It wasn't charging *us*,” she said.

“Not until we stood in front of it.”

“That is sometimes how being in the way begins.”

Brunti returned her hammer to its belt loop.

She helped Mara fit the warning strip to a higher branch where travelers could see it before turning off the road. Then she examined the broken post, found the sound part of its base, and wedged it upright with two stones.

Mara watched the work.

“You could have been useful ten minutes earlier,” she remarked.

Brunti tightened the fastening.

“I am useful now.”

“That will do.”

***

They ate beneath the shade of an old tree while Mara repaired a folded field chart.

Its pages were made from thick cloth coated in wax. Marks ran across them in different colors, some showing roads, others water, pasture and animal trails. At the corners Mara had drawn the shapes of hooves and claws.

Brunti studied the broad mark beside the hollow.

“Bloodfang?”

“Yes. Or at least the beast travelers call Bloodfang.”

“You don't know whether it's the same one?”

“I know the one that passed us has a scar under its left shoulder. I've seen the mark in its tracks and heard of a similar animal farther west. That doesn't make them one beast.”

She turned the chart. On the opposite fold was a drawing of a chest.

“Another thing people confuse with a useful find,” she said.

Sira leaned closer.

Mara described a bronze treasure chest half-concealed among wildflowers on the Seraphic Plains. Age had turned its surface green. The sides held delicate patterns of curved lines, and the lock had fitted levers and tiny gears. A trader had found it at the edge of a flowering ridge, examined the mechanism and left it where it lay.

“Why leave it?” Brunti asked.

“Because he couldn't open it.”

“He might have found a matching key.”

“He might have found its owner.”

“Perhaps the owner was dead.”

“Perhaps.”

Mara looked at Brunti's hammer.

“Some people think anything they can break belongs to them. I prefer people who know the difference between opening a thing and being permitted to open it.”

Brunti took out her notebook and drew the chest from Mara's account. The bronze had weathered for years, but its workmanship remained distinct. She would have liked to see the lock.

She admitted as much.

Mara smiled. “At least you're honest about it.”

For the rest of the afternoon they traveled together, the tracker choosing a route above the lower animal passages. She had agreed to bring the two travelers as far as a spring where another road joined the route toward Eldertree Forest.

Before they left the shade she showed Brunti a drawing of a great boar with horns like polished gold.

“Goldhorn,” she said. “You see it once, if you're fortunate, and remember the light on those horns long after you've forgotten which way it ran.”

The animal in Mara's telling had burst from low vegetation with dust streaming from its hooves. Its gilded horns flashed as it lowered its massive head, too fast for the frightened traveler who had tried to stand and admire it.

“Did it attack the traveler?”

“It was charging. Whether it had selected him as its target is not something he stayed to learn.”

Mara laughed softly.

Brunti looked back toward the hollow where Bloodfang had passed.

She began to see why trackers drew routes before they drew creatures.

***

That evening a storm gathered beyond the western hills without reaching them. The light went amber under the clouds. They stopped beside a low stone wall and made a small fire from fallen wood.

Mara spread out her chart to dry.

“You've drawn all these?” Brunti asked.

“Most of them. Some were copied from others. I keep the names beside the hand that gave them.”

She pointed to a mark shaped like a deep split hoof.

“Charger. A great Nagar with crimson eyes and curved tusks. Fast enough to leave stones rolling behind it. A caravan driver said he saw the animal coming and had just time to get his people off the road. It did not stop. That's what makes the Charger dangerous: momentum.”

She moved her finger to a slower, broader imprint.

“The Elder was different. Heavy, scarred across its shoulders, with tusks worn by old fights. It stood under trees and watched a company crossing the clearing. They thought it had not noticed them. Then it scraped the earth once, and every bird in the branches took flight.”

“Did it chase them?” Sira asked.

“Only the ones who came closer.”

Brunti examined the marks. The Elder's tracks were farther apart than those of the Charger but deeper where the weight had settled.

“You can tell that from the earth?”

“I can tell some things from earth. Not an animal's entire history.”

As they spoke, the light dwindled, and Mara's account became less like a lesson than a series of roads opening out of the darkness.

She told them of the Berserk, a Nagar whose thunderous roar broke through a thicket before its body appeared. Its great tusks caught bark as branches snapped against its shoulders, and its heavy hooves flung earth backward without slowing. Hot vapor streamed from its nostrils as it threw its head, fixed its fierce eyes ahead and lowered its tusks for another charge. There was no cunning in that particular rush, only rage and great weight. Even a sturdy tree had been a poor shield for a traveler who chose the wrong side.

Then she showed them an ordinary Nagar Boar, mottled grey and brown so thoroughly that a hunter had mistaken its back for a patch of brush. The creature had risen out of the greenery one shoulder at a time, its broad nostrils testing the air and curled tusks catching the grass. One hoof pressed into the soil hard enough to tremble the stems, while its watchful eyes surveyed the clearing. Its shoulders tightened beneath grizzled bristles before it moved.

“No golden horns,” Mara said. “No tale worth singing at the market. Just an enormous animal, quite capable of killing you if you forgot it was there.”

Sira leaned back against the wall.

“I suspect most creatures prefer not to be sung about.”

“Most creatures have better things to do,” Mara replied.

A small rush of rain crossed the distant fields and moved away. The camp remained dry.

Mara showed Brunti another sketch. Here the boar's hide had been left pale, almost white. Old scars crossed its shoulders, and the horns were drawn sharp and hard, their worn edges marked by repeated blows.

“Bonecrusher. The name comes from what people claim those horns can break. I saw one from the far side of a ditch. The white hide caught the sun between green stems, and its eyes were red as hot coals. It snapped a fallen branch beneath one immense hoof and lowered its horned head. Even at that distance the ditch wall shed dust.”

Brunti traced the pale line around the drawn body.

“Did you cross to see it?”

“No.”

“Good,” Sira said.

Mara turned the cloth again.

There was the Nagar Humpback, so high-backed that its rounded ridge rose above the brush before the rest of it came into sight. A heavy neck carried great tusks between its powerful shoulders. Mara had watched one force its way between two trees, snag a low branch on a tusk and tear free with an impatient twist that showered leaves across its coarse hide. Its uneven hooves made loose stones shift beneath the brush.

Beside that drawing ran a narrow line of sharp projections.

“The Razorback,” she said. “You can hear it moving through dry growth before you see its face. A jagged row of horns runs along the spine. I saw one twist sharply around a fallen trunk without losing speed. Its hind hooves dug into the earth, throwing dust behind its shoulders while the horns passed scarcely a hand from the wood. The space behind a log is no safer than the space before it.”

Brunti regarded the pattern of the horns.

“I would have tried to get behind it.”

“Many people do,” Mara said. “That is why I draw the back.”

The last sketch took almost the whole page.

A colossal Nagar stood with towering horns above a lowered head. Great hooves pressed into broken ground. Mara had marked the shapes of falling grit along a nearby bank.

“Thunderhoof,” she said. “I didn't see the first one that came through the pass. I felt it. No wind at all, but the pebbles beside my sleeping place began to tremble. Each slow hoofbeat pressed the earth down until the bank shed another trickle of grit. Then it came from behind the rise, horns framing its head.”

“Did you move?”

“Very carefully. The first thing it did was stop at the sound of a snapping twig. Its head turned. Then it lifted one great hoof and held it above the earth before bringing it down with enough weight to shake the bank again.”

Brunti looked at the great broad hoof drawn near the edge of the chart.

All these animals belonged to a family of immense, dangerous beings. Yet the details were not interchangeable. The Charger might outrun an unready traveler. The Elder might wait until an approach became too close. The Razorback made a certain sort of escape worse. Thunderhoof announced itself through the ground.

Mara was not making a list of enemies to defeat.

She was teaching travelers how to remain alive among creatures that did not owe them a clear road.

Brunti found herself listening with more patience than she had brought to any account of treasure.

When Mara finished, she rolled the chart toward the fire.

“So,” Sira asked, “how do you know when to fight?”

Mara considered the question.

“When somebody leaves you no other way to keep another creature alive.”

“That is a hard rule,” Brunti said.

“It's only a guide. A rule needs to be more certain of the ground than I usually am.”

Sira smiled.

Brunti watched the fire catch on a piece of bark. The little flame bent in a passing draft and righted itself. She thought of the Bloodfang's horns cutting through the air and of her hammer rising in answer.

The road was wide enough for all of them, once she had moved.

***

Mara's stories did not end with the Nagar.

While the others prepared to rest, she spoke of a feline she had seen far to the east. It had crouched in grass that withered where pale sparks touched it. Its shoulders shifted as if their outline could not hold still, and for an instant one foreleg seemed to dissolve into light before gathering again in the shape of muscle and claw.

“A Zeelai,” she said. “Or that is the name the nearby village used.”

“What held it together?” Brunti asked.

“I don't know.”

“Was it sick?”

“I don't know that either. It had its eyes fixed on us. We did not wait to discover whether it meant to leap.”

Brunti wrote the name on a separate page. She did not draw a line between it and the Crumblegheists in Orven's earlier reports.

Sira had been quiet while Mara spoke. Now she unfastened one of the folded messages from her bag and checked that its wrapping was still dry.

“Is it urgent?” Brunti asked.

“It is important.”

“Those are not always the same.”

“No.” Sira glanced at her with a small smile. “You're learning.”

Brunti might have answered more sharply a few days before. Instead she offered to keep watch for the first part of the night.

Sira went to the side of the fire where the light was gentlest. Mara settled behind the stone wall. Brunti remained awake with one hand resting against the earth.

After a while she felt the footfalls of something enormous passing among the trees.

A Silvermane emerged in the moonlight.

The creature's fur was dark along its flanks and silver over the great ridge of its shoulders. It moved with a weight that broke small twigs beneath its paws. Its head turned, revealing a formidable jaw, and the pale light touched the long edges of its claws.

Brunti did not reach for her hammer.

The beast stood still. The fur along its back lifted, and its lips drew away from the teeth.

It was close enough that Brunti could hear the breath leaving its nostrils.

She knew how to strike. She knew where to move. But the Silvermane had not yet made the choice she feared.

Brunti lowered her shoulders and remained beside the cold side of the wall, making no movement toward it.

The beast looked once toward the campfire, then turned into the trees.

Only when its heavy steps faded did Brunti release the grip she had kept on the stone.

High above the valley, a wolf began to call.

The Yowler stood on a narrow rocky outcrop, mottled grey and brown against the stone. Its lean legs braced against the uneven ledge as a ragged howl traveled across the hollow and returned in broken echoes. A second, sharper cry followed. The wolf fell silent, one ear turned to the distant slope, the other to the valley below. Its pale eyes searched the ground beneath the outcrop, and its forelegs stiffened as it lowered its muzzle. A low growl replaced the fading echo.

The Silvermane did not come back.

Brunti listened to the yowl until the valley swallowed the final note.

***

In the morning Mara left them at the spring.

She gave Sira a marked route to the nearest shelter and made Brunti copy the place where the animal track crossed the main path.

“You're certain this way will take us toward Eldertree?” Brunti asked.

“Certain enough to tell you which landmarks you'll see. Not certain enough to promise what will be waiting between them.”

She rolled up her chart.

Sira thanked her and offered a short song in return. Mara listened without interrupting, then touched two fingers to her brow.

Brunti was preparing to give her the little dark pebble the Earthsinger had offered, but something stopped her. She had already imagined giving it to Nemi. Mara would have no use for an unexplained stone.

Instead she helped repair the leather thong on Mara's chart case. It had been worn thin at the buckle. Brunti used a piece from her toolkit, fitted it carefully and made certain the new fastening would not cut into the cloth.

Mara examined it.

“That,” she said, “I know what to do with.”

They parted while the morning mist still lay in the low ground.

Brunti and Sira followed the narrower track above the spring. It wound through flowers and scattered trees, rising toward a meadow where the grass grew tall enough to hide a resting animal.

Sira was telling Brunti about a river village whose people kept songs in the order they were spoken at gatherings. Brunti listened, trying to understand how a song could carry a name without writing it down.

A low growl stopped them.

At the edge of the grass stood a mother wolf, grey and brown fur blending into the stems. She had moved sideways across an opening in the greenery. Behind her, something small shifted. A cub's muzzle appeared, then vanished.

The mother's eyes remained fixed on the travelers.

Brunti's hand moved toward her hammer.

Then she saw the grass trembling behind the wolf's shoulders. More young shapes were hidden there, close to the den.

Sira stopped speaking.

Neither of them advanced.

Brunti looked for another route. The slope behind them was steeper, but it led toward the same line of trees. She could see a narrow space between two rocks where they might pass without coming near the den.

She stepped backward.

The mother wolf did not follow. Her low growl continued until Brunti and Sira were far enough away for the grass to close between them.

They took the rocky path.

A little farther on, a russet shape bounced above the meadow grass, landed awkwardly and disappeared. Two crimson eyes appeared among the stems, bright with curiosity. The young Redeye sneezed as a flower brushed its nose, shook its thick coat and pounced on something unseen in the soil.

Brunti smiled.

“Is it one of her cubs?” she whispered.

“We don't know,” Sira said.

They watched from a distance while the pup investigated an insect, a fallen leaf and its own shadow. It lifted one paw, ears unevenly raised, then vanished when the breeze swept grass across its muzzle.

Brunti put the question in her notebook without answering it.

Mother wolf with hidden young. A Redeye pup farther along the meadow. Relationship unknown.

Sira read over her shoulder.

“You could have left out the last line,” she said.

“I could.”

“Why didn't you?”

“Because I might forget that I didn't know.”

Sira did not reply at once.

Then she reached into her bag and drew out a small piece of dried fruit. She offered half to Brunti, who accepted it and added a pinch of mineral salt from her own supplies.

They ate as they walked.

***

By afternoon the longer trail had carried them beneath taller trees.

A broad band of shadow lay across the path, its edges softened by leaves. Far ahead the forest rose in layers, the crowns of ancient trees standing over the younger growth like dark mountains of living wood.

Sira walked beside Brunti now, matching her pace rather than asking her to slow.

“You could have made us a shorter road past the wolves,” she said.

“I could.”

“You might have fought them.”

“I might have.”

Sira looked ahead. “You didn't.”

Brunti considered what to say.

The old habit was to explain how easily she could have struck the Bloodfang, or how much danger the Silvermane had posed, or how a warrior's first duty was to protect anyone traveling beside her.

All of that was true enough.

But she had seen the mother wolf standing between them and her cubs. She had watched the little Redeye disappear into grass. She could still feel the ground moving beneath the Bloodfang as it hurried across its own passage.

“Nothing was asking to be conquered,” she said.

Sira touched the flowers woven among her horns. One had begun to wilt in the afternoon heat. She carefully removed it and laid it beneath a tree.

“That's a strange thing for a warrior to say.”

“Is it?”

“Some warriors would think so.”

Brunti walked on.

“Perhaps they should take longer roads.”

Sira laughed.

They passed under the first great branches of Eldertree Forest as the light began to turn green, and the sound of the open meadows softened behind them.
