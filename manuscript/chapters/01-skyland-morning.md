# Chapter One — Skyland Morning

The first wrong note sounded while Brunti was repairing a child's hammer.

It was a little thing, hardly more than a tap heard through the stone beneath her feet. She felt it in the narrow seams of crystal that crossed her palms. Then it passed, leaving the morning as it had been: the scrape of tools, the warm smell of grain and ground minerals from the breakfast stalls, and the deep, patient hum of the Skyland moving through the air.

Brunti turned the hammer in her hands.

The head had been shaped from a pebble of black ironstone, polished by many lessons. Its haft was too short for her grip. A pale crack ran along the binding where someone had tried to mend it with a loop of wire.

“Someone has been very inventive,” she said.

Nemi, who stood beside the workbench, looked pleased.

“Does that mean it is good?”

“It means you are inventive.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

Nemi considered this. Her small face was a pale arrangement of growing mineral plates, still smooth where Brunti's own had gathered ridges and dark seams. Light glimmered behind her eyes like sun caught in a shallow crystal. She was old enough to work beside the adults for part of the morning, and young enough to believe a hammer might forgive whatever was done to it.

“I made the wire hold,” she said.

“For three strikes.”

“Four.”

“The fourth is what brought it here.”

Brunti loosened the binding, cleaned the split and showed her how to wind a better fastening. Nemi leaned close, attentive to the sound the little tool made as the metal settled into place. Brunti could have finished the work in a moment. Instead she passed the haft back and let Nemi repeat it, poorly at first, then with a steadier hand.

Beyond the workshop arch, morning light moved across terraces of stone.

The Skyland was not an island broken from a mountain, although travelers sometimes called it that when they saw its shadow drifting over the country below. Its walls, bridges and hanging gardens had been planned, built and rebuilt by Grimnir hands over more lifetimes than Brunti could name. Metal frameworks gripped vast layers of carved rock. Crystal ribs carried their slow, measured vibrations to the sky-core anchors far beneath the inhabited streets. Here and there, an older wall had become the foundation of a younger room; a path could pass beneath a carving made by someone who had died centuries before the first stone of the path was laid.

The whole place traveled.

From the eastern terrace, clear weather offered a view of distant mountain summits and, below them, clouds with wide hollows full of blue. Other Skylands sometimes passed near enough to see their bridges and great cranes. Today the sky was open. The city moved so gently that a stranger might have mistaken its motion for the wind.

Brunti never did. When she laid her hand against a pillar, she could feel the work of its makers continuing beneath the surface.

She gave Nemi the repaired hammer.

“Test it against the practice block,” she said. “Not the arch.”

“I know.”

“You knew yesterday.”

“I know more now.”

Nemi went off with the solemnity of someone carrying an important instrument. Three steps later she broke into a run.

Brunti watched her disappear among the morning traders. Then she picked up the tray that had been waiting under her bench and went to find something to eat.

A woman at the nearest stall was grinding a grey-green mineral into a warm bowl of roots and barley. Brunti bought a portion, added a pinch of dark salt and sat on the low edge of a cistern. The food had a sweet, earthy smell; the mineral gave it a pleasing grain that caught for a moment against the fine stone of her teeth. Below the terrace, water passed through narrow channels toward the nurseries and communal baths.

A breeze touched the polished ridges of her face. She liked this hour, when the Skyland was full of work but no one had yet begun to hurry.

A familiar vibration came through the cistern wall.

Brunti lifted her head.

The memorial passage lay beyond the next courtyard. Its stones were part of the route she took most mornings, a quiet place where some people slowed their steps and others walked through without noticing they had done so. The wall held the preserved echoes of Grimnir who had reached the end of their lives and undergone the rites that kept something of their identity in memory stone.

It did not speak in words.

On certain mornings, when the wind crossed the outer vents at the proper angle, the passage gave a low, measured note. Brunti had known that sound since childhood. It was not anyone's voice, not precisely, but there was a contour to it that had become as familiar as the turn of a particular stair.

Today it answered itself.

One note. A pause. Then another, slightly late and slightly lower, trembling against the first.

Brunti set the bowl aside.

She waited for the sound to come again.

It did.

Across the courtyard a pair of stonecarvers stopped speaking. One glanced toward the memorial passage, listened a moment and resumed his conversation. The other kept watching the arch.

Brunti was already walking.

***

The memorial wall faced a narrow interior court where thin shafts of daylight entered through the roof. Dark plates of polished stone rose from floor to ceiling, fitted among older blocks with the care usually given to a delicate instrument. Names had been carved on many of them. Some inscriptions were fine enough to catch light only at an angle; others had been touched so often that their letters shone softly.

Brunti laid her palm against the nearest uncarved support.

The familiar note moved into her hand.

Then came the second one.

She followed it along the wall to a panel low enough to examine without a ladder. A slender metal cover ran beside the memorial stone, protecting a join between the old work and a later support. It sat a little proud of the surface.

That was new.

She pressed one fingertip against it.

The cover shifted.

Not much. Barely the width of a grain of sand. But beneath her touch came a small irregular tremor, the unmistakable answer of two parts that no longer bore their weight together.

Someone had repaired this joint long ago. The outer fastening was newer than the stone around it, though even its edges had softened with use. Brunti could not see where the oldest work ended.

She took out the thin measuring blade she kept in her work belt and fitted it against the gap. The blade trembled with the two notes.

A support had loosened. That seemed simple enough.

She looked along the passage. One of the older craft stewards could be called to examine the joint. Darrik would probably come himself. He would ask when she had first heard the change, what she had touched and whether she had checked the rooms behind the wall.

Brunti knew all those questions.

She also knew a loose support when she found one. Her training on the terrace watch had taught her to clear a path before anyone tested a failing bridge. But the memorial court was empty, the resting chamber was hidden behind another wall, and she mistook the absence of people in sight for the absence of risk.

There was a length of soft-worked metal in her satchel, left over from Nemi's hammer. It was broad enough to bridge the open section, narrow enough to fit beneath the lip of the cover. If she seated it carefully, the joint would hold until a proper repair could be arranged.

She knelt and tested the metal against the stone.

The second note faltered.

Brunti smiled to herself. There. It could be steadied.

She pressed the brace home with the heel of her hand.

The first note sharpened. The second fell into it, for one pleasing instant, and the wall seemed almost quiet.

Then something answered from the other side.

A hard, uneven knocking ran through the stone at her knees. It traveled along the floor, turned beneath the passage and vanished toward the resting chambers.

Brunti took her hand away.

The knocking came again.

She knew where it had gone. The communal resting rooms occupied the passage behind the memorial court, separated from it by a lattice of stone and metal designed to keep their soothing resonance even. Injured and exhausted Grimnir slept there on shaped beds, allowing their living mineral bodies to mend in peace.

Brunti put her palm flat against the floor.

The floor was answering her brace.

“No,” she said.

She tried to draw the metal out. It had caught under the cover. The wall shuddered when she pulled.

From beyond the passage came a sound unlike the steady tones of rest: a sharp cry, followed by the clatter of something striking stone.

Brunti abandoned the blade and ran.

The chamber door stood open. A caretaker was kneeling beside one of the resting beds, one hand held firmly against the shoulder of an older Grimnir whose dark stone face had drawn into painful stillness. Mineral water spilled from a small bowl at the bedside. The room's usual low hum had broken into short, conflicting pulses.

“Stop the passage,” the caretaker said, without looking up.

“I know.”

“Then do it.”

Brunti turned back. She had taken three strides when she realized what the caretaker had asked: not for an explanation, not for help with the patient, but for the sound to stop.

At the joint she found the metal still wedged beneath the cover.

She set both hands against the brace and pulled in the direction she had driven it. Nothing moved. She changed her grip, braced one knee against the floor and eased the strip sideways rather than forcing it farther into the seam.

It came free with a small, ugly scrape.

The knocking ended.

For a few breaths the passage held a silence so complete that Brunti could hear the wind passing through the high vents.

Then the old, low memorial note returned.

The second note followed it.

They were still there, separate and uneasy, but the violent passage of sound into the resting room had ceased.

Brunti held the strip of metal in her hand. It had bent where it crossed the unseen joint. She could feel the imperfect shape under her thumb.

In the chamber beyond, the caretaker spoke quietly to the injured resident. The reply was weak but clear.

Brunti did not go back to offer another repair.

She went to fetch Darrik.

***

Darrik arrived with three apprentices, a case of tools and the expression he wore when someone had presented him with a damaged stair that was still being used.

He did not ask Brunti what she had meant to do.

He asked what she had done.

She showed him the joint, the strip of metal and the mark it had left beneath the cover. She described the first unfamiliar note and the second, the test with her measuring blade, the brief quiet when she pressed the brace home.

She did not hurry through the part about the resting room.

Darrik listened with his hands against the wall.

He was broad even for a Grimnir, built of dark stone crossed by seams that shone like worn silver. His palms had been polished smooth by years of work. Around his wrists, old insets of different minerals marked repairs to his own body, fitted with the same exactness he demanded of the city's bridges.

When Brunti finished, he asked, “Did you check the other side before you seated the metal?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“The joint was moving.”

“It was.”

“I thought it would keep moving until it failed.”

“That might have been true.”

She waited.

“You did not know,” he said.

His voice held neither anger nor comfort. That made the words harder to receive.

Darrik opened his tool case. At his instruction, the apprentices placed dampening pads against the side supports and lifted the protective cover without touching the central stone. He listened to each part in turn, using a small hammer that rang so faintly Brunti felt the strikes more clearly than she heard them.

The memorial answered in two rhythms.

He worked until the wall's outer support could be held without carrying the uncertain vibration toward the resting chambers. Then he ordered the neighboring passage closed to ordinary traffic. The memorial stones themselves remained untouched.

A few residents gathered at the far end of the court.

One was an older memory-keeper who used to bring young apprentices here and ask them to listen before she told them whose names were carved on the wall. She stood very still while the pads were fitted.

“Will it go quiet?” she asked.

“The living rooms will,” Darrik said.

“And this?”

He glanced at the memorial.

“This needs more than a quick joint.”

Brunti looked down at the piece of bent metal in her hand.

The caretaker from the resting chamber entered the court. The injured resident had settled again, she said. He would need time and a quieter bed, but the sudden strain had passed.

Darrik thanked her. Then he asked Brunti to walk with him.

They went through an older part of the Skyland, where the floors bore the shapes of tools that had been laid down before the stone had fully cooled. A broad window looked out over the clouds. Beyond it, a lattice of crystal ran beneath a bridge and disappeared into the city's lower works.

The Skyland's deeper hum continued without interruption. Brunti took some comfort from that, though she knew it did not excuse what had happened in the resting room.

“The wall has been mended before,” she said.

“Yes.”

“There is a second seam beneath the cover. I could feel it.”

“So could I.”

“Then there is something to find.”

“There may be.” Darrik stopped beside the window. “There is also a room in use behind it.”

“I know.”

“You know now.”

Brunti faced him. “I removed the brace.”

“You did.”

“I told you what I did.”

“You did that too.”

He looked at her for a long moment, then rested his hand against the window frame. In the crystal beneath his palm, the Skyland's movement sounded deep and steady.

“You were right to stop,” he said. “You were right to tell me. Neither makes the first choice safe.”

Brunti had an answer ready. She could have said she had been trying to keep the memorial from cracking, that another moment of delay might have been worse, that the whole city was built by Grimnir willing to trust their hands.

But he already knew all of those things.

“What happens to the wall?” she asked instead.

“We isolate the panel until the council can inspect it. At the next convergence the outer support can be replaced without the living chambers depending on that old joint.”

“And the memorial?”

“We move it to a protected archive setting while we work.”

“You mean take it out of the passage.”

“Yes.”

“People stop there every morning.”

“They can stop elsewhere.”

“That is not quite the same.”

“No,” Darrik said. “It isn't.”

He was quiet for a moment.

“The stones can be preserved. The room behind them must remain safe. Those are two things we can do. We do not yet know whether we can do them in the old arrangement.”

Brunti looked back toward the court, though the memorial was several corridors away.

She imagined the passage without its low note. Nemi would one day cross it without hearing what Brunti had heard since childhood. The memory-keeper might come to the wall and find new stone, correctly fitted and silent.

A protected archive would keep the old pieces from harm. Brunti understood that. What troubled her was the thought of the voices becoming something stored rather than something met along an ordinary walk.

“Who fitted the earlier join?” she asked.

Darrik shook his head. “The outer repair is older than my appointment here. The local copy of that work is incomplete.”

“Then there will be another.”

“Perhaps.”

“The Skykeepers carried records between the Skylands. The trade ledgers went below as well. If a drawing was sent—”

“If,” Darrik repeated, not unkindly.

Brunti rubbed her thumb along the bend in the strip of metal.

She had been so sure of the joint. For one instant it had even sounded right.

“I want to find out,” she said.

“I know.”

“Before you move it.”

“That is a question for the council.”

***

By evening the craft council had assembled in a long room above the lower terraces. Its seats were ordinary stone, worn at the edges by years of use. In the center stood a table bearing three samples from the memorial passage: a harmless fragment of outer casing, Darrik's measurements and Brunti's bent brace.

No one had brought a ceremonial weapon. Brunti had left hers in the armory with the rest of her working gear.

The council's oldest member turned the strip of metal over in her hands. She asked Brunti the same questions Darrik had asked, then a few more.

Brunti answered them all.

When she described the sound from the resting chamber, she heard again the caretaker's sharp instruction. Stop the passage.

One of the council members asked whether she believed the memorial's second note belonged to the memory stone itself.

“I don't know,” Brunti said.

It was a small sentence. She disliked how much room it occupied.

“Can you find out?”

“Not from the exposed joint. The repair is older than our surviving copy. Someone may have carried another record to a different Skyland, or sent a drawing below.”

The older councilor set the brace on the table.

“Darrik has proposed safe removal of the panel when the next convergence makes the necessary work possible. Until then it stays isolated. That decision does not depend on what the second note means.”

Brunti nodded.

“An inquiry may proceed,” the councilor continued. “We have Skykeepers returning with accounts from the roads below. They will speak tomorrow. If there is a route by which an old record might be found, and if the work here can continue safely without you, we will consider it.”

The council did not ask her to become a savior. No one spoke of a hidden evil beneath the world. They discussed how long a protected chamber could remain out of use, what replacement materials had to be prepared, and who would check the isolated support while Darrik attended to his other duties.

Brunti listened.

By the time the room emptied, the light beyond its windows had softened to a narrow gold line.

She took the bent brace with her.

At the threshold Darrik caught her attention.

“Keep that,” he said.

“I was going to.”

“Not as a punishment.”

She looked at him.

“As a measurement,” he said. “It tells you exactly where your first answer failed.”

Brunti closed her fingers around the metal. It fitted her palm badly now, curved where she had forced it into a shape that was not its own.

Outside, the Skyland drifted across the darkening sky. Lamps came awake along the terraces. Somewhere in the stone nurseries, a little voice called for help with a stubborn piece of work; someone answered with laughter, and the clink of a tool followed.

The deep, steady hum beneath the city went on.

Far away in the closed memorial passage, two old notes sounded through the stone, with a space between them that Brunti could not yet explain.
