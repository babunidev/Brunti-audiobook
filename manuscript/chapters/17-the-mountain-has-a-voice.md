# Chapter Seventeen — The Mountain Has a Voice

There were three cups at the first roadside shelter, though only two travelers sat beneath its roof.

Brunti noticed the third because the keeper had set it beside a little bowl of dried berries. Sira would have taken the berries, thanked the keeper, and tried to discover who had picked them. She had a talent for making even a hurried meal last as long as a story.

Brunti pushed the cup back across the table.

“Only two,” she said.

The keeper took it away. Velen watched the place where it had stood.

“There's a messenger leaving tomorrow,” they said.

“For the west?”

“For the southern road. The next station can forward a letter.”

“Have we received one?”

“Not yet.”

Brunti unfolded the sheet on which they had drawn their route. Sarga was marked in the margin of a trader's map, with a loose line leading toward the cliffs. Farther north, beyond several roads and an unmeasured stretch of inhabited country, the Frostwind Mountains were marked by a row of white teeth.

The cartographer had made both places look rather close.

“The map lies about how tired we'll be,” Brunti said.

“It does not claim to describe tiredness.”

“It has omitted several days of it.”

Velen turned the sheet around. Beneath the inked ridges, an earlier traveler had drawn a little sun and written a warning about the exposed ledges of Sarga. There was no scale.

“We should ask how long the road takes.”

“We should ask two people,” Brunti said. “They may disagree.”

Velen looked up, suspicious of the smile in her voice.

It was the first joke Brunti had made since Sira's cart disappeared around the western river bend.

***

They reached the Cliffs of Sarga after days of climbing through country where the grass grew shorter and the roads narrowed to pale shelves of stone. A caravan master left them at a marked turning and sent a local pathkeeper ahead with them. The pathkeeper was an older woman named Orena, whose walking staff had acquired small metal rings from places she had visited.

The rings made no sound when she walked. She had bound them with thin strips of cloth.

“The cliffs sing enough without me,” she explained.

Brunti first heard the note while there were still trees between them and the open stone.

It was low and mournful, carrying across the hillside in a steady line. A second tone rose after it, higher, close enough to form a harmony that seemed too deliberate for an empty place.

She stopped.

Velen walked on two paces before noticing.

“Something wrong?”

“No. Listen.”

The trees thinned. Beyond them stood an enormous weathered wall of rock, its face cut into fissures and narrow openings. Wind moved through the broken height, entering one dark passage and emerging from another. The sound filled the space between the cliffs without belonging to any visible mouth.

Brunti placed her fingertips against a low stone. Her own mineral seams answered the vibration faintly. It was not the same double rhythm she had heard in the memorial wall at home, yet the resemblance was enough to draw her attention into the cracks.

“Two tones,” she said.

“More than two,” Velen replied. “The higher one wavers.”

Orena sat on a flat rock and removed a knot from the cloth around her staff.

“People say the cliffs guard a treasure,” she said. “If they sing at the right hour, a door opens. If they sing the wrong song, someone disappears.”

“Have you seen either?”

“Only people looking for a door.”

Brunti looked toward a fissure beneath a high shelf. Loose fragments had collected around its mouth. Some were smooth from weather. Others had broken recently, exposing bright mineral faces.

“What makes the lower note?”

“The air, I suppose,” Orena said. “Or something inside. I have no reason to go far enough in to settle it.”

The wind stopped.

Both notes vanished so abruptly that the cliff seemed to grow taller.

A scatter of pebbles broke away high above them. The stones struck the wall once, then fell into a dark opening. The impacts continued after the fragments disappeared, softer and farther apart, until only silence remained.

Velen had lifted their pen, but the hand holding it did not move.

Brunti studied the exposed face. A rock could be hollow and still strong. It could also hold together for centuries before an unnoticed seam gave way.

Orena rose.

“That's where I turn back,” she said. “The crossing we need is below the singing shelf.”

From an outcrop above the path came the dry sound of another pebble sliding.

A tawny lioness emerged from a narrow cleft. She placed each paw with extraordinary care, her broad shoulders moving under a golden coat warmed by the afternoon sun. On a ledge overlooking the valley, she paused. One ear turned toward the wind while the other remained still.

Her tail drew a slow curve through the air.

Velen stood very close to Brunti.

“She hasn't looked at us,” they whispered.

“She has,” Orena said. “Not with the part of her face you're watching.”

The lioness lowered her head. Her nostrils widened. A forepaw rose from the stone, held there in perfect silence.

Brunti could have reached for her hammer. The habit traveled through her arm before she stopped it.

Orena indicated a shallow recess beside the path. They stepped backward into its shadow, keeping their movements small. For several moments nothing happened.

Then a hare darted through the grass below the ledge.

The lioness descended in a flash of muscle and dust. She disappeared into the broken slope so swiftly that the place where she had stood looked unoccupied long before her final pawfall reached them.

They did not follow.

Velen let out a breath.

Brunti watched a little grit settle over the flat rock beside her. Their guide had known where to stand without needing to defeat the animal, or find the rumored treasure, or explain the song.

The wind returned to the cliff.

This time the lower note began alone. The higher one arrived only after a long, imperfect pause.

Brunti took out her road notebook. She wrote what she had heard, then drew the openings she could see. Beneath the sketch she left an empty line for whatever lay within the mountain.

***

The northern journey took longer than either of their maps suggested.

They changed caravans twice. One road climbed a wooded shoulder and descended again into open country. Another passed a trading settlement where the stones of the houses held the day's warmth well after sunset. At the third station a storm had damaged a bridge, and travelers waited while a work crew fitted new timbers across the smaller channel.

Brunti helped carry the beams. Velen copied a badly weathered distance marker for the road office, then discovered that three generations of travelers had used the same number for different routes.

“Your people keep remarkable records,” Brunti told them.

“That is not praise.”

“It wasn't meant as criticism.”

“It is both.”

During those days, no letter came from Sira. They left two forwarding addresses and one short account of their progress, properly dated. Brunti refused to add that all was well. The bridge had been broken, Velen's boot had needed new stitching, and the northern wind was growing colder.

They had been safe enough. That was different.

At last the high peaks rose before them.

Even in daylight the Frostwind Mountains carried shadows blue enough to resemble deep water. Their ridgelines vanished in drifting snow. A guide waiting at the last lower station pointed toward a gap almost lost between two white walls.

“That is the pass,” he said.

Brunti could see no road.

“Are you certain?”

“Yesterday, yes.”

The guide's name was Hesk. He was a Rokur with a heavy fur hood and a narrow, soot-darkened scarf. He knew the lower shelters, the places where snow collected against stone, and the signs of a slope about to move.

“Beyond that bend we stop talking unless we need to,” he said. “The mountain answers too easily.”

The first part of the ascent followed worn grooves revealed wherever the wind had swept the snow away. They had been cut by feet, wheels, and water over an age the guide did not pretend to know. In other places the trail had disappeared beneath ice. The outer edge dropped into a broad sea of cloud.

Brunti had been born in a city that moved through the sky. She knew what it was to see white distances beneath an inhabited floor. Here there was no engineered foundation beneath her, only a narrowing shelf of old stone.

The wind pushed against her mineral shoulders. It found the seams of her pack and pulled the strap sideways.

Velen came after her, one gloved hand on the rock.

“Don't look down,” Hesk called.

“I was looking at the route,” Velen answered.

“Then look at the part that remains.”

At a sheltered bend, the wind briefly fell quiet. Snow lay against the cliff in smooth folds. An opening appeared beneath the white edge, a thin dark fissure near the old path. Someone at the lower station had spoken of a hoard abandoned somewhere on these heights, and Velen glanced toward the opening before catching Brunti's eye.

“It's not evidence,” they said.

“I didn't say anything.”

“You were going to.”

“I was going to ask whether we had enough light.”

They had one good lantern and a small reserve of oil. Hesk had already warned that the exposed ledge beyond the bend was difficult in failing light. Brunti looked from the dark cleft to the lengthening shadow against the pass.

“We'll keep the light for the road.”

As they crossed the next shelf, the mountain shook loose a fine spray of snow. It struck Brunti's shoulder and filled Velen's footprints behind her. Hesk stopped, waited for the movement to settle, then turned them toward a shallow stone shelter above the main trail.

“We rest here,” he said.

“The next shelter is marked only a little farther on,” Brunti replied.

“On a summer map.”

She felt the pull of wanting to finish the pass. They were tired. Their food was measured. Each delay seemed to push the work waiting at home farther away.

Hesk knelt and scraped the snow from the sheltered floor.

“Look at the edge,” he said.

Below them a crack ran through a layer of ice that had supported the path that morning. The outer lip had sunk. Another heavy step might not break it, but the guide would not ask anyone to prove that.

Brunti set down her pack.

She helped Hesk put up the wind screen while Velen unwrapped the lantern. There was time enough for a small flame, a portion of warm broth, and the repair of a fraying strap.

No one went into the fissure.

In the morning the outer lip of the path was gone.

***

Beyond the pass, the Frostwind Slopes opened in long bright descents, broken by cliffs and snow-filled gullies. A frozen river shone far below, silver under a pale winter sun.

The wind changed without warning. A curtain of powder slid from the ridge above them and tore apart in the air before it reached the valley. Fine crystals hissed over the exposed stone.

Hesk brought the party to a halt.

At the side of the trail, another gust lifted snow from a narrow ledge, revealing a dark recess beneath an overhang. The opening lasted only a moment before the powder drifted back.

Brunti heard a delicate ticking.

Not a hammer. Not the broad note of the cliffs. It came in small clear intervals, like crystal touching crystal in an unseen room.

Velen looked toward the recess.

“Did you hear it?”

“Yes.”

“Could it be water?”

“Possibly.”

“Could it be something made?”

“Possibly.”

Hesk tightened the cord of his hood.

“It can be ice moving under its own weight. Don't decide it's a room because you like rooms.”

Brunti took a step closer, not toward the hole but toward the safer rock beneath which the sound carried. She set two fingers against its surface. The ticking continued, too irregular to follow, then stopped.

For an instant she wanted to hold her palm there until it returned. A maker might have left something within. A naturally fractured hollow might tell her just as much about the way sound moved through stone.

Above the recess, a ribbon of snow began to drift downward.

Brunti withdrew her hand.

Velen sketched the location while Hesk marked it as an unstable overhang. They wrote down the crystalline sound without assigning a cause.

A few hours later the snow gave way to bare, sun-struck rock. Green hills could be seen in a valley far below the ridge, an unlikely softness beyond so much ice.

Here, a fragrance reached them on the wind.

It was warm, almost floral, and so unexpected that Velen searched their pack for a crushed herb packet.

“I haven't opened anything,” Brunti said.

“Neither have I.”

Hesk pointed with his staff.

Near the summit, an immense moss-flecked boulder rested at a perilous angle on a narrow foundation. Its lower edge barely touched the stone beneath it. A small opening lay behind hanging moss, leading into darkness under a weight large enough to crush the entire path.

The fragrance came from there.

Brunti crouched where the rock was firm and examined the pale dust near the base. A gust lifted the moss. For a heartbeat a deeper passage was visible, then hidden again.

“There might be a garden beneath it,” Velen said.

“There might be a pocket of air,” Brunti replied.

Hesk made a sound in his throat.

“There might be the last traveler who tried to see.”

They waited while another gust passed. The giant boulder did not move, but several grains of sand slipped inward at its base.

Brunti backed away.

She took a careful drawing of the entrance from the opposite side of the trail. Velen described the scent. Hesk recorded the boulder as a fall hazard on his route notes.

None of those accounts settled why a flower seemed to breathe beneath a mountain stone.

That evening, as they ate beside a low fire shielded from the wind, Velen turned to the page of the Sarga cliffs.

“The sound there came from air,” they said.

“We think so.”

“The scent here may have come from below the rock.”

“Yes.”

“And the ticking from the ice.”

“Possibly.”

Velen closed the book.

“I am beginning to dislike that word.”

“It's a useful word.”

“You've taken quite a liking to it.”

“Sira used to say I needed more of them.”

The name entered the small camp and stayed there.

Hesk stirred the coals. The three listened to the fire for a while. Then Velen opened their pack, found the forwarding-copy of their last letter, and moved it nearer the dry side of the canvas.

***

In the next valley a shape rose above the broken ground like a person too immense to belong to any living country.

The Giant stood against the mountain, almost perfectly vertical. Channels of weathering ran down its face in folds resembling a solemn garment. The summit disappeared into a passing cloud. Wind moving along the deep grooves produced a low, hollow sound.

Brunti stopped at its foot.

The resemblance was powerful enough to trouble the eye. It had the suggestion of a brow and the long severity of shoulders, yet every line dissolved when she changed position. The same fractures appeared in the loose stones at its base, shaped by frost and time.

Hesk waited beside a fallen fragment.

“Some say it watches the road,” he told them.

“Does it?” Velen asked.

“I have never found it looking anywhere else.”

A small stone broke from high above. It struck the immense face twice and vanished among the rubble. Brunti followed its passage, then looked at the deep seams running along the formation.

“Even something that does not move can lose pieces,” she said.

Velen had already written the description of the falling stone.

“Would you call that motion?”

“Not the kind people mean when they say the Giant walks.”

They moved on.

Beyond the rock pillar, the high ground widened into yellow tundra. Moss grew where stone protected it, and pale lichen spread over the faces exposed to the wind. There were no great trees here, only low shrubs bending in the cold.

Something small skittered between two bushes and disappeared beneath a brown crag.

Hesk took no notice. Velen crouched.

A faint scrape came from within the stones. They waited, and the movement returned: a tiny creature, quick and indistinct, pushing grit aside before withdrawing again.

“What is it?” Brunti whispered.

“I don't know,” Velen said.

“You can put that in the book.”

“I intend to.”

The creature moved once more, no longer than a finger and almost exactly the color of the ground. It vanished through a crack barely wider than its own body.

Velen sat back on their heels.

“There is more life under these rocks than I expected.”

“That's usually where it hides,” Hesk said.

Farther along the slope, a second colossal formation stood with one mass planted deeply in the mountain and another raised forward. Snow streamed between its stone legs. The Frost Strider seemed arrested halfway through a step, its jagged upper body lost in blown powder.

Stories said it had once been a living being. Other stories said it was a golem waiting for a forgotten command.

Brunti walked a careful half-circle around the exposed base. The formation did not shift. The raised mass remained suspended where the ridge supported it. Snow gathered in a long seam and spilled out, briefly giving the illusion of muscles relaxing beneath a white cloak.

Velen compared the Giant's drawing with the shape before them.

“The Giant looks as if it has been standing forever. The Strider looks as if it is about to go somewhere.”

“Neither has taken a step since we arrived,” Brunti said.

“Yes, but the small creature did.”

Brunti looked back toward the tundra.

Velen smiled, pleased with their distinction.

For once she had no wish to correct them.

***

The tracks appeared in fresh snow on the eastern side of the trail.

They were deep enough to show the pads of a large wolf, pressed into the ice between exposed stones. The marks ran close together, then spread where the animal had quickened its pace.

Hesk crouched.

“Embersnout,” he said.

Brunti had heard the name in a mountain trader's warning. The creature was said to be unmistakable in daylight: its coat burned red against the pale slopes, and it moved faster across ice than a traveler carrying supplies.

They found it at the next rise.

The wolf came low over the snow, its fiery fur bright beneath the cold sky. Its nose traced a scent that crossed the trail and vanished among the rocks. On a stretch of bare ice it slowed just enough to set its paws, then moved swiftly again.

Brunti's hand closed around the handle of her hammer.

The animal turned.

Its lips drew back from thin, needle-like teeth. A brief cloud of breath escaped its jaws. The fur over its shoulders lifted, and its body settled toward the ground.

Hesk did not run. He raised an arm to indicate a line of stones behind them.

“Slowly,” he said.

Velen stepped backward, boots dragging in the powder. Brunti remained between them and the wolf, facing it without advancing. The animal's eyes followed the movement.

A gust struck from the west. For a moment snow filled the space between them.

When it cleared, Embersnout had turned its head toward a different sound beyond the ridge.

The wolf sprang away.

The three retreated along the stones until the trail curved out of sight. Only then did Brunti release the hammer.

She could feel the stiffening at the joints of her fingers. The day's cold had worked into the fine mineral seams there, and gripping the handle too tightly had made them ache.

“You should warm those,” Hesk said, noticing.

“They still move.”

“That isn't what I said.”

He offered her the wrapped heat-stone from his kit. Brunti took it, held it between her palms, and waited until the uncomfortable tightness eased.

On the other side of the ridge, bronze flashed through a break in the cloud.

A great vault stood among broken stones, half-sheathed in frost. Etched lines covered the metal, catching light in a mesh of fine shadows. A heavy lock occupied its front, dark where the rest of the bronze shone.

Velen drew nearer, then halted outside the ice-crusted stones.

“No maker's name,” they observed.

Brunti examined the fitting from where they stood. The lock was massive, its mechanism hidden. Fine patterns continued below a crust of ice, but the metal had not been opened recently, if ever.

“Could a Grimnir have made it?” Velen asked.

“Certainly.”

“Did one?”

“I have no idea.”

Velen began a sketch.

Above them, clouds closed across the sun. The etchings lost their brightness. A loose ice crystal slipped down the bronze and stopped against the lock.

Brunti might have measured the seams. She might have tried to find a maker's mark among the ornament, or asked Hesk to help clear the frost. There were countless ways to spend a day beside an unanswered object.

Instead she counted the remaining fuel in their packs.

“Two small fires,” she said. “Enough for tonight and the next shelter.”

Hesk nodded.

“The trading country begins below the next ridge. We can make the lower wayhouse if we keep moving.”

Velen looked at the vault.

“Someone might have traveled a great distance to build that.”

“Yes,” Brunti said. “And it will still be here after we have eaten.”

They left the bronze door closed.

***

By the time they found the Frostwind Junkyard, a pale crack had appeared near Brunti's wrist.

It was fine and shallow, but she could feel the irregular drag when she flexed her hand. The cold had not made her weaker all at once. It had worked patiently at the places where her mineral body joined itself in delicate layers.

Hesk led them along a rocky track to a crevice from which a faint clink of metal rang through the air. Torchlight glowed beyond the narrow opening. Inside, the mountain gave way to a cramped shop with crowded shelves and leaning stacks of boxes.

Potions sat among rusted tools. Strange runes glimmered from objects half-buried beneath dust. A small kettle rested near the warm stones of a stove, and the room smelled of resin, mineral paste, old leather and lamp oil.

Behind a desk, a Grimnir keeper was sorting the clutter.

The keeper moved a tarnished fitting from a pile of relics to a group of potions, considered it, then returned it to the relics with a dissatisfied click.

When the travelers entered, the Grimnir raised stern crystal eyes.

“No, I don't know whose it was,” the keeper said.

Brunti had not spoken.

“Whose what?”

“Whatever you've come looking for. Everyone wants to know whose things these were. Very few want to know whether they work.”

Velen's expression brightened.

“We want both.”

“Then you'll be twice as disappointed.”

Hesk pulled back his hood.

“Ordo,” he said. “Do you have mineral binding paste, two heat clasps, and something for an Aevis who insists on making notes with bare fingers in a snowstorm?”

The keeper looked at Velen.

“Common sense?”

“I have some,” Velen answered.

“It's poorly stored.”

Ordo disappeared behind a stack of jars. Brunti looked along the shelves. A thin band of dark metal lay between a broken chime and a carved stone stopper. Along its edge ran two shallow seats, one beside the other, beneath a joined strip.

For a moment she stood perfectly still, the way she did when a piece of craftwork demanded her whole attention.

The old drawing from Aetherion showed two positions beneath a shared casing. So did this fitting.

She lifted it carefully.

“Where did this come from?”

Ordo returned carrying the supplies.

“The shelf.”

“Before the shelf.”

“A cart.”

“Before the cart?”

The keeper examined the metal.

“I couldn't tell you.”

Velen stepped closer, studying the seams.

“It resembles the paired support in our diagram.”

“It resembles many paired supports,” Brunti said.

Ordo took the piece, turned it toward the torch, and tapped its edge with a small tool. The sound was dull.

“An ornament, perhaps. Or a housing for something. Its central seat has been filed. Whoever did that left no maker's sign.”

“Was it part of a memorial?” Velen asked.

“Could have been part of a lantern.”

Brunti watched the single worn groove where the metal had been worked too many times. It was an ordinary piece in an ordinary shop. She had wanted the journey to reward her with an object that knew more than its owner did.

“What would you charge for it?” she asked.

“Enough for a day's regret.”

“That's not a number.”

“It's my most reliable measure.”

Ordo named a price. It would cost nearly what remained of their uncommitted supplies.

Velen looked at her. They did not urge her to buy it.

Brunti set the fitting back on the counter.

“We need the paste and the clasps.”

Ordo grunted and began wrapping the useful goods.

“For what it's worth,” the keeper said, “the metal isn't bad. Someone cared when they shaped it.”

“That may be all we know of them,” Brunti replied.

“That's more than most things get.”

They ate beside the stove while Hesk reviewed the remaining route. Brunti warmed her wrist and applied the mineral paste to the thin crack, drawing the edges together slowly until the discomfort subsided. Ordo watched the repair from behind the counter, making no comment until she finished.

“Too much pressure on cold joints,” the keeper said.

“I know.”

“Knowing it after the crack is a popular method.”

Velen laughed.

For a little while the shop became a place where the mountain could be held outside by a wooden door and several carefully tended flames. Ordo argued with Hesk over the value of a cracked compass. Velen bought a pair of gloves with seams fine enough to handle paper. Brunti replaced a worn strap clasp and found a small pouch of minerals prepared for cold-weather travel.

At dusk the travelers stepped back into the mountain air.

A long slope led down toward the southern approaches of Everwinter Valley. A trader coming upward passed them with an empty provision cart and spoke of a post farther on where a Rokur woman kept a hearth alive through the bitterest weather.

“Food there costs more than you expect,” the trader warned.

“Warmth too?” Velen asked.

“No,” the trader said. “She gives you that before she asks what you've brought.”

The sky beyond the peaks was turning the color of worn silver. In the stillness between gusts, Brunti could hear the small movements of their equipment: a clasp striking leather, the quiet drag of boots through thin snow, the soft creak of Velen's newly gloved hand closing around the strap of their papers.

She thought of the cliff that sang with two imperfect notes, the living creature hiding beneath the tundra, and the piece of metal she had left behind in Ordo's shop.

The mountains had offered sounds, shapes and a great many stories. Not one of them had spoken the answer she wanted.

Her wrist felt steadier within its warm binding. The lantern still had oil. Velen was close beside her, matching the pace Hesk set on the descending trail.

Below, where the last light touched the valley, a small orange point appeared.

Someone was keeping a fire.
