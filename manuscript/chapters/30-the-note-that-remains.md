# Chapter Thirty — The Note That Remains

The two stones did not sound together.

Brunti heard the first while the morning lamps were still burning in the archive court. Its light, clear resonance passed into the stone beneath her feet and faded. The second followed a little later, lower and slower, its last tremor lingering in the dark crystal seams along the wall.

Between them lay a pause.

For most of her journey, she had tried to close that space.

Now she stood inside it, and nothing was wrong.

Rain had come during the night. Drops gathered along the sheltered roof and fell at intervals into the narrow channel beneath the outer wall. The benches were dry. The two memorial stones rested in separate mounts, their surfaces catching the pale light from an opening high above.

Tavra's pale mineral carried fine threads of metal.

Korrum's darker stone held its brightness deeper, along broad veins of crystal.

The temporary name slips remained beside them. Their corners had curled in the damp, and a worker had placed a small weight upon each to keep it from moving.

Brunti had brought the finished drawings for permanent markers.

She laid them on the dry worktable.

Then she looked again at the passage beyond the court.

The handrail had still to be repaired.

Someone had come early to inspect it. The young worker from the previous evening was kneeling beside the turn, checking the temporary brace. Nemi stood near him with a tool case that nearly reached her knees.

They saw Brunti.

Nemi raised a hand.

“It's still holding,” she called.

“That's good.”

“Are we going to mend it now?”

“That's why I'm here.”

Nemi looked at the brace around Brunti's left wrist.

“Then you're going to watch.”

“I expect to do some measuring.”

“Watching and measuring,” Nemi said. “A heroic morning.”

Brunti approached.

The rail was a simple thing: a length of shaped metal set along a short turn in the corridor where an older Grimnir liked to steady herself on her way to the listening court. The fastening had loosened where new stonework met an older plate. It had not failed, but the first inspection had shown that its original fitting was too short to carry the changed load safely.

During the night, someone had tied a pale strip of cloth around the rail to warn people not to depend upon it.

Brunti touched the nearest wall with her sound hand.

The old support carried no unusual vibration.

She did not touch the rail.

“What did the morning check show?” she asked.

The worker opened his measurement sheet.

“The temporary brace stayed where we set it. The upper fastening hasn't moved, but the lower plate is resting against the new stone instead of bearing through it.”

“And the passage?”

“Open for walking. Not for leaning on the rail.”

Brunti turned to Nemi.

“Did anyone try to use it?”

“One person. I told him the cloth meant not to.”

“What did he say?”

“That he thought it was decoration.”

Brunti looked at the pale strip.

It had been tied with considerable care.

“That does seem unlikely.”

“He said he didn't notice it.”

The worker gave a helpless shrug.

Brunti considered the corridor.

The rail stood at the point where a visitor coming from the archive turned toward a sheltered bench. It was exactly where someone seeking balance would reach without looking.

“The warning needs to be farther back,” she said. “Before people come to the turn.”

Nemi took the second strip of cloth from the tool case and walked toward the approach.

She stopped at the first place where the rail was visible.

“Here?”

“Could you see it if you were looking at the floor?”

Nemi lowered her gaze.

“No.”

“Then higher.”

Nemi tied the cloth to the wall marker above the approach.

Brunti watched her test the view from several positions.

Only when the marker was visible to a person walking slowly with eyes lowered did she nod.

“Better.”

Nemi returned, pleased.

“I didn't hit anything.”

“No.”

“Are you going to make me do every repair without a hammer?”

“Only the ones that don't need one.”

“That seems like a great many repairs.”

“More than I thought,” Brunti said.

***

The replacement fitting had been prepared at the workshop.

It was not a beautiful object.

A short bar of plain metal had been shaped to bridge the changed join, with a broad bearing face on the new stone and a separate fastening into the old support. Its edges had been softened so that no one passing the corner would catch a hand upon it.

Brunti turned the piece over with her sound hand.

The shape was unremarkable and precise.

She liked it.

Nemi watched her inspection.

“Could you make it prettier?”

“I could.”

“Would it work better?”

“No.”

“Then why would you?”

Brunti studied the bar.

“I don't think I would.”

Nemi considered this answer and looked a little disappointed.

The worker explained the repair plan. The temporary brace would carry the rail while the old plate was released. The new fitting would be seated against sound stone, checked for load and fixed without allowing pressure to travel into the archive's memorial mounts.

Brunti listened all the way through.

Then she asked, “Has Darrik seen the bearing surface?”

“He measured the original plan,” the worker said. “He asked us to show him the final seating before fastening.”

“Good.”

They waited for him.

Waiting was not difficult because there was nothing to do. It was difficult because Brunti could see the steps clearly and knew which of them she might once have done without another person present.

Nemi leaned against the opposite wall.

“Why can't we begin?” she asked.

“We can prepare the tools.”

“Then why don't we?”

Brunti handed her the tool case.

Nemi looked inside.

“Everything is already there.”

“Check anyway.”

Nemi spread the tools across a cloth, matched them to the repair sheet and discovered that the short setting pin was missing.

The worker went to fetch it.

Nemi looked up at Brunti.

“Oh.”

“Yes.”

“Would you have noticed?”

“Perhaps after I needed it.”

“That's worse.”

“It usually is.”

They arranged the remaining tools in the order they would be used.

Beyond the passage, Tavra's pale stone sounded once.

A moment later Korrum's deeper resonance followed.

Nemi stopped moving.

“Does it always do that?”

“Not always in precisely the same way. But the tones are distinct now.”

“Which one comes first?”

“Tavra's did this morning.”

“Would it be wrong if Korrum's came first?”

“No.”

Nemi considered the two slips of names beyond the doorway.

“Then why did we used to hear one note and then the other?”

“Their old supports had settled. The shared casing carried part of each resonance into the other.”

“And your brace?”

“Made it worse.”

Nemi glanced at Brunti's wrist.

“Was that when it broke?”

“No. That happened much later, in Keldor.”

“Was that when you learned not to mend things too fast?”

Brunti looked at the neatly arranged tools.

“I was learning before that.”

“Then why did you do it?”

It was a fair question.

“I thought knowing a danger was possible meant I had to solve it myself.”

Nemi frowned.

“That doesn't sound sensible.”

“It wasn't.”

The young Grimnir took up a measuring wedge, inspected its narrow edge and put it back where it belonged.

“Good that you're older now,” she said.

Brunti laughed.

“I was older when I left.”

“Not this much.”

***

Darrik arrived with an inspection tablet and a bowl of mineral broth.

He handed the bowl to Brunti before examining the rail.

“Did you eat?”

“Not yet.”

“Then do that.”

Brunti took it.

Nemi watched with open interest.

“You tell her that too?”

“Frequently,” Darrik said.

“Does she listen?”

“More often than she used to.”

The two of them looked at Brunti.

She drank the broth.

“Inspection,” she said afterward.

Darrik studied the prepared fitting and the lower bearing surface. He asked the worker to remove the protective cloth from the temporary support so he could see exactly how the old plate met the new stone.

He pressed a measuring blade into the narrow gap.

The blade entered farther on one side than the other.

“Don't tighten against that,” he said. “It will turn the plate.”

The worker nodded.

“Then we need to dress the bearing.”

“Only the new insert,” Darrik replied. “Not the old wall.”

The worker loosened a removable piece of the prepared fitting and brought it to the bench. Nemi watched him make two small corrections to its edge, each no wider than a grain of mineral dust.

When he returned, the piece seated evenly.

Brunti tested the gap with a thin gauge.

It held.

She wanted to say the fitting was ready.

Darrik looked toward the membrane of protective stone that separated the passage from the listening court.

“Which side carries the load?” he asked.

“The outer support,” Brunti answered.

“Can you show me?”

Brunti placed a fingertip against the independent bearing stone and followed the visible join to its foundation. It passed beneath the corridor, away from the mounts that carried the two memory stones.

Darrik examined the same line for himself.

Then he nodded.

“Proceed.”

The crew worked without hurry.

The worker released the old fastening while the temporary brace carried the rail. Nemi held a light and passed each tool when requested. Brunti recorded the measurements and watched the bearing surfaces from the angle the worker could not see.

At one point the old plate caught.

The worker stopped.

No one asked Brunti to force it free.

They found a small burr along the underside, removed it without cutting the surrounding stone and tried again.

The plate lifted.

The new fitting settled into place.

When the last fastening had been seated, the worker asked everyone to stand clear while he tested the rail.

He pressed against it at several points.

No shifting answered.

Darrik tested it separately.

Then Brunti laid her sound hand against the outer support and listened for any new vibration passing toward the archive.

She felt only the ordinary slow hum of the Skyland moving through its frame.

The caretaker from the neighboring resting rooms had been told the work was happening. Afterward, she checked the resident chamber's stable line as part of the same inspection.

When she returned, she gave her report.

“No change.”

The worker recorded it.

Nemi untied the pale warning cloth.

“Now people can lean on it?”

“Yes,” Darrik said. “Within the ordinary purpose of a handrail.”

Nemi leaned on it as though attempting to keep a mountain from falling.

Brunti raised an eyebrow.

“Ordinary purpose.”

Nemi straightened.

“It holds.”

“So it does.”

***

The memory-keeper arrived shortly after.

She walked slowly through the outer passage, one hand resting on the newly repaired rail. At the turn she stopped and pressed it more firmly.

Brunti watched.

The fitting remained still.

The elder looked toward the workers.

“That is better.”

The worker who had made the repair bowed his head in thanks.

Brunti did not claim the praise.

The elder passed into the listening court and took the nearer bench.

Beyond her, the two memorial stones waited beneath the roof.

Nemi followed, then stopped to read the temporary name slips.

“Tavra,” she said.

“Korrum.”

The names did not sound alike.

They did not need to.

The memory-keeper gestured to the space beside her.

“Sit for a little while.”

Nemi sat at once.

Brunti remained standing until the elder looked toward her too.

“You as well.”

“My wrist—”

“Can rest just as well on the bench as it does in the air.”

Brunti sat.

They listened.

Tavra's resonance moved through the pale stone.

A pause.

Then Korrum's.

Nemi leaned forward.

“I can hear the space between them.”

The elder nodded.

“That's part of hearing both.”

“Is that how they sounded when they were alive?”

Brunti looked at the two stones.

“I don't know. The echoes preserve something of the lives and the craft that placed them here. They aren't a complete account of either person.”

The elder rested her hands against the bench.

“Tavra traveled,” she said. “That's the part of her history I heard most often as a child. A Skykeeper who brought other people's reports between cities and didn't always bring back the kind of answers they wanted.”

“What did she bring?”

“Names. News. Sometimes a correction to a name that had been written wrongly.”

Nemi looked interested.

“That sounds like Velen.”

“Velen would be pleased,” Brunti said.

“Would they?”

“Possibly even more pleased than was necessary.”

The elder continued.

“Korrum tended the inhabited structures. Some of the old workers used to say you could tell when that person had visited a chamber because a loose step would no longer be loose.”

“Did Korrum repair everything?” Nemi asked.

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because there were always more things to mend than one pair of hands could reach.”

Nemi looked toward the rail.

That seemed to satisfy her more than a heroic account would have.

The elder listened again.

“When I was young,” she said, “I knew Tavra's name from the old records. I didn't know the second stone was Korrum's. I suppose some older keeper knew. Then a cover was fitted and a name fell out of use.”

“Did someone mean to hide it?” Nemi asked.

“We have no record of that.”

Brunti glanced at the old mounting locations beyond the stone.

“The strip lay across the inscription. It appears to have been fitted to support the casing. That doesn't tell us what the worker intended about the name.”

Nemi frowned.

“I could have moved the strip.”

“You couldn't have done it safely until the stone's weight was supported,” Brunti replied.

The elder smiled.

“And now the names can be spoken separately.”

She settled back.

The next note came.

No one hurried to fill the pause.

***

The permanent name markers had been cut from two small pieces of compatible stone.

They were made for the new mounts, not for the old memorial surfaces themselves. Each would rest on a separate shelf beside its stone and could be removed for future inspection without disturbing the original carving.

Brunti had chosen the material with Darrik the previous evening.

One marker was pale grey, the other a deeper shade, different enough that the pair could be recognized by touch and sight. Neither borrowed the exact color of the memorial it named.

The archive record supplied the names, the original inscriptions' locations and the evidence for reading them. The markers would offer a clear way for visitors to know which history they were hearing.

Brunti brought the two unfinished pieces to the workbench.

Nemi came with her.

“Are you making those yourself?”

“I can shape the letters with my sound hand. The finer cuts may need help.”

“From whom?”

“You, if you wish.”

Nemi stared.

“Me?”

“You know how to use the small lettering tool.”

“I've used it on practice blocks.”

“Then we'll begin on one of those.”

“That's not the same as making a name for a memorial.”

“No,” Brunti agreed. “It isn't.”

She placed a spare stone between them.

Nemi traced the letters of Tavra's name with a dry marker, following the copy of the original inscription. Her hand was steady until she reached the third letter. Then she pressed too hard, leaving a groove deeper than the others.

She stopped.

“I've spoiled it.”

“It's a practice stone.”

“It still looks wrong.”

“Then tell me which cut is wrong.”

Nemi pointed.

Brunti examined it.

“The depth changes here. What happened?”

“I pushed because the line was fading.”

“Did you ask the tool to cut deeper, or did you ask your hand?”

Nemi looked at her.

“That makes no sense.”

“It made sense when Darrik asked me how I fitted the first brace.”

“Did it help?”

“Eventually.”

Nemi studied the cut.

“I was trying to make the letter all at once.”

“Then try making the line before you make the whole letter.”

Nemi began again.

The next practice mark was better.

It was still not even.

Brunti showed her how to turn the stone rather than twist her wrist into an awkward angle. They worked through the letter slowly, then another.

When Nemi had finished, she looked at the two practice names.

“They aren't beautiful.”

“They're legible.”

“That seems a small compliment.”

“People need to read them.”

Nemi lifted the stone.

“And if someone says they aren't pretty?”

“You may tell them why.”

“What if I don't know why?”

“Then you may ask.”

Nemi looked thoughtfully at Brunti.

“Are you teaching me to be difficult?”

“Only when the question deserves it.”

***

They did not cut both finished markers that morning.

Brunti's left wrist began to ache long before she had completed the second practice. The repaired crystal had settled enough to allow small movements, but the healer's instructions remained clear about strain.

She put down the tool.

Nemi looked disappointed.

“We've only done one.”

“We've done the practice.”

“That's not the finished work.”

“No.”

“Can we finish tomorrow?”

“Yes.”

The next day the worker responsible for the archive's inscription standards came to inspect the designs.

The name **Tavra** matched the independently witnessed rubbing, with the letter damaged by the old fastening represented in the accompanying record rather than hidden beneath an invented perfect reconstruction.

**Korrum** was copied from the newly uncovered lower inscription and checked against the secondary record Velen had found.

The worker asked Nemi to explain which name belonged to which mount.

She answered correctly.

Then she asked how the name markers were attached.

“Not by driving a pin into the memory stones,” the worker said.

Nemi looked briefly offended.

“I knew that.”

“Then show me the support.”

She did.

The markers rested on independent shelves.

No tool or fastener would pierce the conserved memorials. The narrow gap between the two stones remained clear.

The worker approved the mounting plan.

Brunti watched Nemi set down the inspection sheet.

The young craftswoman had done something Brunti would once have called a small part of the real work. She had checked why a name was being placed where it belonged and how its placement might affect the stones themselves.

It was the real work.

***

Velen came to the court carrying a bundle of papers.

They had remained on the Skyland while the final archive records were prepared, partly because the source copies needed their signature and partly because there were still questions about the old transport notation that could not honestly be answered.

They found Brunti at the bench.

“You've begun making the markers.”

“Yes.”

“Has the elder approved their position?”

“She has. Nemi helped check the approach.”

Velen examined the supporting documents.

“Good.”

They placed a small folder beside Brunti.

“These are the copies that will remain here. The original Aetherion drawing stays in its own archive, under the old citation. The names came from the stone inscriptions uncovered here, and the supporting Skyland records are identified separately.”

Brunti turned the pages.

The gaps remained.

No one had drawn the missing half of the old workshop seal.

No one had made the transport marks agree by altering a line that had survived on paper.

She ran her sound fingertip along the edge of the folder.

“Will there be another copy?”

“Yes. One for the Skykeeper records, one for the archive and one for the craft office.”

“Who decides if a later reading is better?”

“Whoever can show why it is better.”

Brunti smiled.

“Will you stay until the next convergence?”

“Not through all of it. Aetherion has its own records waiting, and the road doesn't stop asking questions because your wall is safe.”

“I suppose it doesn't.”

Velen watched her a moment.

“You sound less troubled by that than you once did.”

“I have a wall here that needs no more answers from Blackwood.”

“That is an unusually sensible sentence.”

“I may repeat it.”

“Then I'll need to record the occasion.”

They sat together beside the open window while the morning air moved through the court.

Brunti's notebook lay between them.

Its Blackwood pages still contained their gaps.

She had not torn them out.

Nor had she put them beside the memorial inscriptions as if they were another kind of evidence for the stones.

They belonged to a different story.

One day another person might read them and recognize something she had not. Or might find her memories impossible to verify. Neither possibility gave her cause to change the record now.

***

A few mornings later the listening court became part of the ordinary route through the Skyland.

The workers removed the last protective screens. The caretaker confirmed the neighboring resting room remained quiet during normal use. The memory-keeper came and sat upon her bench. Two visitors brought warm mineral water in covered cups. Someone stopped only long enough to look at the two names before continuing to a morning shift.

No bell was rung to announce the opening.

There had already been a modest gathering when the stones were transferred.

Now the court belonged to the day's traffic.

Brunti stood near the entrance, holding the two finished markers in a padded cloth.

Darrik inspected their independent shelves one last time.

“Ready?” he asked.

“Nearly.”

Brunti went to the memory-keeper.

“Are the names placed where you can read them from the bench?”

The elder leaned forward and looked.

“Tavra on the left. Korrum on the right.”

“Is the light sufficient?”

“Most mornings, yes. When the sky turns dark, we may need another lamp.”

Brunti made a note.

Darrik glanced at the work sheet.

“You don't have to solve the lamp today.”

“I know.”

“It can go on tomorrow's inspection list.”

“That's where I put it.”

He looked briefly satisfied.

Brunti returned to the mounts.

The two stones stood intact.

She took the first permanent marker from its cloth, checked the inscription once against the documented rubbing and set it upon the prepared shelf beside the pale memorial.

**Tavra.**

The stone's resonance sounded softly.

Brunti lifted the second marker.

She read it.

Then she placed it beside the darker memorial on its own shelf.

**Korrum.**

The lower note followed.

Between the two was a pause that belonged to neither inscription and did not have to be explained.

Brunti withdrew her hand.

Darrik checked both markers and their supports.

“Secure,” he said.

The memory-keeper looked at the two names.

“Now I know whose histories I'm hearing,” she said.

Brunti did not correct her by explaining how much of each life the stones could preserve.

The elder knew that already.

She had been caring for the old passage long before Brunti learned to hold a hammer.

***

Nemi brought someone to the court after the midday meal.

The child was younger than she was, with pale mineral plates still smooth across the forehead and crystal eyes that reflected the sheltered light. They carried a small practice slate and held it against their chest.

Nemi approached Brunti with the earnestness of someone entrusted with a difficult duty.

“This is Iri,” she said. “They wanted to hear the two stones. I told them what we know, but they had other questions.”

Brunti knelt so her face was level with the younger Grimnir's.

“Would you like to sit?”

Iri looked at the benches.

“May I go near them?”

“Yes. The mounts are protected, but the walkway is open. The stones themselves mustn't be handled.”

Iri stepped closer.

Brunti watched their feet.

The floor was clear. The handrail stood firm along the turn. The child could reach the bench without crossing the workers' former path.

She followed at an ordinary pace.

Iri sat and listened for a while.

Then the little Grimnir asked, “Did they know each other?”

Brunti looked at the two stones.

“We know they came to rest beside each other under one casing. That doesn't tell us everything about their lives or whether they knew one another well.”

“But they sound like they're talking.”

“The sounds can seem that way.”

“Are they?”

“They're echoes,” Brunti said. “The stones preserve traces. They don't make new replies to one another.”

Iri looked disappointed.

“Then why keep them?”

The elder memory-keeper, sitting a little farther down the bench, did not answer for Brunti.

Brunti considered the question.

“Because the people who lived left marks in the world. These are some of them. Other marks survive in records, in buildings and in what people remember.”

Iri looked toward the handrail.

“Like that?”

“Like that, perhaps. Korrum worked on inhabited structures long ago. We don't know whether that particular rail has anything to do with that work. But someone else fitted it recently because people needed it.”

“Who?”

“The worker from the outer passage.”

“Did you?”

“I helped measure it.”

Iri seemed satisfied by the difference.

The two notes sounded again.

Iri pressed the slate against their knees.

“Can I draw them?”

“You can draw what you see.”

“Can I draw the sounds?”

“You can try.”

The child began making two wavy lines upon the slate.

They were not alike.

Brunti watched the mark of one curve away from the other.

Iri left a gap.

***

After a while, the questions turned toward the rest of Brunti's journey.

Nemi had told Iri that Brunti had traveled beyond Everwinter and into Keldor, where she had seen trees that did not stand the way trees usually did and a hall beneath the forest that might have been much larger than any hall in the Skyland.

“Did you go under the whole world?” Iri asked.

“No.”

“How do you know?”

“I don't. But I have no reason to think I did.”

“Did the dark place have a king?”

“I saw a figure upon a throne. I don't know whether it was a king, a keeper or something else.”

“Was it alive?”

“Perhaps.”

Iri stared.

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the answer I can give.”

Nemi shifted beside them.

“Tell them about the singing trees.”

Brunti looked at Nemi.

“You've heard me describe them?”

“Velen told me there were faces that sang.”

“That's what I remember. Faces seemed to be set into hanging wood, and I heard voices in the dark.”

“Were the faces real?” Iri asked.

“I saw them. I can't tell you what they were.”

“Was it dangerous?”

“It was dangerous for me. I went farther than the safe paths, and my body was badly hurt.”

Iri glanced at the brace around her wrist.

“Did something bite it?”

“No.”

“Then what?”

“A fracture worsened while I was in a place where I felt deep vibrations. I don't know exactly what made them.”

The child lowered their slate.

“Did you find your way out?”

“I woke in a healer's room at the edge of the forest. I don't remember the journey between.”

“That sounds like you lost.”

Brunti smiled faintly.

“Some things can't usefully be counted that way.”

Iri thought for a while.

“Then what did you bring back?”

Brunti looked toward the two stones.

There had been a time when that question would have emptied her chest with shame.

She had come home with no treasure from Blackwood. The notes had no proof of a keeper, a hidden kingdom or a message from the dead. Her wrist still needed care.

But the journey had brought her to a room where someone had asked for help and another person had stayed to give it. To a cliff where she had chosen not to climb. To a lost child who needed a road more than an explanation.

Some of those moments belonged to her.

Others belonged first to people whose lives would go on without her.

“Accounts,” she said. “Some I saw myself. Some other people told me. A sketch from Aetherion helped us know where to look in this wall. And I learned that I must say when I don't know something.”

Iri frowned.

“Is that all?”

“No,” Brunti said. “I brought back this wrist.”

Nemi laughed.

“That doesn't count as a prize.”

“I'm glad to hear it.”

Iri seemed to decide the wrist was an unsatisfactory treasure.

“What if another traveler knows what the singing trees were?”

“Then we can listen to their account. We should ask how they know, and whether they saw the same things.”

“What if they say something different?”

“We can keep both accounts and mark where they disagree.”

Iri looked down at the slate.

Two lines crossed the surface with a gap between them.

“Like the stones?”

Brunti hesitated.

“Similar as a way of keeping things apart. But the stones have their own history. The people in Blackwood may have another.”

Iri nodded.

Then, without asking, they added a third line at the edge of the slate.

It was shorter and curved in a different direction.

Brunti did not tell the child what it meant.

***

The afternoon moved through the archive.

A visitor brought a record of Tavra's last known road, copied from a Skykeeper's old ledger. Another remembered being told that Korrum had repaired a stair in a hall that no longer existed. Neither person could prove every detail. Velen noted the sources and left the differing versions visible for anyone who wished to compare them.

Darrik came to examine the new lamp proposal.

Nemi carried the record back to the workshop.

Iri stayed to finish their drawing.

Brunti listened to the small sounds of the place.

The scrape of a slate.

A bowl set down beside a bench.

A door opened softly at the far end of the passage.

No sound from the memorial carried into the resting chamber behind its new independent wall.

The child beside her finished the third line and turned the slate around.

“Is it right?”

Brunti looked.

The first line rose sharply, then curved.

The second remained low and long.

The third wandered toward the edge of the slate without touching either.

“I don't know what you meant to draw,” she said. “Tell me.”

Iri pointed.

“That's Tavra. That's Korrum. This one is the path you took in the forest.”

“Why is there a space?”

“Because you said you couldn't remember all of it.”

Brunti looked at the little Grimnir.

“I did.”

“Then I left it empty.”

She considered the slate.

The lines were uneven. Their curves were not precise, and they did not look like a proper map or a record of resonance.

They were a child's attempt to make room for three things that had not become one.

“Would you like to put your name on it?” Brunti asked.

Iri shook their head.

“It's for you.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes.”

“Then thank you.”

Iri handed her the slate.

Brunti received it with her sound hand.

She did not correct the lines.

***

When the court emptied near evening, Brunti returned the slate to her workshop.

Her old hammer lay on the bench, where she had left it after the memorial work. Nemi's practice block stood beside it, now carrying more strikes than she could easily count.

The hammer was familiar in her hand.

It had traveled with her through Ethera's Vale, Aetherion, Everwinter and Keldor. It had been useful in shelters and dangerous places. It had also been close at hand in moments when she should have asked another person what was needed before deciding to use it.

Brunti set the hammer down.

Beside it she placed Iri's slate.

The little drawing caught the evening light.

For a moment she thought of the vast dark hall beneath Blackwood, its silent kneeling figures and the immense green pulse that might have traveled through something living.

She did not know what had become of the figure upon the throne.

She did not know whether the marchers had been real or remembered.

She did not know whether another traveler would ever return with an account she could compare to her own.

Those questions remained beyond her workshop.

The bench beneath her hand was real.

The hammer was real.

So was the sound of someone coming along the passage.

Nemi appeared at the doorway carrying two bowls.

“One for you,” she said.

“What's in it?”

“Dinner.”

“That's a category.”

“Roots, grain, ground mineral and something the cook claims is a vegetable.”

Brunti took the bowl.

“Did you ask the cook what it was?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“She said to eat it before making judgments.”

Brunti looked down at the pale, unfamiliar pieces among the warm grain.

“That seems sound advice.”

Nemi climbed onto the low bench opposite her.

The two of them ate while the Skyland moved through the evening air.

Outside, a bell called the last workers home. A cart crossed the bridge beneath the workshop. Somewhere in the lower works, an anchor crystal answered another with the patient, sustaining hum Brunti had known since she was a child.

Nemi set down her bowl.

“Will we mend the next thing tomorrow?”

“Probably.”

“What's broken?”

“I don't know yet.”

Nemi considered the answer.

“Then we should find out.”

“Yes.”

A warm breath of air moved through the open arch.

Brunti reached out with her sound hand and moved the little slate into a place where it would not be knocked from the bench. Then she returned to her meal.

Beyond the workshop, the new listening court held two names and two separate notes, with space enough between them for silence.

The Skyland traveled on.
