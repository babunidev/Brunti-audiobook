# Chapter Six — Under Eldertree

By morning the path had changed its mind.

At least, that was Sira's explanation.

Brunti stood where the forest track divided around a bank of roots. She had marked the left-hand tree with a loose strip of cloth when they passed it the evening before. Now the same strip hung beside them again, although they had walked steadily forward since sunrise.

“The tree has moved?” Brunti asked.

“I didn't say the tree moved.”

“You said the path changed its mind.”

“Yes.”

“Paths do not have minds.”

“Then ours may be better at losing itself than we are.”

Sira sat on a low root and retied one of the flowers that had slipped from her horn. The forest was alive with water and leaves. High above them, the immense canopy filtered sunlight into slender green shafts, leaving the ground cool and dark between them. The air smelled of damp bark, fresh earth and a faint sweetness rising from moss.

Brunti examined the strip of cloth.

It was hers. She had tied it there with the knot she used for a temporary fastening. She knew the knot. She also knew they had not turned around.

She was about to say so again when Sira pointed toward the bank.

“What is that?”

Beneath the spreading roots of an ancient tree, something glimmered through the moss.

They approached without stepping across the exposed roots. The thing was a chest, small enough for Brunti to lift but too large to carry unnoticed. Fine metalwork traced the shape of feathers along its sides, and narrow engraved lines curled across the lid like currents of wind. The roots rested against the corners without breaking the form. Even beneath the green growth the workmanship was precise.

“Aevis craft,” Brunti said.

Sira leaned forward. “You can tell?”

“Look at the lines. They turn without closing on themselves. That is common in their work. And the metal has been drawn thinner here than a Grimnir would choose for an ordinary traveling chest.”

She ran her fingertips over the air just above the lock, not touching it.

“There's a mechanism behind the faceplate. Narrow parts. It would require a matching key or a very careful hand.”

“You could open it?”

“Perhaps.”

Sira watched the chest.

“Then we should leave it shut.”

Brunti looked up. “I wasn't going to open it.”

“You were thinking about it.”

“Of course I was. It is an excellent lock.”

“That is not a reason.”

“No,” Brunti admitted. “It is not.”

They examined the chest without moving it. The roots had grown around its sides, making it difficult to judge how long it had rested there. A traveler might have left it after an accident. It might have been placed beneath the tree intentionally. The engravings showed Aevis influence, but neither the name of a maker nor an owner's mark that Brunti could recognize.

Sira took a small piece of cloth from her bag and used it to mark the place on her own rough route sketch.

“What will you do with that?” Brunti asked.

“Ask at the next road shelter whether someone is looking for a lost chest.”

“That could bring anyone here.”

“Then I'll ask what the owner can tell me about it before I bring anyone back.”

Brunti studied her for a moment.

“You've done this before.”

“Carrying messages sometimes includes carrying news of things no one asked you to find.”

They left the chest beneath the roots. Brunti looked back once, thinking of the intricate mechanism waiting quietly behind its bronze face.

Farther along the track, another pale glimmer drew her eye.

This time the object was much smaller: a lockbox of light metal half buried in moss and trailing vines. The surface had a delicate opalescent sheen. Flowers grew near its edges, and the fine patterns along the lid resembled curling wind and long feathers. At the center a gilded crest remained bright where the rest had weathered.

Brunti knelt.

“This one has a different hand behind it,” she said. “The lines are finer. Even the corner fittings carry the same pattern.”

Sira crouched beside her.

“A merchant once told me that crest belonged to an Aevis royal house.”

“Does it?”

“I don't know. The Aevis I have met speak of councils and scholars. The merchant spoke of kings because kings make good stories for expensive goods.”

Brunti copied the crest into her notebook. She resisted the urge to press a thumb against its exacting lock.

She sketched the crest, then moved the page into the shade to compare the gilding against her lines. The box was still more beautiful than her drawing. A moss beetle climbed over the lid and disappeared beneath a leaf.

“Would a keeper know?” she asked.

“A keeper might know who has passed this way. That would be a beginning.”

They returned to the path. Behind them, the vines remained where they had found them, and the two containers lay in separate root shelters, unopened.

***

The forest grew stranger as the morning passed.

Its trees rose on trunks so broad that the spaces between them felt like corridors. Great roots crossed the ground in long ridges, sometimes worn smooth where feet had passed over them and sometimes covered in moss too soft to bear a careless step. Small streams emerged beneath stones and vanished again among ferns.

At one turning, Brunti heard running water and followed it into a shallow hollow.

There was no stream.

Instead, a little fount of light rose from the ground, flowing and gathering in a basin of smooth stone without spilling over its edge. Its surface moved like water, though Brunti could see no source feeding it. Tiny sparks drifted above the glow, joined briefly into delicate shapes and dissolved.

Sira stopped at the threshold of the clearing.

“Do you know this place?” Brunti asked.

“No.”

“Does it have a name?”

“Not one I've been told.”

Brunti moved nearer.

Through the luminous surface, for a moment, she seemed to see fragments of stars. They formed a scattering of distant points and then vanished into the flowing light. The air hummed softly. The vibration reached the stone beneath Brunti's feet and passed through the fine seams of her body, but it did not behave like the structural note of a Grimnir support.

She opened her notebook.

Sira waited while she drew the shape of the basin and marked where the sparks had gathered.

“Could you touch it?” Sira asked.

“I could.”

“Would you?”

Brunti studied the light.

The fount was small enough that a child might have crossed the clearing to dip a hand into it without hesitation. Its glow was gentle, and the surrounding flowers showed no sign of damage. Yet she knew nothing about what lay beneath the surface.

“I don't know what would happen,” she said.

“That has not stopped you before.”

Brunti gave Sira a narrow look.

“Before, I thought I knew.”

Sira's expression changed. “What happened?”

Brunti sat on a flat stone beside the clearing.

For the first time since they had met, she told Sira about the memorial wall.

She described the two notes in the passage, the old joint hidden beneath its protective cover, and the brace she had fitted too quickly. She told her about the resting chamber behind it, about the injured resident whose quiet had been broken, and about Darrik's work to keep the living safe. When she reached the council's decision, she took the bent strip of metal from her bag and laid it between them.

Sira picked it up carefully.

“It is a little thing,” she said.

“That is what I thought when I put it there.”

“And the old memorial?”

“Still isolated. Darrik intends to move it safely while they rebuild the inhabited wall.”

“You don't want that.”

“I don't want the people who pass the memorial each morning to lose its sound. The stones carry echoes of those who lived before us. Moving them into a store may keep them whole, but it changes who hears them.”

“Would moving them harm the echoes?”

“Not if it is done properly.”

“Then what are you afraid of?”

Brunti looked at the shining fount.

“Of being the one who made everyone stop listening.”

Sira turned the bent brace over in her hands.

“Did Darrik tell you that was what would happen?”

“No. He said the stones would be conserved.”

“Then perhaps there is more than one way to keep them heard.”

Brunti had no answer to that. It was the sort of possibility Darrik might have considered if she had allowed the conversation to reach it.

She took back the metal and tucked it away.

The fount continued to shimmer. For one breath of time its sparks gathered into the shape of a small constellation, then spread and disappeared.

Brunti stood.

“I want to know where the light comes from,” she said.

“So do I.”

“Shall we examine it?”

Sira considered the luminous water.

“Another day,” she said. “When we have someone with us who knows the place.”

They left it untouched.

***

The great tree rose above the rest of Eldertree Forest like a mountain grown from a seed.

Brunti saw its crown long before they reached the trunk. Branches spread across the sky, carrying leaves that whispered in an air scarcely moving at ground level. When they came beneath it, the shade was deep and cool, and the roots stood from the earth like weathered stone walls.

The bark was folded into ridges and hollows broad enough to hold the shadow of a traveler.

Carvings lay among them.

Some were enormous, worn almost beyond recognition. Others followed the curves of the wood in delicate lines, so that one might mistake them for the tree's own growth until the patterns crossed a ridge in a deliberate way.

Brunti touched a low carving.

The trunk carried a faint vibration, like distant water moving below the roots. Its rhythm was irregular but not troubled. It changed when the wind moved through the branches above, then settled again.

Sira came to stand beside her.

“People say the first giants carved stories into this tree,” she said.

“Did they?”

“I don't know. Others say Verdani gathered here and sang of water and life. The patterns nearer the roots look a little like songs woven into wood.”

Brunti considered the lines.

She could see no name, no date, no mark that identified a maker. Yet the difference between the broad ancient carvings and the delicate patterns was plain enough to her hands.

“Someone worked here,” she said.

“Many someones, perhaps.”

“Over a long time.”

“Certainly long enough for stories to grow.”

Brunti listened again to the deep trunk. The sound seemed to come from far below, as if water passed through a chamber of roots beyond the reach of sunlight. She did not mistake it for a message. She did not need to know who had carved the first mark to feel how much care had been left behind.

A little breeze stirred the high branches.

Leaves turned silver at their edges and returned to green.

They stayed for some time without speaking.

***

The next hollow was colder.

Tangled roots and trailing ivy concealed a narrow opening in the forest floor. Brunti found it while searching for a place sheltered from an approaching shower. Inside, damp stone gave off a cool scent, and small crystals glimmered along the cavern wall where pale daylight reached them.

Veins of metal crossed the rock in thin, irregular bands.

Brunti examined them with professional interest. Some were natural, carried through the stone in long, branching lines. Others had been opened by tools. Old rough cuts marked the wall beside delicate flowing signs that might once have belonged to an enchantment.

Water dripped in the deeper dark.

Beneath it sounded a faint resonance, almost musical.

Brunti moved a few steps into the hollow.

Sira stayed near the entrance.

“Is it safe?” she asked.

“So far.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the answer I have.”

Brunti waited until her eyes had adjusted to the dimness. She could see scattered crystal fragments on the uneven floor and several older toolmarks leading toward a passage too dark to measure. The marks were familiar in their purpose but not in the hand that had made them.

She thought of Nemi's hammer: a repair made badly, then made better. She thought of the old memorial joint, where an unknown craftsperson had once brought two kinds of stone together.

She could have followed the marks deeper.

Instead she studied the wall where there was still enough light to see clearly.

“These cuts are old,” she said. “There may have been miners here.”

“Who?”

“I can't tell.”

Sira lifted one hand and let a little water gather across her palm. The drops caught the faint glimmer of the crystals, making tiny moving stars in the darkness.

“Perhaps we should ask the forest before taking anything from it,” she said.

Brunti glanced at her.

“Does it answer you?”

“Not with words.”

“That sounds familiar.”

Sira laughed. The sound traveled a short way into the passage and returned to them softened by the stone.

When the rain began beyond the roots, they sheltered beneath the shallow roof of the hollow and shared a small meal. Brunti prepared minerals in water, watching the fine particles settle and rise. Sira ate fruit and roots from her travel pouch.

They did not go deeper.

When the rain ended, they returned to the daylight with nothing from the cave except the sound of dripping water in their memories.

***

The Sunlit Dell opened among the trees in late afternoon.

Warm light fell through a broad gap in the canopy, touching soft grass and little wildflowers. Butterflies moved among the blossoms. At the edge of the clearing lay a circle of weathered stones, their surfaces smoothed by age.

Sira went to sit on one.

Brunti followed.

“Giants again?” she asked.

“So the stories say. They sat here and shared their wisdom.”

“Did they?”

“Possibly.”

Brunti examined the broad stone seat. It was large enough to support several travelers at once. Its surface had been worn smooth, but by what she could not tell. Around the clearing, faint paths crossed the grass in curved lines, like the traces of dancing feet.

“And the faun?” Sira asked.

“What about them?”

“Some say they celebrated here.”

Brunti looked down at the faint paths.

“Would you believe them if I said I had seen the dance?”

“I would ask when.”

“And who danced?”

“Of course.”

“And whether they had horns?”

“That seems reasonable.”

Sira smiled. “You are becoming a difficult person to tell stories to.”

“I was always difficult.”

“Yes, but now you are difficult in a useful way.”

They ate their evening meal beneath the open sky.

Sira told Brunti about a village where songs were passed through generations by changing one small part each time a new singer took the story into their own life. Brunti found the idea troubling.

“How does anyone know the original song?”

“They don't always.”

“Then how do they know what has been lost?”

“They listen to the differences.”

Brunti watched a butterfly settle on a flower.

“On my Skyland,” she said, “we preserve the echoes of those who died. We keep them in the walls, and in some places within the cores that carry our cities. The echoes are not the whole people, but they are something we can still hear.”

Sira considered this.

“Perhaps your stones and our songs are not so different.”

“They are very different. A stone can hold a pattern without changing it every time someone listens.”

“Can it?”

Brunti opened her mouth.

She thought of the memorial wall and the two notes that had begun to sound where she expected one.

Sira did not press the question. She picked a fallen blossom from the grass and set it beside Brunti's meal, a small offering of color.

They remained in the dell until the light reached the tops of the great trees, then gathered their things and followed the path into the cooler forest.

***

They met the keeper at a crossing where three old tracks came together.

The person sat beside a low stone basin, sharpening a small wooden marker with a knife. Their clothing was made from patched cloth and woven bark, and a wide hat shaded the upper part of their face. Around the basin lay several bundles of dried leaves and a few worn tools.

“Travelers,” the keeper said, looking up.

“Lost ones,” Sira replied.

“That is not unusual here.”

Brunti showed the keeper her route sketch. It contained the chest beneath the roots, the opalescent lockbox, the shimmering fount, the great tree, the shadowed cavern and the sunlit dell.

The keeper examined it carefully.

“You've walked farther than you needed to,” they said.

“We took the paths that were there,” Brunti answered.

“They were there when you took them.”

Sira laughed.

Brunti did not.

The keeper returned the sketch.

“Some of these places are known. Some are known only to the people who have reason to go there. The forest is not uninhabited, even where you see no dwelling.”

Brunti asked about the two Aevis containers.

The keeper knew the old chest beneath the tree. Travelers had reported it for years, sometimes claiming it held jewels, sometimes letters, sometimes nothing but dust. No one had brought a trustworthy account of its owner. The smaller lockbox was less familiar, though the keeper had heard of a pale metal object among the roots.

“May we send word to a record-keeper?” Sira asked.

“Yes. There are people who watch for things lost on the roads.”

Brunti showed the drawing of the gilded crest.

The keeper studied it.

“That is a fine mark,” they said.

“Some call it royal,” Sira added.

“People call many things royal when they want them to be worth more.”

“So you don't know it?”

“I don't.”

Brunti returned the notebook to her bag.

She was beginning to recognize the comfort of an answer that did not pretend to be more than it was.

“Is there a safe place to rest before we reach the next road?” she asked.

The keeper pointed toward a wooded cliff.

“There's a small shelter beyond the stream. Moss gives it light, and the stone stays cool. You may use it if you leave it as you found it.”

They hesitated, then added, “There is another place people talk about—a hollow deep under roots where the fae are said to keep old secrets. Some claim the walls shine without any sun and the air hums like voices remembered by the earth.”

Brunti looked toward the darkening forest.

“Have you been there?”

“No.”

“Has someone you trust?”

“Someone who trusted someone else.”

Sira smiled.

“Then we will leave that one in the stories,” she said.

The keeper nodded.

Brunti asked whether they had seen an old craft ledger from the Skylands, one marked with the sign of a joint between two stones. The keeper had not. But they knew a woodland trader who kept records of work done by travelers of several peoples.

“Not in this part of the forest,” they said. “Farther along the public paths. Ask after the people who repair books and bindings. They hear more about old hands than those who merely sell old things.”

It was not a precise answer.

It was a direction she could follow.

Brunti thanked the keeper and offered to help mend the broken handle of the marker they were shaping. The keeper considered the offer, then handed it to her.

“It keeps slipping,” they said. “Not the wood. The cord.”

Brunti examined the fastening.

This time she asked how it was meant to move before she tightened anything.

***

The Sylvan Sanctuary lay behind a thick curtain of green foliage in the side of a rocky cliff.

Brunti found the entrance by listening for the distant stream. The sound came through the trees, steady beneath the leaves, and grew clearer as they approached the rock face. A narrow opening curved inward beyond the vines.

Inside, the shelter was small and unadorned.

Moss glowed faintly along the stone walls, laying pale green light across the floor. Roots hung from the ceiling, and ferns grew near the entrance where a little daylight still reached them. The air smelled of pine, wet earth and cool rock. One broad flat place beside the wall offered room to sit and rest.

Sira slipped inside first and set down her bag.

“Not much of a palace,” she said.

“Which is fortunate,” Brunti replied. “I have grown suspicious of royal things.”

Sira laughed.

They arranged their resting places without disturbing the moss. Brunti set her small tools on a dry ledge and checked the fastening of her pack. Sira laid her messages beneath a folded cloth to keep them away from the damp air.

A long quiet passed.

Brunti had thought of the hidden fae den all the way from the keeper's crossing. Its story was compelling: luminous walls, ancient secrets and a hum like remembered voices. It might have held a clue to the second note of the memorial.

Or it might have been no more than a place someone had described to someone else.

She looked at the moss-lit wall of the sanctuary.

This shelter was real. Its stone was cool beneath her palm. The stream outside could be heard clearly, and its sound did not change when she closed her eyes. The roots in the ceiling had grown around small cracks without breaking them.

It asked nothing of her.

Sira stirred on her resting place.

“May I ask you something?” she said.

“Of course.”

“If you find the old drawing in Aetherion, and it tells you that Darrik is right to move the memorial, what will you do?”

Brunti watched a pale patch of light move across the floor as the leaves outside shifted.

“I will bring the drawing home.”

“And then?”

“I will help make the wall safe.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

Brunti turned.

Sira's expression was gentle but not yielding.

Brunti looked down at her hands. The seams of crystal across her knuckles caught the sanctuary's green light.

“I don't know,” she said at last.

Sira nodded and pulled a corner of her blanket free of a root.

“Then don't decide tonight.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“You have been staring at the same stone for a quarter of an hour.”

Brunti withdrew her hand from the wall. The moss where her palm had rested was warm.

She lay back against the resting place, feeling the cool support beneath her body.

In the small shelter the two travelers settled into silence. Sira's breathing was soft. The moss held its faint light, and somewhere beyond the vines the stream ran on through Eldertree Forest, carrying its own sound into the night.

Brunti closed her eyes.

For the first time since leaving the Skyland, she slept without dreaming of the memorial wall.
