# Chapter Nine — Where Stories Are Sold

The traveler had been to the hidden market three times before he admitted he could not remember where its entrance was.

This confession came after a long description of the tunnel, the smell of roasted spices, and the peculiar sound of a fiddle playing somewhere beneath the earth. The wayhouse table was crowded with people willing to listen. Someone had bought the traveler another cup, and he held it close to his chest as if it were a prize won by storytelling.

“Three times?” Brunti asked.

“Four, possibly. One visit was very brief.”

“Which tunnel did you use?”

“Ah. That is the trouble with secret entrances. Once you know the way, someone changes the way.”

Sira leaned against the low window beside Brunti. Her messages lay safely wrapped in her bag, which she had placed on the bench between them.

“Did the way change,” she asked, “or did you forget it?”

The traveler considered the distinction.

“A little of both.”

His name was Pel. He sold ordinary woven goods along the public roads and collected extraordinary stories as eagerly as other merchants collected coin. He had a patched cloak, a very fine belt, and a voice that became quieter whenever he wished the people around him to lean closer.

“The tunnel opens,” he told them, “and the sound arrives before the light. Voices everywhere. Laughter, arguments, a fiddle that can scarcely keep up. Then there are lamps along the stone and awnings made of dyed cloth—blue, gold, red, colors that should not look so bright in a place without daylight.”

He spread his hands wide.

“Gems on one table, spices on another, sealed crates piled where no one can see what the sellers have brought. Fauns bargaining over little enchanted things. Goblins quarrelling with everyone. A giant with an ornate weapon longer than this table. And beside the book stalls—”

“A dragon,” called someone at the far end of the room.

Pel looked offended.

“You've heard this before.”

“Twice already.”

“Then you know it was reading.”

Sira's eyes brightened with amusement.

“A dragon reading?”

“Turning pages with its breath,” Pel said. “Very carefully. It had better manners than the bookseller.”

The room laughed.

Brunti tried to imagine the creature. Its head bent over ancient tomes, pages rising and settling in a warm draft. There was a strange loveliness to the image.

“Did you speak to it?” she asked.

Pel shook his head.

“Didn't wish to interrupt.”

“Did you see it yourself?”

He hesitated just long enough.

“A reliable fellow told me about that part. He'd had business beside the book stalls.”

“Then you didn't see the dragon.”

“I saw the market.”

“The giant?”

“That too, though only from behind. Giants are difficult to mistake.”

Brunti opened her notebook.

Pel watched her pencil.

“Are you recording my story?”

“Yes.”

“Then make certain you write down the dragon.”

“I will.”

“And the giant.”

“Yes.”

“And that I have been there three times.”

She paused.

“Three or four?”

Pel closed his eyes for a moment.

“Three. Let us settle on three.”

Sira gave a small cough, concealing a laugh.

Brunti wrote: *Pel reports three visits to a hidden market. Location uncertain. Dragon described by another seller, not witnessed by Pel.*

When she looked up, Pel had grown less pleased.

“That makes it sound a little ordinary.”

“Is it untrue?”

“No.”

“Then I shall leave it.”

The traveler studied her, then began to laugh as well.

“You are a difficult audience,” he said. “But at least you are listening.”

***

Pel's story about the Aevis merchant was less extravagant.

He had encountered the old trader, he said, near a passage serving Celesterra's outer traffic. The city lay deep beneath the southwestern mountains, though its vast chambers held artificial skies and spires that made visitors speak as if they had walked into daylight.

“The old fellow described the city as though he had built it himself,” Pel said. “I didn't understand half the names, but he loved every one.”

A cluster of tiny amulets hung beneath the man's canvas awning. They chimed whenever a current passed through the stall. There were polished stones, delicate charms, small objects of uncertain purpose, and a few ordinary keepsakes made finer by the hand that had shaped them.

“He wore faded robes,” Pel continued, “and a hood too deep for his narrow face. You could see the light moving along his form when he leaned over the counter. Aevis, certainly. Old enough to have learned how to slow a story down until you almost forgot you were buying something.”

“What did he sell you?” Brunti asked.

“A little ornament.”

Pel drew a ring of fine metal from his pouch. It carried three small suspended pieces that moved when the air stirred, but not together. A quiet chime passed between them.

Brunti held out her hand, and he passed it over.

She examined the joining points. The metal had been bent with unusual precision. No visible mechanism forced the pieces to move in order. Each turned in a slightly different current.

“Did he tell you who made it?”

“He told me a long story about a woman who kept the winds from leaving a house.”

“Was she the maker?”

“Perhaps. I asked, and he told me about the house instead.”

Sira leaned forward to look at the little ring.

“Did you ask why he kept it in a pouch?”

“Because I thought it was valuable.”

“And was it?”

Pel smiled.

“I paid more than I intended. But when I hear the little pieces chime I remember the old man speaking of home. That was worth something.”

Brunti gave the ornament back.

“He could have been telling you the truth.”

“He could.”

“Or making it sound more beautiful.”

“He could have done that too.”

Pel slipped the ring into its pouch.

“What is it you want from a merchant, Grimnir? The truth or a thing worth carrying?”

“Both, if possible.”

“Then you're shopping in a difficult market.”

Brunti might have argued, but Sira had begun to watch the window.

A caravan was drawing up outside. Its wheels sank a little into the soft earth beside the wayhouse, and several travelers climbed down slowly. Among them was an older pilgrim wearing a cloak edged with dark embroidery. The woman stood beside the cart for a while before accepting the arm of a companion.

Sira rose.

“I know her,” she said.

***

The pilgrim's name was Orena.

She had come from a settlement near the southern roads, where Sira had promised to carry spoken news to people waiting for word of distant relatives. Orena sat with them beneath the wayhouse awning while the caravan's animals were watered. Her hands were small and deeply lined, and she kept one palm wrapped around a cup of warm tea.

Sira knelt beside her.

“I have a message from the river families,” she said. “They asked me to speak it to you, if you still wished to hear it.”

Orena nodded.

Brunti moved away toward the cart, but Sira looked up.

“You may stay,” she said, “if Orena agrees.”

The older woman turned toward Brunti.

“Are you traveling together?”

“Yes,” Brunti answered.

“Then stay. There is nothing in this message I am ashamed to have heard.”

Sira spoke quietly.

She did not unfold a letter. She told Orena that the bridge near the lower river had been repaired sufficiently for small carts, though not yet for laden wagons. A family who had been separated during the spring floods had found each other again. The youngest child had begun walking with a carved stick, insisting that it made her an explorer. Someone who had once kept the village gardens had died in the early season, and the flowers beside her house were blooming without her.

Orena's expression changed at the last piece of news.

Sira stopped speaking.

For a while they sat in silence beneath the awning, listening to water being poured into a trough and the low creak of cart straps.

“Who told you?” Orena asked eventually.

“The gardener's sister.”

“Did she say whether they sang?”

“They did. The old song about the first rain, and another I had not heard before.”

Orena closed her eyes.

“Good.”

Sira waited.

There were no more questions. When the pilgrim rose to return to the caravan, she touched the flowers woven among Sira's horns and thanked her.

Brunti watched the cart leave.

“You didn't tell her everything,” she said.

“No.”

“Did you forget?”

“I was given more than one message. The rest belonged to someone else.”

Brunti studied her.

“You remembered which parts were hers.”

“I had to. That is the work.”

They gathered their things.

Outside the wayhouse the road divided. One branch followed the open country toward the central trade ways. Another turned southward along the forest's edge, where pilgrims traveled between the countryside and the Pantheon Temple.

Orena had mentioned the temple before she left. She thought the travelers might find shelter there, and Sira knew a person who kept records of visitors to the southern road.

Brunti looked at the two routes.

“How far out of our way?” she asked.

“Not so far that the city will vanish,” Sira said.

Brunti sighed.

Sira smiled.

“Are you going to tell me that Aetherion has been there a long time?”

“No. I was going to tell you that I know.”

***

The Pantheon Temple rose among quiet gardens south of Eldridia, where the green lands reached toward the old canopies of Eldertree Forest.

Its columns stood high above the path, carved with symbols of many gods. Light gathered faintly in the grooves, even where the afternoon sun could not reach them. A low chant passed through the open doors. The scent of sacred herbs mingled with the ordinary smell of warm stone and growing leaves.

Brunti stopped at the base of the steps.

She knew the tribunals of her own people. In the Skylands, their symbols appeared in tools, halls and the rites by which memory stones were prepared. But here several sacred traditions shared a single building without losing their distinct signs.

A group of pilgrims entered ahead of them. Some carried little offerings wrapped in cloth. Others came with empty hands. No one asked which god they had come to honor.

Inside, warm light lay across polished floors. The temple halls were spacious and quiet, though the soft chanting never quite stopped. An Aevis scholar stood beside an inscription, reading with lips barely moving. Two travelers knelt before different carvings. Near a window, a Rokur with low, steady emberlight sat in meditation while a Verdani attendant arranged fresh herbs along a shallow basin.

Brunti looked up at the columns.

“No one symbol holds the whole roof,” she said.

Sira followed her gaze.

“No.”

“How do they decide where each belongs?”

“Perhaps the people who built the temple argued about it for years.”

“That sounds possible.”

“And perhaps they still do.”

A smile touched Brunti's face.

The hall was not silent in the way the Skyland's memorial passage had been silent after Darrik isolated it. The voices here rose together, then parted again. Some chants were slow and low; others moved lightly above them. At no moment did they become one single note.

A temple keeper approached and asked whether they had come to pray or to seek a place to rest.

“Both, perhaps,” Sira replied.

“Then both are welcome.”

Brunti knelt near a low column, placing one palm against the stone floor.

The building carried the vibration of feet and voices. The carved symbols above her glowed steadily. She felt no hidden instruction, no new answer to the old repair. The presence of several divine emblems did not turn the temple into a key for every mystery of the world.

She was grateful for that.

For a while she listened.

***

A pilgrim near the herb basin spoke of a blacksmith he had seen on the trade road.

The smith was Rokur, broad-shouldered and usually bright with contained emberlight. He had worked at a forge where travelers brought broken tools and unfinished metal. The pilgrim had stopped to request a new hinge for a cart wheel.

“When I came in,” the man said, “the hammer was ringing steadily. Then it slowed. The smith lowered it beside the anvil and rested one hand on the bench.”

Brunti turned toward him.

“Was he injured?”

“I saw no wound.”

“Then why stop?”

“He was exhausted. The light along his shoulders had grown faint, and the furnace behind him seemed brighter than he was.”

The pilgrim described the half-shaped blade on the anvil, its edges still glowing red. Tools stood in rows along the wall, many worn by long use. The smith looked toward his work and tightened his fingers around the hammer, but did not lift it.

“He told me he had been working through the night. Said there were orders he could not leave unfinished.”

“Did he have anyone to help?” Sira asked.

“An apprentice had gone to fetch supplies. There was little prepared nourishment left in the shop.”

Brunti thought of the weary figure by the anvil. Rokur bodies were made of living flame, their forms held by discipline and care. The dimness of his emberlight did not tell her which part of his life had become too heavy. It was not a sign she could simply repair with a better fastening.

“What did you do?” she asked.

“I left the hinge for another day. Then I found someone at the nearby hearth who knew him and could check on him. I offered to carry the unfinished work to another shop if he agreed.”

“Did he?”

“Not while I was there. He said he would think about it.”

Brunti looked down at her hands.

She could see herself walking into that forge, taking the hammer and finishing the blade because she knew how to work metal. She could imagine the relief of a problem solved.

But the smith's work was not hers to claim. His exhaustion would not disappear merely because another person completed a piece of metal.

Sira seemed to follow her thoughts.

“Did you ask the smith what he needed?” she asked the pilgrim.

“I asked. He said he wanted one quiet hour in which no one asked him for anything.”

Sira nodded.

“That is a clear answer.”

The pilgrim looked at Brunti.

“You are a craftswoman?”

“Yes.”

“Then perhaps you understand.”

“I do,” Brunti said, though she was not certain she would have understood before leaving home.

They remained in the temple through the late afternoon. Sira delivered another brief message to a caretaker who served along the pilgrim routes. Brunti watched the different groups of worshippers come and go, their voices sometimes meeting beneath the high ceiling and sometimes passing in separate rhythms.

When they departed, the sky had begun to color above the gardens.

***

The Verdani Treasury stood on a woodland route near the southern edge of Eldertree Forest.

Sira found it by the vine-covered roof.

The building was small, made of wood and thatch that had softened beneath years of moss and trailing growth. Inside, shelves held herbs, woven baskets, talismans, little jars of colored potions and several curious objects whose purposes Brunti could not guess. A teapot sent a thin curl of fragrant steam into the air.

Behind the counter sat an elderly Verdani with a book open before him.

He was drawing a length of twine through its worn binding.

“Travelers,” he said without looking up. “One moment. This stitch must pass before the last one loses its place.”

Brunti waited beside the counter.

The old man's fingers moved slowly but with remarkable exactness. He drew the thread snug, smoothed the loose edge of a page and pressed it back against the spine. He tested the book's opening, then adjusted the tension once more.

Sira watched with evident pleasure.

“You still use the old stitch,” she said.

“For books that were made with it,” he replied.

He looked up, and his eyes warmed.

“Sira.”

“Hello, Thalen.”

The old man rose slowly from his chair and came around the counter. They embraced, the flowers in Sira's hair brushing his shoulder. He asked after people Brunti had never heard of, and Sira answered with the patient accuracy she gave to each message.

Brunti stood beside a shelf of jars, holding her travel bag, feeling suddenly like an object that had been put down in the wrong place.

Thalen noticed.

“And who is this?”

“Brunti. A warrior and craftswoman from a Grimnir Skyland.”

“Welcome,” he said. “Tea?”

“Yes,” Brunti answered, before she had decided whether a Grimnir ought to accept tea that was mostly herbs.

Thalen smiled.

“I have prepared mineral infusion as well.”

“Then yes, please.”

He brought three cups. Sira's smelled of flowers and warm leaves. Brunti's was darker, with a fine mineral grain settling at the bottom. She drank it slowly while Thalen returned to his book.

“What is wrong with the binding?” she asked.

“Wrong?”

“It has separated along the lower seam.”

“That is where it has worn. The pages have been opened for many years, and the thread has carried more weight than it was meant to.”

“You are strengthening it?”

“I am replacing the part that failed. If I make the new thread too tight, the pages will tear the next time someone reads them.”

Brunti leaned closer.

He had left a small allowance between the old pages and the new stitch. She could see how the thread would move as the book opened, sharing the pressure instead of forcing one corner to bear it.

“May I?” she asked, holding out her hand.

Thalen passed her the book.

It was lighter than she expected. The pages had a soft, worn texture, and their edges were darkened from many fingers. Brunti opened it gently. The new binding gave beneath the movement without slackening.

“Good work,” she said.

“Thank you.”

“Where did you learn it?”

“From someone who learned it from someone else. I have added a few mistakes of my own over the years.”

Brunti smiled.

Sira set down her cup.

“We came because Brunti is looking for a record of a repair,” she said. “Something made long ago in a Skyland, perhaps copied onto the ground roads.”

Thalen looked at Brunti with new interest.

“What kind of repair?”

“A memorial assembly,” Brunti said. “Two rhythms sound from an old joint beneath a shared cover. I braced it badly, and the sound traveled into a resting chamber. The steward at home has made the wall safe, but we need to know what the old work joined before the final restoration.”

She took out the copied ledger page.

Thalen examined the mark. He held it near the window, tilted it, then placed a blank sheet beside it to compare the proportions.

“This is a transport notation,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Not a maker's drawing.”

“No. That is what I need to find.”

He followed the old lines with his gaze.

“The entry may refer to a joint, a fitting, or simply a consignment of shaped stone. The surrounding lines might tell us who received it, but I cannot read the old clerk's abbreviation from this copy.”

Brunti felt a familiar disappointment.

“So it is useless?”

Thalen looked up.

“I did not say that.”

He set the sheet on the counter.

“A book can survive when its binding fails. But the loose pages may fall out, and when they are gathered again someone must decide which order they belonged in. That is a different question from whether the words themselves remain legible.”

Brunti glanced at the book he had been repairing.

“You have to preserve both.”

“As well as you can. Sometimes there is no perfect order left to find.”

He returned the ledger copy.

“Your mark is a place to begin. Not the answer.”

Sira leaned against the counter.

“Who keeps the old craft receipts from the Skyland traders?”

“There are workshops in the Stonebound Terraces that preserve old material accounts,” Thalen said. “But for a drawing sent through several hands, I would ask at the Archive of Echoes in Aetherion. Their catalogue includes copied descriptions whose original owners were not in the city.”

Brunti looked up.

“The Archive of Echoes?”

“Yes. Do not be deceived by the name. Their books hold traces of their creators, but the cataloguers still have to work like everyone else. A book may whisper; it does not necessarily tell you where the missing page went.”

Sira laughed softly.

Thalen took a small scrap of paper and wrote a name upon it.

“Ask for the intake records of old restoration sketches. This is someone who has helped me compare copies before. I cannot promise they are still working in that hall, but the name should lead you to the right desk.”

Brunti accepted the note.

It was not a spell. It was not even a guarantee that the person would be there.

It was a name given by someone who had spent his life keeping old things usable.

“Thank you,” she said.

Thalen inclined his head.

“Before you go,” he added, “would you help me test this binding once more? Your hands are larger than mine. The pages should be able to open for any reader without being torn.”

Brunti placed her palm beneath the book.

She waited for him to show her how far the cover ought to move. Then she opened it slowly, allowing the new thread to give as the pages settled.

Nothing snapped.

Thalen examined the spine and smiled.

“That is right.”

***

They left the treasury in the falling evening.

The road toward Aetherion was busy again. Carts moved past laden with fruit, cloth, metalwork and clay jars. Lamps glowed at the rest stops, and voices carried from one traveler to another through the darkening air.

Sira walked beside Brunti without singing.

After a while Brunti took out Thalen's note. She held it carefully between two fingers, turning it to catch the last of the light.

“You could have asked him for an enchanted book,” Sira said.

“I did ask him for one.”

“No. You asked for a name and a catalogue.”

“That is more useful.”

“Perhaps.”

Brunti looked at the little scrap.

Thalen's writing was small enough that she had to tilt the paper toward the lanterns. The name remained clear, but an ink blot beside it looked almost like a second initial.

“Would he be offended if we went back to ask?” she said.

“He might charge you for another lesson in reading,” Sira replied.

Brunti tucked the slip into the ledger sleeve. This time she knew whose name to ask for.

The road bent between low hills, carrying the warm smell of evening meals from the caravan shelters ahead. Somewhere beyond the dark fields, Aetherion waited with its markets, its workshops and the great archive where a missing maker's mark might still have left a trace.

Brunti put the note away beside the ledger copy.

A cart driver called a late greeting to Sira. She answered with three sung notes that made the man's tired horses prick their ears.
