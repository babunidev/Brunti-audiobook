# Chapter Two — The Weight of Other Stones

Nemi's hammer survived five strikes.

She arrived at Brunti's bench with the battered practice block tucked beneath one arm and the expression of a craftswoman announcing a great discovery.

“Five,” she said.

Brunti turned the block over. There were five fresh marks in the stone, the last one deeper than the others.

“You've improved,” she said.

“I told you I knew more.”

“You did.”

Nemi waited for the praise to become something larger. When it did not, she set the block down and looked at the empty space beside Brunti's tools.

“Why did they close the memorial passage?”

Brunti had expected the question eventually. She had not expected it before breakfast.

“One of its supports is uncertain.”

“Uncertain about what?”

“About holding safely.”

Nemi frowned. “Stone does not think.”

“Not all uncertainty belongs to thinking.”

“That sounds like something Darrik says.”

Brunti looked up.

Nemi's eyes shone with innocent satisfaction. She had delivered the comparison perfectly.

“Darrik says sensible things,” Brunti replied.

“So do you, sometimes.”

Before Brunti could decide whether this deserved a correction, Nemi reclaimed the block and went to find someone willing to count to six.

The workbench felt unusually quiet after she left.

Brunti had taken her resting hours on a shaped stone beside her workshop. Its familiar vibrations should have eased the small strains left by the previous day's labor, but she had spent much of the night listening for a note that was no longer carried through the open corridors. Darrik's isolation had done its work. The memorial's dissonance remained contained beyond its temporary barriers.

The residents in the adjacent resting chamber were safe.

She had asked the caretaker herself before sunrise.

That fact mattered. So did the fact that it had been Darrik, not Brunti, who made the room safe again.

She set her tools in order, took a small serving of grain and prepared minerals, then went to the council hall.

***

The first Skykeeper arrived with road dust in the fine joints of his hands and a case of records strapped across his back.

He introduced himself as Orven. Brunti had seen him before at the convergence bridges, where travelers came and went between moving cities. His work was not to bring back only the stories people wished to hear. It was to remember who had spoken, what they had claimed, and what remained beyond his knowledge.

He laid three narrow tablets on the council table.

“Separate accounts,” he said. “Different places. Different witnesses.”

Brunti recognized the warning. She was beginning to hear it everywhere.

Darrik sat beside her with the measurements of the memorial wall. He had spent the morning checking the pads and support frames. The resting chambers now used an independent, stable line of resonance. Their quiet no longer depended on the uncertain old joint.

Orven touched the first tablet.

“A scout returning through the caves found one of our people on her knee in the dark. She held herself upright against her weapon. The armor across her shoulders had been split, and the cave floor carried the marks of a struggle. The scout could not tell who had struck her.”

Brunti pictured a warrior who had kept her weapon in her hand even after her strength was spent.

“Was she helped?” she asked.

“The record says the scout approached. It does not say what happened afterward.”

“Then the record is unfinished.”

“It is,” Orven said.

“You should find the rest.”

“I have sent the request. Roads do not answer at the speed of a question.”

He laid the second tablet beside the first.

“Another traveler sheltered in the ruins of a temple. He found Stonefist Ironwill beneath a broken arch, resting on a broad slab of stone.”

Brunti knew the name. Stonefist had been spoken of in training halls when she was younger, always with more certainty than the person doing the speaking could justify.

“The traveler saw deep damage to his armor,” Orven continued. “One fastening had torn loose. A fracture along his side had not yet settled. He held his weapon even while resting, as though releasing it would mean consenting to the end of the fight.”

A member of the council asked where the temple stood. Orven named the region recorded by the traveler, then admitted that the older maps gave the place several names.

“The traveler stayed with him for a short while,” Orven said. “Stonefist declined to explain the battle. He accepted water.”

The small detail troubled Brunti more than the damaged armor.

She could imagine refusing an explanation. She could imagine holding a weapon long after there was no enemy left in sight. Accepting help while saying so little was harder to picture.

The third tablet bore a rough drawing of a cavern.

“A Crumblegheist,” Orven said.

Brunti leaned forward.

The creature's name belonged to stories of stone that moved with terrible purpose. She had heard accounts of shattered walls and tunnels collapsing without warning, though no two tellers agreed on what made such beings rise.

“A worker found a wounded Grimnir in a cave after a struggle. There were fresh gouges in the rock and broken slabs scattered around her. A narrow passage toward daylight remained open, but she could not rise to reach it.”

Orven traced the drawn opening with one finger.

“Her side had split along a mineral seam. She pressed one hand against the fracture and watched the light. The worker heard water falling somewhere deeper in the dark.”

“Did she survive?” Brunti asked.

“The report ends there.”

Brunti sat back.

“Three wounded warriors,” she said. “Three places where someone ought to have been beside them sooner.”

Orven looked at her.

“You think they needed the same thing?”

“Help.”

“Yes. But not necessarily the same help.”

“They all needed to be brought out.”

“Perhaps. One may have needed a guide. Another a safe place to rest. Stonefist may have wished only that someone remain with him for a while. We cannot ask the record to decide for them.”

Brunti touched the edge of the third tablet. She felt the fine scored line that marked the cave's opening. It had been carved with care, yet the figure of the wounded woman remained no more than a few strokes against a wall.

The drawing contained the shape of a place.

It did not contain the rest of her life.

Darrik shifted a little in his seat. She could tell he was listening to her as closely as to Orven.

“Who receives these accounts?” he asked.

“Skykeepers, archive clerks, sometimes the council of a trading city. One copy goes where help might be found. Another stays with the people who first told it.”

“And if the two copies differ?”

Orven gave a small, tired smile.

“They often do.”

He gathered the tablets. None of the stories had become less urgent for remaining incomplete.

***

The second visitor was a woman who traveled with supply caravans.

Her name was Kelna. She came from a descent station where the Skylands exchanged metalwork and prepared minerals for food, textiles, timber and news. She had brought an empty bowl with her, wrapped in cloth.

It was a plain object, made from dark earthen material, with a shallow groove worn into its rim.

“An old Grimnir gave me this,” she said. “He wanted the bowl carried somewhere it could be used.”

She set it on the table.

Brunti studied its worn surface.

Kelna described a little underground dwelling that had been stripped during a passage of Urcogors. Its shelves had been broken, its fastening cords torn away. The old man sat at the entrance, his large hands resting around the bowl, too hungry and worn to lift himself easily.

“He had set it beside him,” Kelna said. “Now and then the rim scraped against the stone. There was no food left in the room.”

“What did the Urcogors want?” one councilor asked.

“He did not know. They took what they could carry.”

“Did you see them?”

“No. I saw what remained afterward.”

Brunti listened to the careful boundary in Kelna's answer.

The Ravage appeared in the histories of the lands below as a recurring upheaval. Its peoples were older than many of the kingdoms that feared them. Some emerged in bands, crossing roads and settlements; some withdrew into forgotten places; some lived apart from the raids altogether. The old man knew who had taken his provisions, but his empty bowl could not tell the council why an entire people moved as it did.

“Did he eat?” Brunti asked.

“He did.” Kelna's voice warmed. “A caravan healer prepared something he could manage. Warm roots, grain, a little ground mineral, and water that had been kept from the cold. He argued about the seasoning.”

“Argued?”

“Said we had forgotten that salt was meant to be tasted.”

A few of the councilors laughed.

Brunti looked again at the bowl. It was easier to picture the man arguing over salt than to picture him waiting without food. She was grateful for the quarrel.

“What became of him?”

“He is staying with a family at the descent station until a safe way home is found. He asked me to bring his bowl back when the road is clear.”

That, at least, was a story with a next act.

Darrik asked how much of the station's mineral stores could be spared. The council took up the question without ceremony. Brunti watched people who had spent their lives fitting stone and shaping metal work out the weight of food, the number of containers, and which travelers could carry supplies where they were needed.

It was not impressive in the way a raised bridge or a great forge was impressive.

It was still work.

Kelna unfolded a second piece of cloth. Inside lay a rubbing of marks made on a cave wall.

“This account came from a different road,” she said. “I did not meet the people myself.”

It concerned the Stoneguard, a group of Grimnir rebels sheltering in a cave. A traveler had seen them sitting close to a dying fire beneath a dripping stalactite. Their rugged mineral bodies blended with the cave's shadows, and their gemstone eyes held faint reflections of the embers. One rebel leaned forward and carefully added a piece of wood; a few sparks lit the tired faces, then vanished. An elderly warrior raised his head and looked across the gathering, toward an empty place by the fire. Without speaking he shifted a stone closer to its warmth. Several other gemstone eyes turned toward the space. No one explained whom the place was for.

“What were they rebelling against?” Brunti asked.

Kelna shook her head.

“The traveler did not know.”

“Were they injured?”

“Some looked exhausted. That is all the account says.”

“Then they need supplies.”

“Possibly.”

“Or help getting out.”

“Possibly.”

Brunti almost said they needed fighters.

She caught the words before they left her.

Kelna seemed to hear them anyway.

“You could go to the cave with a strong arm and a good weapon,” she said, “and discover that you had brought the one thing they did not want.”

“A weapon?”

“Another person's decision.”

Brunti looked at the rubbing.

The carved lines marked the place where the fire had burned, the drip from the stone above it, and a cluster of figures crouched close together. No one had drawn the empty ground beyond the edge of the flame. It was not possible to tell whether the company wished to leave the cave or remain hidden within it.

She remembered how quickly she had fitted the brace into the memorial wall.

A space had opened. She had filled it with the first answer her hands could make.

The council's master of provisions asked Kelna to leave the rubbing so that a request for contact could travel with the next supply route. Kelna agreed, on the condition that no one described the Stoneguard's needs as settled.

The master nodded and wrote the condition down.

Brunti watched the mark being made.

For the first time that morning, she took out her own notebook.

She wrote: The Stoneguard are in a cave. They are tired. No one here knows what they are fighting for.

Then she left a blank line.

***

The accounts went on through the afternoon.

Some concerned bridges washed away by sudden water. Others described harvests, markets, strange lights above distant plains, and a young craftsman who had made an entire cart wheel from a material no one believed would last through the first mile. It had carried him six. On the seventh it had broken spectacularly, and by the next market day the story had improved so much that he was said to have crossed half the continent.

Brunti listened to the laughter with less impatience than she might have the day before.

When the hall finally emptied, Orven remained behind with a flat wooden case.

“You asked about old repair drawings,” he said.

He opened it and lifted out a long ledger bound with metal strips.

The pages were thin leaves of prepared material, their edges darkened by handling. Some names had been entered twice, once in a maker's hand and once by the clerk who received the record. The ledger held movements of things between the Skylands and the roads below: crystals, shaped stone, old tools sent for restoration, copies of plans that needed to survive the departure of the craftsperson who drew them.

Brunti leaned over the pages.

“These are not memorial records.”

“No. They are transport records. But memory stone requires skilled hands, and skilled hands carry drawings.”

He opened the ledger at a marked place.

A few lines had been crossed out by a clerk. Beside them was a narrow symbol representing a joint between two sections of stone. The name of the Skyland had survived. The purpose of the work had not.

“An earlier repair,” Orven said. “The copy was sent below with other craft plans. The receiving mark belongs to a civic archive network, though I cannot tell you which copy survived.”

Brunti bent close to the symbol.

It was not the same as the uncertain joint behind the memorial wall. It might have been a stair, a vault, a kitchen cistern. Yet its proportions resembled the sort of old cover Darrik had lifted.

“May I take it?” she asked.

“A copy of the page. Not the ledger.”

“Of course.”

Orven looked faintly amused.

“Do not make the drawing say more than it says.”

“I wasn't going to.”

The pause that followed was not long. Brunti disliked it anyway.

“All right,” she said. “I might have.”

Orven made the copy with a steady hand. He included the lines before and after the marked entry, even though Brunti had been looking only at the symbol.

“Those may tell you whether the entry belongs to the same consignment,” he said. “Sometimes the lines people ignore are the only ones that say where a thing came from.”

Brunti accepted the page.

Her fingers were large beside the delicate copy. She could feel no resonance in its surface, no whisper of the person who had made the first mark. It was a record of someone moving something between places, and nothing more.

It was enough to begin.

Darrik approached from the far end of the hall.

“The affected wall is stable in isolation,” he told them. “Its support will remain separate from the resting rooms. I have enough trained hands to prepare the replacement.”

Brunti waited, holding the page.

“The next convergence will provide the materials and access we need,” he continued. “It is not tomorrow. But it will come, and we will not postpone the safety work for a rumor.”

“No,” Brunti said.

“You may go looking for the record. The council agreed.”

She looked up sharply.

“You were outside when they concluded,” Darrik said. “Someone had to be certain the work here could continue without you.”

Brunti did not know whether to thank him or object to the familiar implication that she ought to have stayed.

“What do you think I'll find?” she asked.

“An old drawing, perhaps.”

“And if it explains the join?”

“Then we will examine the explanation.”

“And if it doesn't?”

“We will preserve the panel safely elsewhere, as planned.”

She looked down at the copied ledger.

“Someone made that old repair,” she said. “They must have known why.”

“They knew why when they made it.”

“Surely they left something behind.”

“Perhaps. Brunti, listen to me.”

She did.

“A record is not the hand that wrote it. Neither is a memory stone the whole life it remembers. If you find an answer, bring the answer. If you find only what someone believed, bring that and name it properly.”

She might once have heard this as an instruction to come home without pride. Now she thought of the three wounded warriors, of a bowl that had scraped across a floor, and of a dim fire where no one knew what the people gathered around it wanted.

“What if it is not enough?” she asked.

“Then it will be what we have.”

Darrik rested a broad hand on the table.

“I would rather work with a true piece of a thing than a complete account you had to invent.”

***

Brunti packed that evening.

There was no need for a great departure ceremony. The Skyland had always sent its people into the world and welcomed them back by the same routes. Travelers went down with craftwork, letters, supplies and questions. They came home carrying objects that had changed hands, stories altered by distance, and sometimes little more than a new way of looking at what they had left.

She chose a small travel hammer, a serviceable weapon and a toolkit light enough to carry for long days. She wrapped the copied ledger page between thin boards and put it beside her notebook. Darrik's measurements went into a separate sleeve. The bent brace she kept in the outer pocket where it would rub against her hand each time she reached for something else.

At the forge, Nemi found her checking the bindings on her pack.

“Are you going down?” the child asked.

“Yes.”

“How far?”

“Farther than I have gone before.”

“Does Darrik know?”

“He does.”

Nemi considered this with evident surprise.

“Then it must be important.”

Brunti sat on the edge of the workbench so they could speak without the child looking up.

“It is important to me,” she said. “And to people who use the resting rooms.”

“Are you going to fix the wall?”

“I am going to learn what I can. Darrik will keep it safe while I am away.”

Nemi held the practice hammer in both hands.

“Can I help?”

“You already did.”

“With this?”

“With the fourth strike.”

Nemi's face grew solemn.

“The fourth was the one that broke it.”

“Yes.”

“Then how did it help?”

“It showed us where the binding was wrong.”

The answer surprised Brunti as she said it. She had not meant to sound like Darrik.

Nemi tapped the haft against her palm, thinking.

“Will you bring me something from below?”

“Something small.”

“A stone?”

“Possibly.”

“Not one from our Skyland.”

“That would be a poor surprise.”

Nemi nodded, satisfied. She turned toward the practice blocks, then paused.

“Brunti?”

“Yes?”

“If the wall has two notes, maybe both want to be heard.”

Brunti looked at her.

The child did not wait for a reply. She was already off to attempt a sixth strike.

Outside the forge, the sky had turned the blue of deep mineral water. Low clouds passed beneath the terraces, their pale edges touching the shadow of the moving city. From far below came no sound the Skyland could hear. The distance was too great.

Brunti crossed toward the departure court, where travelers would gather when the next trade descent began.

She carried five unfinished stories in her notebook, an old line of transport ink, and a piece of metal bent by her own mistake. None could tell her what waited on the ground.

Behind her, somewhere beyond the closed passage, the memorial kept its two quiet rhythms.

Brunti did not try to make them one.
